## The Model of Hannah's Prayer: Intent, Articulation, and Sobriety




### Kavana from Heart

The *Gemara* resumes four lines from the bottom of 31a. *Amar Rav Hamnuna* — Rav Hamnuna said: *kama hilchata givarvata ika le-mishma me-hanei karai de-Hannah* — how many powerful *halachot* can be inferred from the verses concerning Hannah. The *Gemara* had just presented, in the preceding passage, the model of Rabbi Akiva — how he would sway in prayer with intense *kavana* when praying privately, yet restrain himself in communal prayer, partly out of concern for others and partly because communal prayer and individual prayer represent two distinct modes. Communal prayer corresponds more directly to the model of sacrificial service, while individual prayer places greater emphasis on *kavana*. Now the *Gemara* hones in on that question of *kavana* through the model of Hannah.

*Ve-Hannah hi medaberet al libah* — Hannah was speaking from her heart. *Mikan le-mitpallel tzarich she-yekhaven libo* — from here we derive that one who prays must direct his heart. The prayer is not merely the recitation of words; it requires genuine *kavana*, feeling, and authentic connection to what one is saying.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב הַמְנוּנָא: כַּמָּה הִלְכְתָא גִּבָּרָווֹתָא אִיכָּא לְמִשְׁמַע מֵהָנֵי קְרָאֵי דְחַנָּה. ״וְחַנָּה הִיא מְדַבֶּרֶת עַל לִבָּהּ״ — מִכָּאן לַמִּתְפַּלֵּל צָרִיךְ שֶׁיְּכַוֵּין לִבּוֹ. ״רַק שְׂפָתֶיהָ נָּעוֹת״ — מִכָּאן לַמִּתְפַּלֵּל שֶׁיַּחְתּוֹךְ בִּשְׂפָתָיו. ״וְקוֹלָהּ לֹא יִשָּׁמֵעַ״ — מִכָּאן שֶׁאָסוּר לְהַגְבִּיהַּ קוֹלוֹ בִּתְפִלָּתוֹ. ״וַיַּחְשְׁבֶהָ עֵלִי לְשִׁכֹּרָה״ — מִכָּאן שֶׁשִּׁכּוֹר אָסוּר לְהִתְפַּלֵּל.
> **Translation:** **Rav Hamnuna said: How many significant** *halakhot* **can be derived from these verses** of the prayer **of Hannah?** As it says: “And Hannah spoke in her heart, only her lips moved and her voice could not be heard, so Eli thought her to be drunk” (I Samuel 1:13). The Gemara elaborates: **From** that which is stated **here: “And Hannah spoke in her heart,”** the *halakha* that **one who prays must focus his heart** on his prayer is derived. And **from** that which is stated **here: “Only her lips moved,”** the *halakha* that **one who prays must enunciate** the words **with his lips,** not only contemplate them in his heart, is derived. **From** that which is written **here: “And her voice could not be heard,”** the *halakha* that **one is forbidden to raise his voice in his** *Amida* **prayer** as it must be recited silently. **From** the continuation of the verse **here: “So Eli thought her to be drunk,”** the *halakha* that **a drunk person is forbidden to pray.** That is why he rebuked her.

### Lips Moving Silently

*Rak sefateha na'ot* — only her lips were moving. *Mikan le-mitpallel she-yachtoch bi-sfatav* — from here we derive that one who prays must articulate with his lips, literally cutting with the lips, giving form to the words. Yet this articulation does not extend to being audible. *Ve-kolah lo yishama* — her voice could not be heard. *Mikan she-asur le-hagbi'ah kolo bi-tfilato* — from here we derive that it is forbidden to raise one's voice in prayer.

This presents a fascinating balance. Ordinarily, *halacha* requires that speech be audible — the discussion of the *Shema* earlier in the tractate included a lengthy treatment of what happens if one cannot hear one's own recitation. Here, however, the moving of the lips without any audible voice constitutes the proper mode of prayer. There are some *Acharonim* who wonder whether, since prayer is fundamentally *avodah she-ba-lev* — service of the heart — it might in principle suffice merely to think the words of prayer. The general assumption is that it does not, but the question does exist, reflecting a sense that prayer occupies a category of almost-silent speech.

### Prohibition against raising one's voice in prayer: The lesson from Hannah's quiet prayer

The *Gemara* derives three principles in sequence from Hannah's posture of prayer: the requirement of directing one's heart, the obligation to move one's lips, and the prohibition against raising one's voice. Together they define the silent prayer that remains the normative mode — not silent in the sense of pure thought, but silent in the sense of being inaudible to others.

### Drunk Cannot Pray

*Vayachsheveha Eli le-shikora* — Eli thought she was drunk. *Mikan she-shikor asur le-hitpallel* — from here we derive that a drunk person may not pray. Even if one might have minimal *kavana* sufficient to discharge the obligation, the fundamental problem is one of standing before God, and therefore it is forbidden. *Vayomer eleiha Eli: ad matai tistakarin* — and Eli said to her: until when will you be drunk? Dispose of your wine.

*Amar Rabbi Eleazar: mikan le-ro'eh be-chavero davar meguneh, tzarich le-hochiho* — Rabbi Eleazar derives from here that if one sees in another something that is inappropriate, one must rebuke him. As *Tosafot* points out, this applies even if the behavior does not technically constitute a violation — *af al gav de-leika isur*, says *Tosafot* — even where there is no strict prohibition. Nevertheless, if one cares about another person and sees them acting in an inappropriate way, one must offer gentle rebuke. Eli, seeing what appeared to be drunken behavior in a sacred place, was thus obligated to speak.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיֹּאמֶר אֵלֶיהָ עֵלִי עַד מָתַי תִּשְׁתַּכָּרִין וְגוֹ׳״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מִכָּאן לָרוֹאֶה בַּחֲבֵרוֹ

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דָּבָר שֶׁאֵינוֹ הָגוּן, צָרִיךְ לְהוֹכִיחוֹ. ״וַתַּעַן חַנָּה וַתֹּאמֶר לֹא אֲדוֹנִי״. אָמַר עוּלָּא וְאִיתֵּימָא רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בְּרַבִּי חֲנִינָא: אֲמַרָה לֵיהּ: לֹא אָדוֹן אַתָּה בְּדָבָר זֶה, וְלֹא רוּחַ הַקּוֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹרָה עָלֶיךָ, שֶׁאַתָּה חוֹשְׁדֵנִי בְּדָבָר זֶה.
> **Translation:** **an unseemly matter, he must reprimand him,** is derived. **“And Hannah answered and she said no, my master,** I am a woman of sorrowful spirit, and I have drunk neither wine nor liquor, but I pour out my soul before the Lord” (I Samuel 1:15). Regarding the words: “No, my master,” **Ulla, and some say Rabbi Yosei, son of Rabbi Ḥanina, said** that **she said to him,** in an allusion: **With regard to this matter, you are not a master, and the Divine Spirit does not rest upon you, as you** falsely **suspect me of this.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיכָּא דְאָמְרִי, הָכִי אֲמַרָה לֵיהּ: לֹא אָדוֹן אַתָּה? לָאו אִיכָּא שְׁכִינָה וְרוּחַ הַקּוֹדֶשׁ גַּבָּךְ, שֶׁדַּנְתַּנִי לְכַף חוֹבָה וְלֹא דַּנְתַּנִי לְכַף זְכוּת? מִי לָא יָדְעַתְּ דְּאִשָּׁה קְשַׁת רוּחַ אָנוֹכִי?!
> **Translation:** **Some say** another version of her response. **She said to him,** questioning: **Aren’t you a master? Aren’t the Divine Presence and Divine Spirit with you that you judged me to be guilty, and you did not judge me to be innocent? Didn’t you know that I am a woman of distressed spirit?**

### Judging favorably: Hannah's response—the obligation to assume the best of others


*Va-ta'an Hannah va-tomer: lo Adoni* — and Hannah responded and said: no, my master. She was not drunk; she was a woman of anguished spirit pouring her heart out before God.

*Amar Ulla ve-itima Rabbi Yose bi-Rabbi Hanina* — Ulla said, and some say it was Rabbi Yose the son of Rabbi Hanina: she said to him, *lo adon atta ba-davar zeh* — you are not the master of this matter, you do not truly know what is happening here. *Ve-lo Ruach HaKodesh shora alecha she-atta hoshedeni ba-davar zeh* — the divine spirit does not rest upon you if you are suspecting me of this.

According to another version — *ika de-amri hachi amar lei* — she said: *lo adon atta*, you are not like God, you don't know what is going on. *Lav ika Shkhinah ve-Ruach HaKodesh gabach she-dantani le-chaf chovah ve-lo dantani le-chaf zechut* — it must be that the divine presence was not with you, for you tipped the scale against me rather than in my favor. When one sees another doing something, one is obligated to judge them *le-chaf zechut* — to tip the balance of judgment in their favor, to assume that their actions are proper rather than improper. Hannah said: you judged me and assumed I was doing something wrong, *velo dantani le-chaf zechut*.

*Mi lo yadata isha keshat ruach anochi, yayin veshichar lo shatiti* — did you not know that I am a woman of anguished spirit? I did not drink wine or mead.

There is a remarkable gloss worth noting here. One might ask: how could Eli make such a mistake? He was the *kohen gadol*; did he not have *ruach ha-kodesh*? He even possessed the *urim ve-tumim*, which delivered direct divine communication. The explanation offered is that the *urim ve-tumim* worked by means of the letters of the tribal names inscribed upon them, with certain letters rising before the *kohen* to convey a message. When Hannah stood before Eli, the letters *shin*, *chaf*, *resh*, *heh* — spelling *shikora*, drunk — appeared before him, and he did not realize that the intended message was *keshera*, that she was like Sarah, a barren woman praying. This is a *vort* of the Gaon of Vilna, the *Ha-Gra* — it is not the plain reading, but it is a striking interpretation.

*Amar Rabbi Elazar: mikan le-nechshad be-davar she-ein bo she-tzarich lehodio* — Rabbi Eleazar further derives: if one is suspected of something of which one is innocent, one must make the matter known and correct the misimpression. Hannah did precisely this: *al titein et amatecha lifnei bat beliyaal* — do not place your maidservant before you as a daughter of *beliyaal*, as a godless person.

*Amar Rabbi Eleazar: mikan le-shikor she-mitpalel ke-ilu oved avodah zarah* — from here Rabbi Eleazar derives that one who prays while drunk is as though he engages in idolatrous worship. The textual link is drawn through a gezera shava: *ktiv hacha lifnei bat beliyaal* — it is written here "daughter of *beliyaal*," *u-ktiv hatam yatzu anashim bnei beliyaal mikirbechem* — and it is written elsewhere that people of *beliyaal* went out from your midst and led the entire city into idolatry. The conceptual connection runs deeper as well: certain forms of idolatrous worship involved becoming intoxicated and achieving a kind of heightened religious frenzy through artificial stimulants. Approaching God in a state of intoxication therefore resembles those forms of pagan worship — it is a fundamental distortion of what standing before God means.

### Appease Wrongly Accused

*Vayaan Eli vayomar: lechi leshalom* — and Eli said to her: go in peace. *Amar Rabbi Elazar: mikan le-choshed et chavero be-davar she-ein bo she-tzarich le-fayeso* — Rabbi Eleazar derives: if one has suspected another of something that turns out to be false, one must appease that person and make peace with them. Eli suspected, Hannah corrected him, he was proven wrong — and the obligation falls upon him to seek reconciliation.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְיַיִן וְשֵׁכָר לֹא שָׁתִיתִי״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מִכָּאן לַנֶּחְשָׁד בְּדָבָר שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ, שֶׁצָּרִיךְ לְהוֹדִיעוֹ.
> **Translation:** With regard to Hannah’s explanation that **“I have drunk neither wine nor liquor,” Rabbi Elazar said: From here** the *halakha* is derived **that one who is suspected of something of which he is not** guilty cannot suffice merely with the personal knowledge of his innocence, but **must inform** the one who suspects him that he is innocent and clear himself of suspicion.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אַל תִּתֵּן אֶת אֲמָתְךָ לִפְנֵי בַּת בְּלִיָּעַל״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מִכָּאן לְשִׁכּוֹר שֶׁמִּתְפַּלֵּל, כְּאִילּוּ עוֹבֵד עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה. כְּתִיב הָכָא: ״לִפְנֵי בַּת בְּלִיַּעַל״, וּכְתִיב הָתָם: ״יָצְאוּ אֲנָשִׁים בְּנֵי בְלִיַּעַל מִקִּרְבֶּךָ״. מַה לְּהַלָּן עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה, אַף כָּאן עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה.
> **Translation:** **“Do not take your maidservant as a wicked woman [*bat beliya’al*]** for out of the abundance of my complaint and anger have I spoken until now” (I Samuel 1:16). **Rabbi Elazar said: From here** the *halakha* **that when a drunk person prays it is as if he engaged in idol worship** is derived as **it is written here** that Hannah, suspected of praying while drunk, defends herself and says: “Do not take your maidservant **as a *bat beliya’al*”;** and **it is written there,** with regard to a city that has been instigated to engage in idol worship: **“*Benei beliya’al* have gone out from your midst** and have lured the inhabitants of their city, saying let us go and serve other gods which we have not known” (Deuteronomy 13:14). By means of this verbal analogy it is derived: **Just as there,** in the case of the idolatrous city, the term *beliya’al* indicates **idol worship, so too here,** in the case of one who prays drunk, *beliya’al* indicates **idol worship.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיַּעַן עֵלִי וַיֹּאמֶר לְכִי לְשָׁלוֹם״, אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מִכָּאן לַחוֹשֵׁד אֶת חֲבֵרוֹ בְּדָבָר שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ, שֶׁצָּרִיךְ לְפַיְּיסוֹ. וְלֹא עוֹד, אֶלָּא שֶׁצָּרִיךְ לְבָרְכוֹ. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״וֵאלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל יִתֵּן אֶת שֵׁלָתֵךְ״.
> **Translation:** The verse continues: **“And Eli answered and said: May you go in peace”** (I Samuel 1:17). **Rabbi Elazar said: From here** the *halakha* is derived **that one who suspects another of something that he has not** done, **he must appease him. Moreover,** the one who suspected him **must bless him,** as Eli continued and offered Hannah a blessing, **as it is stated: “And may the God of Israel grant your request** that you have asked of Him” (I Samuel 1:17).

### The obligation to bless one wrongly suspected: Eli's blessing to Hannah

*Velo od ela she-tzarich le-varcho* — and not only must one appease the wrongly suspected person, one is further obligated to bless them. *Shene'emar: velohei Yisrael yitein et sheilatecha* — as it says: may the God of Israel grant your request. Eli did not merely acknowledge his error; he blessed Hannah.

It is worth pausing to note how striking this passage is as a whole. The *Gemara* opened with the promise of many great *halachot* derivable from the verses of Hannah — and indeed, while one or two of the principles extracted are directly about prayer, the majority concern interpersonal obligations: the duty to rebuke, the obligation to judge favorably, the requirement to correct a false suspicion, the duty to appease and to bless one who has been wrongly accused. All of this is derived from a narrative about prayer, illuminating how deeply intertwined the ethics of human relationship are with the practice of standing before God.

## Hannah's Bold Prayer: Divine Names, the Body's Purpose, and the Obligation of God



### Tzevaot Divine Name


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַתִּדֹּר נֶדֶר וַתֹּאמַר ה׳ צְבָאוֹת״, אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מִיּוֹם שֶׁבָּרָא הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא אֶת עוֹלָמוֹ, לֹא הָיָה אָדָם שֶׁקְּרָאוֹ לְהַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא ״צְבָאוֹת״, עַד שֶׁבָּאתָה חַנָּה וּקְרָאַתּוּ ״צְבָאוֹת״.
> **Translation:** Incidental to this discussion of Hannah’s prayer, the Gemara explores related topics. In her prayer, Hannah said: **“And she swore an oath and said, Lord of Hosts [*Tzeva’ot*]** if You will indeed look upon the affliction of Your maidservant and remember me, and not forget Your maidservant and will give Your maidservant a male child, I will give him to the Lord all the days of his life, and there shall be no razor come upon his head” (I Samuel 1:11). **Rabbi Elazar said: From the day that the Holy One, Blessed be He, created His world, there was no person who called the Holy One, Blessed be He, Lord of Hosts until Hannah came and called Him Lord of Hosts.** This is the first time in the Bible that God is referred to by this name.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרָה חַנָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, מִכׇּל צִבְאֵי צְבָאוֹת שֶׁבָּרָאתָ בְּעוֹלָמְךָ קָשֶׁה בְּעֵינֶיךָ שֶׁתִּתֵּן לִי בֵּן אֶחָד?
> **Translation:** Rabbi Elazar explains that **Hannah said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe,** are You not the Lord of the Hosts, and **of all of the hosts and hosts** of creations **that You created in Your world, is it difficult in Your eyes to grant me one son?**

*Vatisdor neder vatomar: Hashem Tzevaot* — and she made a vow and said: Lord of Hosts.

*Amar Rabbi Eleazar: miyom she-bara Hakadosh Baruch Hu et olamo lo haya adam she-kara le-Hakadosh Baruch Hu Tzevaot ad she-baata Chana ve-karata lo Tzevaot* — Rabbi Eleazar taught: from the day that God created the world, no person had ever called upon God by the name *Tzevaot* — the heavenly host, *tzava*, meaning army, the celestial hosts — until Hannah came and used this name.

*Amra Chana lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu: Ribbono shel Olam, mikol tzevaei tzevaot she-barata be-olamecha, kashe be-einecha she-titein li bein echad?* — Hannah said before God: Master of the Universe, from all the hosts of hosts that You have created in Your world — all the universes and stars and galaxies — is it so difficult for You to give me one son?

## Hannah's Aggressive Prayer: Challenging God and Demanding Accountability



### Poor Man Parable

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָשָׁל לַמָּה הַדָּבָר דּוֹמֶה — לְמֶלֶךְ בָּשָׂר וָדָם שֶׁעָשָׂה סְעוּדָה לַעֲבָדָיו. בָּא עָנִי אֶחָד וְעָמַד עַל הַפֶּתַח, אָמַר לָהֶם: תְּנוּ לִי פְּרוּסָה אַחַת! וְלֹא הִשְׁגִּיחוּ עָלָיו. דָּחַק וְנִכְנַס אֵצֶל הַמֶּלֶךְ. אָמַר לוֹ: אֲדוֹנִי הַמֶּלֶךְ, מִכׇּל סְעוּדָה שֶׁעָשִׂיתָ קָשֶׁה בְּעֵינֶיךָ לִיתֵּן לִי פְּרוּסָה אֶחָת?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests **a parable: To what is this similar?** It is similar to **a flesh and blood king who made a feast for his servants. A poor person came and stood at the door. He said to them: Give me one slice** of bread! **And they paid him no attention. He pushed and entered before the king. He said to him: My lord, the King, from this entire feast that you have prepared, is it so difficult in your eyes to give me a single slice** of bread?


*Mashal lema hadavar domeh: le-melech basar va-dam she-asa seudah le-avadav* — to what is this comparable? To a human king who made a feast for his servants. *Uva ani echad ve-amad al ha-petach* — one poor person came and stood at the doorway. *Amar lahem: tenu li prusah achat* — he said: please give me one little scrap of bread. *Ve-lo hishgichu alav* — and they paid him no attention. *Dachak ve-nichnas etzel ha-melech* — so he forced his way past the servants into the king's own presence. *Amar lo: Adoni ha-Melech, kol seudah she-asita, kashe be-einecha liten li prusah achat?* — he said: my master the king, this entire lavish feast you have prepared — is it so difficult to give me one small scrap of bread?

The force of the parable lies in the act of forcing one's way in — the servants would not help, so the poor man pressed past them to reach the king directly. This images Hannah's own boldness in approaching God with her demand. So too Hannah says: with all that You have created in Your world, how difficult is it to give me a son?

### Conditional Threat

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵלֵךְ וְאֶסְתַּתֵּר בִּפְנֵי אֶלְקָנָה בַּעֲלִי, וְכֵיוָן דְּמִסְתַּתַּרְנָא מַשְׁקוּ לִי מֵי סוֹטָה, וְאִי אַתָּה עוֹשֶׂה תּוֹרָתְךָ פְּלַסְתֵּר, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״וְנִקְּתָה וְנִזְרְעָה זָרַע״.
> **Translation:** What was Hannah threatening? She said: **I will go and seclude myself** with another man **before Elkana, my husband. Since I secluded myself, they will force me to drink the *sota* water** to determine whether or not I have committed adultery. I will be found innocent, **and** since **You will not make Your Torah false [*pelaster*],** I will bear children. With regards to a woman who is falsely suspected of adultery and drank the *sota* water, the Torah **says:** “And if the woman was not defiled, but was pure, then **she shall be acquitted and she shall conceive”** (Numbers 5:28).

*Im ra'oh tireh* — if you shall see... *Amar Rabbi Eleazar: amrah Chanah lifnei ha-Kadosh Baruch Hu: Ribbono Shel Olam, im ra'oh* — if You see, *mutav* — good; You should see my affliction and answer me. *Ve-im lav tireh* — but if You do not attend to my suffering, then *estater bifnei Elkanah ba'ali* — I will go and seclude myself with another man in the presence of my husband Elkanah. *Ve-keivan de-mistatarna, mashkei li mei sotah* — and once I have been in private seclusion with another man, they will make me drink the *sotah* waters. *Ve-i atah oseh Toratcha palaster* — and You will not be able to make Your Torah a forgery and a falsehood.

*Palaster* is a Greek-derived word meaning false or forged — from the Greek *plastos*, meaning a molder or modeler, used metaphorically in this context to mean counterfeit. She is saying: I will force Your hand. *Shene'emar: ve-niktah ve-nizre'ah zara* — as it says: she will be cleansed and she will give forth seed, meaning a woman who is wrongly suspected and has not sinned will become pregnant.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אִם רָאֹה תִרְאֶה״, אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמְרָה חַנָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, ״אִם רָאֹה״ — מוּטָב, וְאִם לָאו — ״תִּרְאֶה״.
> **Translation:** As for the double language in the verse, **“if you will look upon [*im ra’o tireh*],” Rabbi Elazar said: Hannah said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, if You will look upon [*ra’o***] me now, **fine, and if not,** in any case **You will see** [***tireh***].

### Sotah Waters Debate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנִיחָא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר: אִם הָיְתָה עֲקָרָה — נִפְקֶדֶת, שַׁפִּיר, אֶלָּא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר, אִם הָיְתָה יוֹלֶדֶת בְּצַעַר יוֹלֶדֶת בְּרֶיוַח, נְקֵבוֹת יוֹלֶדֶת זְכָרִים שְׁחוֹרִים יוֹלֶדֶת לְבָנִים קְצָרִים יוֹלֶדֶת אֲרוּכִּים, מַאי אִיכָּא לְמֵימַר?
> **Translation:** However, Rabbi Elazar’s opinion **works out well** according **to the one who said** that the verse means: **If she were barren, she will be remembered** by God and granted children. That works out **well. But** according **to the one who said** that the verse means that childbearing will be easier and more successful, i.e., **if she had** previously **given birth with pain,** she now **gives birth with ease,** or if she had previously given birth to **daughters,** she now **gives birth to sons,** or if she had previously given birth to **black** children, considered to be unattractive, she now **gives birth to fair** children, or if she had previously given birth to **short,** weak children, she **gives birth to tall,** strong children, **what can be said?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא: ״וְנִקְּתָה וְנִזְרְעָה זָרַע״ — מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאִם הָיְתָה עֲקָרָה — נִפְקֶדֶת, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל. אָמַר לֵיהּ רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא: אִם כֵּן, יֵלְכוּ כׇּל הָעֲקָרוֹת כּוּלָּן וְיִסְתַּתְּרוּ, וְזוֹ שֶׁלֹּא קִלְקְלָה — נִפְקֶדֶת. אֶלָּא מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאִם הָיְתָה יוֹלֶדֶת בְּצַעַר — יוֹלֶדֶת בְּרֶיוַח, קְצָרִים — יוֹלֶדֶת אֲרוּכִּים, שְׁחוֹרִים — יוֹלֶדֶת לְבָנִים, אֶחָד — יוֹלֶדֶת שְׁנַיִם.
> **Translation:** **As it was taught** in a *baraita* that the *tanna’im* disputed the interpretation of the verse in Numbers: **“Then she shall be acquitted and she shall conceive” teaches that if she was barren, she will be remembered** by God and granted children; this is **the statement of Rabbi Yishmael. Rabbi Akiva said to him: If so, all barren women will go and seclude themselves** with men who are not their husbands, **and any** woman **who did not** commit the **sin** of adultery **will be remembered** by God and granted children. **Rather,** the verse **teaches** that this is merely a promise for greater ease in childbirth; **if she has** previously **given birth with pain,** she now **gives birth with ease,** if she has previously given birth to **short** children, she **gives birth to tall** children, if she has previously given birth to **black** children, she now **gives birth to fair** children, if she has previously given birth to **one** child, she now **gives birth to two** children.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״אִם רָאֹה תִרְאֶה״ — דִּבְּרָה תּוֹרָה כִּלְשׁוֹן בְּנֵי אָדָם.
> **Translation:** According to Rabbi Akiva’s explanation, **what** is derived from the double language uttered by Hannah: ***Im ra’o tireh*? The Torah spoke in the language of men,** meaning that this double language is not extraordinary and nothing may be derived from it. It is common biblical vernacular.

At this point the *Gemara* raises an objection. Hannah's threat presupposes that the *sotah* waters will cause a barren woman to become pregnant — but this is itself the subject of a *machloket*.

*Ha nicha le-man de-amar im hayta akara nifkedet, shapir* — this works well according to the opinion that if the woman was barren she will become pregnant. *Ela le-man de-amar im hayta yoledet be-tza'ar yoledet be-revach* — but according to the opinion that the *sotah* waters do not cause barren women to become pregnant, but rather improve the conditions of childbearing: *nekevot yoledet zecharim* — if she had daughters she will have sons; *shechorim yoledet levanim* — if the children were dark they will be fair; *ketzarim yoledet aruchim* — if they were short they will be tall. According to this view, barren women do not become pregnant through the *sotah* waters — what then could Hannah's threat accomplish?

*De-tanya* — as it is taught in a *baraita*: *ve-niktah ve-nizre'ah zara, melamed she-im hayta akara nifkedet* — she will be cleansed and have seed, teaching that if she was barren she will have children. *Divrei Rabbi Yishmael* — these are the words of Rabbi Yishmael. *Amar lo Rabbi Akiva: im ken yelchu kol ha-akarot kulan ve-yistatru* — Rabbi Akiva objected: if so, all barren women will figure this out and use the *sotah* process as a method to become pregnant. *Ve-zu she-lo kilkela nifkedet* — and a woman who did not sin would thereby become pregnant through what amounts to a scheme. *Ela melamed she-im hayta yoledet be-tza'ar yoledet be-revach* — Rabbi Akiva therefore maintains that the verse teaches only that one who previously gave birth with pain will now give birth with ease; *ketzarim yoledet aruchim, shechorim yoledet levanim, echad yoledet shenayim* — if the children were short they will be tall, if dark they will be fair, if she had one child she will now have multiple births. These benefits, while real, are apparently not so extreme that Rabbi Akiva fears they will motivate women to manipulate the process.

The *Gemara* now asks: *ma im ra'ot tireh* — given Rabbi Akiva's position, how does one explain what Hannah said? If the *sotah* waters would not cause a barren woman to become pregnant, her threat would not achieve anything. The answer: *dibra Torah ke-lishon bnei adam* — the Torah speaks in the language of human beings. The double expression *ra'oh tireh* need not be pressed into service as a legal threat; it is simply an idiomatic way of speaking, and one should not derive legal consequences from the doubled form.

## Hannah's Bold Prayer: Divine Names, the Body's Purpose, and the Obligation of God (continued)



### Three Women's Mitzvot

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״בָּעֳנִי אֲמָתֶךָ״. ״אַל תִשְׁכַּח אֶת אֲמָתֶךָ״. ״וְנָתַתָּה לַאֲמָתְךָ״.
> **Translation:** In the oath/prayer uttered by Hannah, she refers to herself as “Your servant” [*amatekha*] three times: **“The affliction of Your maidservant…**and **not forget Your maidservant and will give Your maidservant”** (I Samuel 1:11).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בְּרַבִּי חֲנִינָא: שָׁלֹשׁ אֲמָתוֹת הַלָּלוּ לָמָּה? — אָמְרָה חַנָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, שְׁלֹשָׁה בִּדְקֵי מִיתָה בָּרָאתָ בָּאִשָּׁה, וְאָמְרִי לַהּ: שְׁלֹשָׁה דִּבְקֵי מִיתָה. וְאֵלּוּ הֵן: נִדָּה, וְחַלָּה וְהַדְלָקַת הַנֵּר. כְּלוּם עָבַרְתִּי עַל אַחַת מֵהֶן?! —
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yosei, son of Rabbi Ḥanina, said: Why** are **these three maidservants [*amatot*]** cited in the verse? They are cited to teach that **Hannah said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, You have created three crucibles** potentially leading to **death in a woman,** where she is particularly vulnerable. Alternatively, **some say:** Master of the Universe, You have created **three accelerants of death** in a woman. **They are** mitzvot that, as a rule, pertain to women: Observing the *halakhot* of **a menstruating woman,** separating ***ḥalla*** from dough, **and lighting** Shabbat **candles. Have I ever violated one of them?** Hannah attests to her status as God’s maidservant [ama]. The reference to these three mitzvot is drawn from the etymological similarity between *amatekha*, your maidservant, and *mita*, death.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְנָתַתָּ לַאֲמָתְךָ זֶרַע אֲנָשִׁים״.
> **Translation:** Later in her prayer, Hannah says: **“And You will grant Your servant an offspring of men.”**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״זֶרַע אֲנָשִׁים״? אָמַר רַב: גַּבְרָא בְּגוּבְרִין. וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: זֶרַע שֶׁמּוֹשֵׁחַ שְׁנֵי אֲנָשִׁים, וּמַאן אִינּוּן — שָׁאוּל וְדָוִד. וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: זֶרַע שֶׁשָּׁקוּל כִּשְׁנֵי אֲנָשִׁים, וּמַאן אִינּוּן — מֹשֶׁה וְאַהֲרֹן. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״מֹשֶׁה וְאַהֲרֹן בְּכֹהֲנָיו וּשְׁמוּאֵל בְּקוֹרְאֵי שְׁמוֹ״. וְרַבָּנַן אָמְרִי: ״זֶרַע אֲנָשִׁים״ — זֶרַע שֶׁמּוּבְלָע בֵּין אֲנָשִׁים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is** the meaning of **“an offspring of men”? Rav said:** Hannah prayed for **a man among men,** a son who would be outstanding and exceptional. **And Shmuel said:** This expression means **an offspring who will anoint two men** to royalty. **And who were they? Saul and David. And Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** Hannah prayed that she would bear **an offspring who** would be **the equivalent of two** of the world’s greatest **men. And who were they? Moses and Aaron. As it is stated: “Moses and Aaron among His priests, and Samuel among those who call His name”** (Psalms 99:6). In this verse, Hannah’s son, Samuel, is equated to Moses and Aaron. **And the Rabbis say: “An offspring of men”:** Hannah prayed for **an offspring who would be inconspicuous among men,** that he would not stand out in any way.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי אֲתָא רַב דִּימִי, אֲמַר לֹא אָרוֹךְ וְלֹא גּוּץ, וְלֹא קָטָן וְלֹא אַלָּם, וְלֹא צָחוֹר וְלֹא גִּיחוֹר, וְלֹא חָכָם וְלֹא טִפֵּשׁ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara relates: **When Rav Dimi came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia, **he said** in explanation: Hannah prayed that her son would not be conspicuous among men; **neither too tall nor too short; neither too small nor too fat; neither too white nor too red; neither too smart nor too stupid.**

The *Gemara* then turns to the three repetitions of the word *amatecha* — "your maidservant" — in Hannah's prayer: *ve-natata la-amatecha zera anashim* — and You shall give to Your maidservant a seed of people.

*Amar Rabbi Yosi bar Chanina: gimel amtot halalu lama amar* — why does Hannah say "your maidservant" three times? *Amra Chana lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu: Ribbono Shel Olam, shlosha bidkei mita barata b'isha* — Hannah said before God: Master of the Universe, You have created in a woman three trials that carry the potential of death. *V'amrei lah shlosha difkei mita* — and some say: three ways that death can cleave to a woman. *V'elu hen: niddah, challah, ve-hadlakat haner* — and these are: the laws of *niddah*, the separation of *challah*, and the lighting of the Sabbath candle.

These three *mitzvot* are enumerated in the chapter *Bameh Madlikin* as particularly the obligations of women, and they speak to the domestic sphere of a woman's life: the *challah* relates to food, the lighting of the candle to the light of the home, and the *niddah* to her bodily sanctity. According to the *Mishna* in *Bameh Madlikin*, a woman who transgresses these obligations faces danger at childbirth, when death can come upon her.

Hannah therefore says: *klum avarti al achat mehen* — have I ever transgressed any one of these? I have kept all of my obligations and all of my *mitzvot*. Therefore You should answer my prayer.

*Ve-natata la-amatecha zera anashim* — and You shall give Your maidservant a seed of people. What does *zera anashim* mean? *Amar Rav: gavra b'govrin* — a man among men, someone outstanding among others. Rashi explains this as someone of great distinction who stands out among his peers.

*U-Shmuel amar: zera she-mosheach shnei anashim* — Shmuel offers a different reading: a seed that will anoint two men. *U-man inun? Shaul ve-David* — who are they? Saul and David, for Hannah's son was Samuel, who would anoint both kings.

*Rabbi Yochanan amar: zera she-shakul ke-shnei anashim* — Rabbi Yochanan said: a seed that is equal in worth to two great men. *U-man inun? Moshe va-Aharon* — who are the two to whom Samuel is compared? Moses and Aaron. *Shene'emar: Moshe ve-Aharon be-chohanav, u-Shmuel be-korei shemo* — as it is written: Moses and Aaron among His priests, and Samuel among those who call upon His name. Samuel is thus considered equal in stature to Moses and Aaron combined.

*Ve-rabanan amri: zera anashim zera she-muvla bein anashim* — the anonymous rabbis offer a contrasting interpretation: a seed absorbed among people, one who does not stand out at all. *Ki ata Rav Dimi amar: lo aroch ve-lo gutz, ve-lo katan ve-lo ilem, ve-lo tzachor ve-lo gichor, ve-lo chacham ve-lo tipesh* — not too tall and not too short, not too thin and not too fat, not too fair and not too swarthy, not even too wise and not too foolish. Average.

It is worth noting that those who gave the interpretation of "average" rather than "outstanding" are the anonymous *rabanan* — perhaps not themselves figures of exceptional renown. As to why one would pray for an average child, Rashi explains that *shelo yehei tamah bein ha-briyot* — he should not be so conspicuous as to attract the evil eye. But there is another way to read it: Hannah is simply saying, I am not asking for anything special. I don't need a superstar. Any child — I will be happy with any child. The very request for an ordinary child speaks to the poignancy of her longing. Don't tell me you'll give me a better one; I just want a child.

## Hannah's Bearing a Child and the Sin of Paskening Before One's Teacher



### Four Cubits Law

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אֲנִי הָאִשָּׁה הַנִּצֶּבֶת עִמְּכָה בָּזֶה״, אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ בֶּן לֵוִי: מִכָּאן שֶׁאָסוּר לֵישֵׁב בְּתוֹךְ אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת שֶׁל תְּפִלָּה.
> **Translation:** When Hannah came to the Temple with her son Samuel, she told Eli: “My lord, as your soul lives, my lord, **I am the woman who stood here with you** to pray to the Lord” (I Samuel 1:26). **Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi said: From here** the *halakha* **that it is forbidden to sit within four cubits of** one who is **praying** is derived. As the verse says: “Who stood here with you,” indicating that Eli stood alongside Hannah because she was praying.

*Ani ha'isha ha-nitzevet imcha ba-zeh* — I was the woman who stood here with you. The following year, having given birth to Samuel, Hannah returned to Eli and said these words, identifying herself as the one who had prayed in his presence.

*Amar Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: mikan she-asur leshev be-toch arba amot shel tefillah* — Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi derives from here that it is forbidden to sit within four cubits of a person who is praying. The derivation: Hannah says *nitzevet imcha* — standing with you — implying that Eli too had been standing; thus one may not sit within the four cubits that surround a person in prayer.

*Tosafot* adds that the word *imcha* is written *malei* with a *hei* — *ayin, mem, chaf, hei* — which textually implies that one may sit in the fifth *amah*, but not within the first four. *Tosafot* further clarifies that the prohibition is not limited to the front of the one praying but applies on the sides as well — *bein milfanav bein min ha-tzedadim* — whether in front or to the sides.

Moreover, *Tosafot* cites the responsa of the *Geonim* who specify that the prohibition applies specifically to one who is *yoshev u-vatel* — sitting and doing nothing at all. But *aval haya osek bi-kriat shema u-berachot ke-yotzeh bahen* — if the person is engaged in reciting the *Shema* or similar prayers, that is permitted. The concern here is therefore not merely one of distraction but of appropriateness: when I share the same space as someone standing before God in prayer, I cannot be entirely oblivious to that reality. I cannot simply sit there doing nothing. But if I am myself engaged in prayer — even listening to the repetition of the *Shaztz* — that addresses the impropriety.

This has practical implications for crowded synagogues. If someone is standing in the *Shemoneh Esrei* behind you and you are already seated in that space, the permissibility depends on whether you are yourself engaged in some form of prayer. Simply sitting and being present while doing nothing is the problematic case; participating in *Chazarat HaShatz* would not be.

### Samuel's precocious ruling: The sin of paskening in front of one's master Eli

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אֶל הַנַּעַר הַזֶּה הִתְפַּלָּלְתִּי״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: שְׁמוּאֵל מוֹרֵה הֲלָכָה לִפְנֵי רַבּוֹ הָיָה. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּשְׁחֲטוּ אֶת הַפָּר וַיָּבִיאוּ אֶת הַנַּעַר אֶל עֵלִי״. מִשּׁוּם דְּ״וַיִּשְׁחֲטוּ אֶת הַפָּר״ הֵבִיאוּ הַנַּעַר אֶל עֵלִי?
> **Translation:** Additionally, Hannah’s emphasis in speaking to Eli, **“for this youth I prayed”** (I Samuel 1:27), indicates that she came to protect him from danger. As **Rabbi Elazar said: Samuel was** one who **taught *halakha* in the presence of his teacher.** Hannah wanted to pray that he not be punished by death at the hand of Heaven for his transgression, **as it is stated: “And they slaughtered the cow and they brought the youth to Eli”** (I Samuel 1:25). This verse is puzzling. **Because they slaughtered the cow,** therefore, **they brought the youth to Eli?** What does one have to do with the next?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, אָמַר לָהֶן עֵלִי: קִרְאוּ כֹּהֵן, לֵיתֵי וְלִשְׁחוֹט. חֲזַנְהוּ שְׁמוּאֵל דַּהֲווֹ מְהַדְּרִי בָּתַר כֹּהֵן לְמִישְׁחַט, אֲמַר לְהוּ: לְמָה לְכוּ לְאַהְדּוֹרֵי בָּתַר כֹּהֵן לְמִישְׁחַט? שְׁחִיטָה בְּזָר כְּשֵׁרָה! אַיְיתוּהוּ לְקַמֵּיהּ דְּעֵלִי. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מְנָא לָךְ הָא? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מִי כְּתִיב ״וְשָׁחַט הַכֹּהֵן״?! ״וְהִקְרִיבוּ הַכֹּהֲנִים״ כְּתִיב, מִקַּבָּלָה וְאֵילָךְ מִצְוַת כְּהוּנָּה, מִכָּאן לַשְּׁחִיטָה שֶׁכְּשֵׁרָה בְּזָר.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** this is what happened: **Eli said to** those who brought the offering: **Call a priest; he will come and slaughter** the offering. **Samuel saw them looking for a priest to slaughter** the animal. **He said to them: Why do you** need **to look for a priest to slaughter** it? **Slaughter** of an offering performed **by a non-priest is valid. They brought him before Eli** to clarify his statement. Eli **said to him: How do you know this?** Samuel **said to him: Is it written** in the Torah: **And the priest shall slaughter** indicating that the offering may only be slaughtered by a priest? **It is written: “And the priests shall offer,”** only **from** the stage of **receiving** the blood in the bowls **and onward is it a mitzva** incumbent upon **priests** alone. **From here** the *halakha* that **slaughter by a non-priest is acceptable** is derived.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מֵימָר שַׁפִּיר קָא אָמְרַתְּ, מִיהוּ מוֹרֶה הֲלָכָה בִּפְנֵי רַבָּךְ אַתְּ, וְכׇל הַמּוֹרֶה הֲלָכָה בִּפְנֵי רַבּוֹ חַיָּיב מִיתָה. אָתְיָא חַנָּה וְקָא צָוְוחָה קַמֵּיהּ: ״אֲנִי הָאִשָּׁה הַנִּצֶּבֶת עִמְּכָה בָּזֶה וְגוֹ׳״. אֲמַר לַהּ: שִׁבְקִי לִי דְּאֶעֶנְשֵׁיהּ, וּבְעֵינָא רַחֲמֵי, וְיָהֵיב לָךְ רַבָּא מִינֵּיהּ. אֲמַרָה לֵיהּ: ״אֶל הַנַּעַר הַזֶּה הִתְפַּלָּלְתִּי״.
> **Translation:** Eli **said to** Samuel: **You have spoken well** and your statement is correct, but **nevertheless, you are** one who **issued a halakhic ruling in the presence of your teacher, and anyone who issues a halakhic ruling in the presence of his teacher,** even if the particular *halakha* is correct, **is liable for death** at the hand of Heaven for showing contempt for his teacher. **Hannah came and shouted before him: “I am the woman who stood here with you** to pray to the Lord;” do not punish the child who was born of my prayers. **He said to her: Let me punish him, and I will pray** for mercy, that the Holy One, Blessed be He, **will grant you** a son who will be **greater than** this one. **She said to him: “For this youth I prayed”** and I want no other.

*Amar Rabbi Elazar: Shmuel moreh halacha lifnei rabbo hayah* — Rabbi Eleazar asserts a remarkable claim: Samuel actually issued a *halachic* ruling in the presence of his teacher Eli, which is a grave transgression. We know from another passage in the *Gemara* that this was among the sins of Nadab and Abihu — that they issued a *halachic* ruling in the presence of Moses, which is why they died.

How do we know Samuel did this? *Shene'emar: vayishchatu et ha-par vayavi'u et ha-na'ar el Eli* — as it says: they slaughtered the ox and brought the child to Eli. What is the connection between these two acts? The *Gemara* explains: *Ela amar lahem Eli, kiru kohen ve-yavo ve-yishchat* — Eli said to the people around the sacrifice: go find a priest to come and slaughter the animal. *Chazei Shmuel de-hava ka-mehadir batar kohen le-mishchat* — Samuel, who was at most three or five years old, saw them searching for a priest to perform the slaughter. *Amar lehu: lamah lachun le-hadurei batar kohen le-mishchat, shechita be-zar kesherah* — he said to them: why are you going to look for a priest? Slaughter performed by a non-priest is valid.

*Ayyitayu le-kameh de-Eli* — they brought him before Eli. *Amar lei: mina lach ha?* — Eli said to him: where did you learn this? *Amar lei: mi ketiv ve-shachat ha-kohen?* — Samuel replied: is it written anywhere that "the priest shall slaughter"? *Ve-hikrivu ha-kohanim* — the verse says the priests shall bring near (the blood). So the priests' service begins from the receiving of the blood. *Mi-kabala ve-eilech mitzvat kehunah* — from the receiving of the blood onward is the priestly obligation. *Mikan de-shechitah kesherah be-zar* — from here we derive that slaughter performed by a non-priest is valid.

*Amar lei: meimar shapir kamart* — Eli said to him: what you said is correct, it is a good argument. *Mihu, moreh halacha bifnei ravcha* — however, you have issued a *halachic* ruling in the presence of your teacher. *Ve-chol ha-moreh halacha bifnei rabbo chayav misa* — and whoever issues a ruling in the presence of his teacher deserves a form of death.

*Atya Chana ve-katzcha kamei* — Hannah came and cried out before Eli. *Ani ha'isha ha-nitzevet imcha ba-zeh* — I was the woman who stood with you in prayer those years ago and prayed for this child. *Amar lah: shivki li de-aktlei, u-veina rachamei, ve-yahiv lach raba minei* — he said to her: let me call upon God to give him his deserved punishment, and I will pray that you be given a son greater than this one in his place. She replied: *el ha-na'ar ha-zeh hitpalalti* — I prayed for this child. This is my child, the one I prayed for — do not tell me you can replace him.

The *derasha* here plays on the word *ha-zeh* — this one, specifically this one. And it also echoes what was said earlier about Hannah not requesting anything special: don't think you can substitute a better child for this one. It is this child. Eli, and somehow Samuel survived the threatened punishment.

*Tosafot* raises an important question: what makes Eli Samuel's teacher at this point? This was the first time Samuel was being brought to him. *Tosafot* answers: *af al pi she-lo lamad adayin lefanav, mi-kol makom gadol ha-dor hayah, u-va lilmod lefanav* — even though he had not yet studied in Eli's presence, Eli was nonetheless the *gadol ha-dor*, the preeminent scholar of his generation, and since Samuel was destined to study under him, he already bore the status of a teacher before whom one does not rule. The fact that Samuel was three years old, or however young he was, is irrelevant — we are speaking not of human courts of punishment but of a divine deserving. This is the extraordinary claim the *Gemara* makes.

### Hannah's refusal: This is the child I prayed for, not a replacement

The exchange between Hannah and Eli on this point conveys something beyond the mere *halacha* of *moreh halacha bifnei rabbo*. When Eli offers her a replacement — a greater son — and Hannah insists that *el ha-na'ar ha-zeh hitpalalti*, she refuses the exchange with the same intensity with which she earlier asked for a child. This child, this specific one. One might connect this to the narrative that follows immediately in the biblical text: Samuel sleeps in the precincts of the sanctuary and hears God's voice calling him, while Eli does not. The story itself then enacts the eclipsing of Eli by Samuel. But Hannah's insistence that there is no substitute foreshadows that irreplaceability.

## Hannah's Bold Prayer: Divine Names, the Body's Purpose, and the Obligation of God (continued) (continued)



### Hannah's argument from creation: Why you gave me a body meant to nurse a child

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְחַנָּה הִיא מְדַבֶּרֶת עַל לִבָּהּ״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בֶּן זִמְרָא: עַל עִסְקֵי לִבָּהּ. אָמְרָה לְפָנָיו: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, כׇּל מַה שֶּׁבָּרָאתָ בָּאִשָּׁה, לֹא בָּרָאתָ דָּבָר אֶחָד לְבַטָּלָה: עֵינַיִם לִרְאוֹת, וְאׇזְנַיִם לִשְׁמוֹעַ, חוֹטֶם לְהָרִיחַ, פֶּה לְדַבֵּר, יָדַיִם לַעֲשׂוֹת בָּהֶם מְלָאכָה, רַגְלַיִם לְהַלֵּךְ בָּהֶן, דַּדִּים לְהָנִיק בָּהֶן. דַּדִּים הַלָּלוּ שֶׁנָּתַתָּ עַל לִבִּי לָמָּה? לֹא לְהָנִיק בָּהֶן?! תֵּן לִי בֵּן, וְאָנִיק בָּהֶן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara continues to deal with Hannah’s prayer. It is said: **“And Hannah spoke on her heart.”** Several interpretations are offered to explain her use of the phrase “on her heart” instead of the common phrase to her heart (Maharsha). **Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Yosei ben Zimra:** Hannah spoke to God **concerning matters of her heart. She said before Him: Master of the Universe,** of **all** the organs **You created in a woman, You have not created one in vain.** Every organ fulfills its purpose; **eyes to see, ears to hear, a nose to smell, a mouth to speak, hands with which to perform labor, feet with which to walk, breasts with which to nurse.** If so, **these breasts that You placed upon my heart, to what** purpose did You place them? Was it **not** in order **to nurse with them? Grant me a son and I will nurse with them.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בֶּן זִמְרָא: כׇּל הַיּוֹשֵׁב בְּתַעֲנִית בְּשַׁבָּת קוֹרְעִים לוֹ גְּזַר דִּינוֹ שֶׁל שִׁבְעִים שָׁנָה. וְאַף עַל פִּי כֵן חוֹזְרִין וְנִפְרָעִין מִמֶּנּוּ דִּין עוֹנֶג שַׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** Tangentially, the Gemara also cites an additional statement that **Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Yosei ben Zimra: Anyone who sits** in observance of a **fast on Shabbat,** his merit is great and **they tear up** and repeal **his sentence of seventy years;** because everyone is enjoying himself and a feast is prepared, it is more difficult to fast on Shabbat than on any other day. **Nevertheless, they then hold him accountable** for failing to fulfill **the *halakha* of delight of Shabbat**.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי תַּקַּנְתֵּיהּ? אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: לִיתֵּיב תַּעֲנִיתָא לְתַעֲנִיתָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is his remedy** to atone and avoid punishment? **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said: He must sit** in observance of another **fast** on a weekday to atone **for the fast** on Shabbat.

*Ve-Chana hi medaberet al libah* — and Hannah was speaking from her heart. Returning to the earlier verse, *Amar Rabbi Elazar mishum Rabbi Yosei bin Zimra: al iskei libah* — regarding matters of her heart. The *Gemara* now unpacks what "matters of her heart" means beyond intent and feeling.

*Amra lefanav: Ribbono shel Olam, kol mah she-barata b'isha lo barata davar echad le-vatalah* — she said before God: Master of the Universe, everything You created in a woman You created for a purpose, nothing was created for nothing. *Einayim lirot* — eyes to see; *ve-oznayim lishmoa* — ears to hear; *chotem le-hariach* — a nose to smell; *peh le-daber* — a mouth to speak; *yadayim la-asot bahem melachah* — hands with which to do work; *raglayim le-halech bahem* — feet to walk; *ve-dadim le-hanik bahem* — and breasts with which to nurse a child.

*Dadim elalu she-natata al libi, lamah, lo le-hanik bahem?* — these breasts that You placed upon my heart — why? Is it not so that I should nurse a child? *Ten li ben ve-anik bahem* — give me a son so that I may nurse him. The *Gemara* reads *al libah* — on her heart — as referring literally to the position of the breasts, giving the anatomical argument its full weight. This is the most poignant of all Hannah's arguments: the body You gave me is built for motherhood. Why have You given me this body if not so that I should use it?

Following this passage, a related ruling of Rabbi Eleazar in the name of Rabbi Yosei bin Zimra is mentioned: *kol ha-yoshev be-ta'anit be-Shabbat, kora'in lo gzar dino shel shiv'im shanah* — one who fasts on *Shabbat* — specifically in the context of a *ta'anit chalom*, a fast following a disturbing dream, which according to the *Geonim* should be observed immediately even if it falls on *Shabbat* — even a divine decree that has stood for seventy years will be torn up. Rashi explains that fasting on *Shabbat* while others are eating and enjoying the day is an act of great difficulty and therefore carries great efficacy.

*Ve-af al pi chein kofin oto le-his'anos od pa'am achas be-Shabbat* — nonetheless, one must still undergo another fast on a subsequent *Shabbat*, because even though the original fast was necessary and effective, one has still transgressed the obligation of *oneg Shabbat*. *Mai takantei* — how does one repair this deficiency of *oneg Shabbat*? *Amar Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak: leisiv tanisa le-tanisa* — one fasts for having fasted, meaning one does a fast that atones for the missed *oneg Shabbat*.

## God's Responsibility in Human Sin: The Three Verses That Sustain Israel



### Aggressive Prayer Models

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: חַנָּה הֵטִיחָה דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״וְתִתְפַּלֵּל עַל ה׳״, מְלַמֵּד שֶׁהֵטִיחָה דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה.
> **Translation:** After explaining the uncommon expression, on her heart, the Gemara cites an additional statement in the matter of Hannah. **And Rabbi Elazar said: Hannah spoke impertinently toward** God **on High. As it is stated: “And she prayed onto the Lord,”** as opposed to the common phrase: To the Lord. This **teaches that she spoke impertinently toward on High.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אֵלִיָּהוּ הֵטִיחַ דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְאַתָּה הֲסִבֹּתָ אֶת לִבָּם אֲחֹרַנִּית״. אָמַר רַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר רַבִּי יִצְחָק: מִנַּיִן שֶׁחָזַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא וְהוֹדָה לוֹ לְאֵלִיָּהוּ,
> **Translation:** **And** on a similar note, **Rabbi Elazar said** that **Elijah spoke impertinently toward** God **on High** as well in his prayer at Mount Carmel, **as it is stated:** “Answer me, Lord, answer me, that this people will know that You are the Lord, God, **and You have turned their hearts backward”** (I Kings 18:37), claiming that God caused Israel to sin. On this topic, **Rabbi Shmuel bar Rabbi Yitzḥak said: From where do we know that the Holy One, Blessed be He, ultimately conceded to Elijah** that he was correct?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּכְתִיב: ״וַאֲשֶׁר הֲרֵעֹתִי״.
> **Translation:** **As it is written** in a future prophecy: “In that day, says the Lord, I will assemble the lame, and I will gather those who are abandoned and **those with whom I have dealt in wickedness”** (Micah 4:6). God states that He caused Israel to act wickedly.

*Ve-amar Rabbi Elazar: Chana hiticha devarim klapei ma'ala* — Rabbi Eleazar teaches: Hannah cast her words upward toward God. *Shene'emar: va-titpalel al Hashem* — as it says: and she prayed *al* Hashem — not *el* Hashem, to God, but *al* Hashem, against God or upon God. *Melamed she-hiticha devarim klapei ma'ala* — this teaches that she struck her words, threw them, almost slapped them, toward Heaven.

*Ve-amar Rabbi Elazar: Eliyahu hitich devarim klapei ma'ala* — not only Hannah, but Elijah as well. *Shene'emar: ve-ata hasibota et libam achoranit* — as it says: and You have turned their hearts backward. Elijah said to God: You are the one who made their hearts turn away from You. God, it is Your fault.

*Amar Rabbi Shmuel bar Yitzchak: minayin she-chazar Hakadosh Baruch Hu ve-hoda le-Eliyahu?* — from where do we know that God went back and conceded to Elijah, admitting some degree of responsibility? *De-chtiv: va-asher hareiosi* — as it is written: and that I have caused them to do evil. God takes responsibility, to some degree, for having created within human beings the *yetzer hara*, the evil inclination.

### Three Saving Verses

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חָמָא בְּרַבִּי חֲנִינָא: אִלְמָלֵא שָׁלֹשׁ מִקְרָאוֹת הַלָּלוּ נִתְמוֹטְטוּ רַגְלֵיהֶם שֶׁל שׂוֹנְאֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל.
> **Translation:** Similarly, **Rabbi Ḥama, son of Rabbi Ḥanina, said: Had it not been for these three verses, the legs of the enemies of Israel,** a euphemism for Israel itself, **would have collapsed, as** Israel would have been unable to withstand God’s judgment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חַד דִּכְתִיב: ״וַאֲשֶׁר הֲרֵעֹתִי״, וְחַד דִּכְתִיב: ״הִנֵּה כַחֹמֶר בְּיַד הַיּוֹצֵר כֵּן אַתֶּם בְּיָדִי בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל״, וְחַד דִּכְתִיב: ״וַהֲסִרֹתִי אֶת לֵב הָאֶבֶן מִבְּשַׂרְכֶם וְנָתַתִּי לָכֶם לֵב בָּשָׂר״.
> **Translation:** **One** is the verse just mentioned in which **it is written: “Those whom I have dealt in wickedness.” And one** is the verse in which **it is written: “Behold, like clay in the potter’s hand, so are you in My hand, house of Israel”** (Jeremiah 18:6). **And one** is the verse in which **it is written:** “And I will give you a new heart and a new spirit I will place within you, **and I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and I will give you a heart of flesh”** (Ezekiel 36:26). These three verses indicate that God influences a person’s decisions, and therefore one does not have sole responsibility for his actions.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב פָּפָּא אָמַר מֵהָכָא: ״וְאֶת רוּחִי אֶתֵּן בְּקִרְבְּכֶם וְעָשִׂיתִי אֵת אֲשֶׁר בְּחֻקַּי תֵּלֵכוּ״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa said** there is a clearer proof **from here: “And I will place My spirit within you and I will cause you to walk in My statutes,** and you will observe My decrees and do them” (Ezekiel 36:27).

*Amar Rabbi Chama bi-Rabbi Chanina: ilmala shalosh mikra'ot elalu, nismotitu ragleihem shel sonei Yisrael* — Rabbi Chama the son of Rabbi Chanina said: were it not for these three verses, the legs of Israel — using the euphemistic expression "the enemies of Israel" to refer to the Jewish people themselves — would have collapsed. We would have fallen.

The first: *chad de-chtiv: va-asher hareiosi* — God says: I have done evil, meaning I have created within them the evil inclination, and I bear some share of responsibility.

The second: *ve-chad de-chtiv: hinei ke-chomer be-yad ha-yotzer, kein atem be-yadi Beit Yisrael* — behold, as clay in the hand of the potter, so are you in My hands, O house of Israel. As we approach the Days of Awe, this verse resonates with particular force. Here too there is a *derasha* present: the word *yotzer* (potter) evokes *yetzer* (inclination) — it is God who has fashioned within us the impulse toward evil, and thus God who bears some responsibility for the way we are formed.

The third: *ve-chad de-chtiv: va-hasiroti et lev ha-even mi-vsarkhem ve-natati lachem lev basar* — I will remove from you the heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh. The implication: if God has not yet done this, if we still have hearts of stone, that is a condition God has not yet remedied — and the human failure to act rightly is therefore not entirely upon us.

*Rav Papa amar me-i-hacha: ve-et ruchi etein be-kirbekhem ve-asisi et asher be-chukotai teileiku* — Rav Papa adds a fourth verse: I will place My spirit within you and cause you to walk in My statutes. This too implies that it is God who enables obedience — and if God has not yet done so, a measure of the failure belongs to the divine side of the equation. What is striking about all four of these verses taken together is that the first three speak to our inner constitution — the desires, weaknesses, and hardness of heart built into us by our Creator — while Rav Papa's addition points to something further: not merely how we were made, but how we have been left. That is to say, the argument shifts from the nature God gave us to the circumstances in which God has placed us and the assistance God has thus far withheld.

### Prosperity & Rebellion

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מֹשֶׁה הֵטִיחַ דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּתְפַּלֵּל מֹשֶׁה אֶל ה׳״ אַל תִּקְרֵי ״אֶל ה׳״, אֶלָּא ״עַל ה׳״.
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Elazar said: Moses** also **spoke impertinently toward** God **on High, as it is stated** in the verse following the sin of those who murmured against God in the desert: **“And Moses prayed to the Lord** and the fire subsided” (Numbers 11:2), and this verse is interpreted homiletically: **Do not read to [*el*] the Lord,** but rather **onto [*al*] the Lord,** which indicates that he spoke impertinently.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁכֵּן דְּבֵי רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר בֶּן יַעֲקֹב קוֹרִין לָאַלְפִין עַיְינִין, וְלָעַיְינִין אַלְפִין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the basis for this interpretation: **As** the Sages **of the school of Rabbi Eliezer ben Ya’akov would** indiscriminately **read *alef* as *ayin* and *ayin* as *alef*** and in this case transforming *el* into *al*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי אָמְרִי, מֵהָכָא: ״וְדִי זָהָב״.
> **Translation:** The Sages of **the school of Rabbi Yannai,** however, **say** proof that Moses spoke impertinently toward God on High is derived **from here,** Moses’ rebuke at the beginning of Deuteronomy: **“And Di Zahav”** (Deuteronomy 1:1). This is an entry in a list of places where Moses had spoken to Israel. As there was no place encountered by that name, it is interpreted as an allusion to another matter.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״וְדִי זָהָב״? אָמְרִי דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי: כָּךְ אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, בִּשְׁבִיל כֶּסֶף וְזָהָב שֶׁהִשְׁפַּעְתָּ לָהֶם לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, עַד שֶׁאָמְרוּ ״דַּי״ — הוּא גָּרַם שֶׁעָשׂוּ אֶת הָעֵגֶל.
> **Translation:** We must clarify: **What is** the meaning of **and Di Zahav?** The Sages of **the school of Rabbi Yannai said that Moses said the following before the Holy One, Blessed be He,** to atone for Israel after the sin of the Golden Calf: **Master of the Universe, because of the gold and silver that you lavished upon Israel** during the exodus from Egypt **until they said enough [*dai*];** it was this wealth **that caused** Israel **to make the** Golden **Calf.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרִי דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי: אֵין אֲרִי נוֹהֵם מִתּוֹךְ קוּפָּה שֶׁל תֶּבֶן אֶלָּא מִתּוֹךְ קוּפָּה שֶׁל בָּשָׂר.
> **Translation:** Establishing a general moral principle, the Sages **the school of Rabbi Yannai said: A lion does not roar** standing **over a basket of straw** from which he derives no pleasure, **but** he roars standing **over a basket of meat,** as he only roars when satiated.


*Ve-amar Rabbi Elazar: Moshe hitia devarim klapei ma'ala* — returning to the main theme of aggressive prayer, Rabbi Eleazar teaches: Moses also threw his words toward God. *Shene'emar: va-yitpalel Moshe el Hashem* — it is written: Moses prayed *el* Hashem. *Al tikrei el Hashem ela al Hashem* — do not read "to God" but "against God."

The textual license for this reading is explained: the school of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov would interchange the letters *aleph* and *ayin* in pronunciation — *shekein debei Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov korin le-alfin ayinin u-le-ayinin alfin*. This linguistic phenomenon, the *Gemara* discusses elsewhere, is associated with communities like Beit Shean, a heavily Hellenistic city, where guttural sounds were lost under Greek cultural influence. Most Ashkenazic Jews today similarly do not distinguish between *aleph* and *ayin* in pronunciation, having lost the guttural sounds through centuries of living in non-Semitic linguistic environments. Accordingly, *el Hashem* and *al Hashem* would sound identical in such a dialect.

*Amrei debei Rabbi Yannai: kach amar Moshe lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu, Ribbono Shel Olam: bishvil kesef ve-zahav she-hishpata lahem le-Yisrael, ad she-amru dai, hu garam she-asu et ha-eigel* — in the school of Rabbi Yannai it was taught: this is what Moses said before God: because of the silver and gold that You poured out upon Israel — *Vedi Zahav*, a place name in the opening of Deuteronomy, is read as *dai zahav*, "enough gold" — until they said enough, until they were overwhelmed with wealth, that is what caused them to make the calf. They had so much gold they were looking for trouble. God, You created the circumstances. It is Your fault.

This represents a deepening of the theme. Earlier arguments held that God created human beings with desires and weaknesses. Here the argument turns to circumstances: the conditions You placed them in made sin virtually inevitable.

*Amrei debei Rabbi Yannai: ein ari no'hem mi-toch kupah shel teven ela mi-toch kupah shel basar* — the lion does not roar from a trough of straw, only after devouring a trough of flesh. When you feel too good, too satisfied, too powerful — then you go looking for trouble. Austerity keeps one on the straight and narrow.

### Spoiled Son Parable

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חִיָּיא בַּר אַבָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מָשָׁל לְאָדָם אֶחָד שֶׁהָיָה לוֹ בֵּן. הִרְחִיצוֹ וְסָכוֹ, וְהֶאֱכִילוֹ וְהִשְׁקָהוּ, וְתָלָה לוֹ כִּיס עַל צַוָּארוֹ, וְהוֹשִׁיבוֹ עַל פֶּתַח שֶׁל זוֹנוֹת. מַה יַּעֲשֶׂה אוֹתוֹ הַבֵּן שֶׁלֹּא יֶחֱטָא?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara offers another analogy: **Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba said** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** This is **comparable to a person who had a son; he bathed him and anointed him** with oil, **fed him and gave him drink, and hung a purse** of money **around his neck.** Then, he **brought** his son **to the entrance of a brothel. What could the son do to avoid sinning?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב הוּנָא אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: הַיְינוּ דְּאָמְרִי אִינָשֵׁי: מְלֵי כְּרֵסֵיהּ זְנֵי בִּישֵׁי. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״כְּמַרְעִיתָם וַיִּשְׂבָּעוּ שָׂבְעוּ וַיָּרׇם לִבָּם עַל כֵּן שְׁכֵחוּנִי״. רַב נַחְמָן אָמַר: מֵהָכָא: ״וְרָם לְבָבֶךָ וְשָׁכַחְתָּ אֶת ה׳״. וְרַבָּנַן אָמְרִי, מֵהָכָא: ״וְאָכַל וְשָׂבַע וְדָשֵׁן וּפָנָה״.
> **Translation:** On a similar note, **Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Huna, said** that **Rav Sheshet said: That is what people say** in a popular maxim: **Filling his stomach is a type of sin, as it is stated: “When they were fed and became full they were sated, and their hearts were lifted and they have forgotten Me”** (Hosea 13:6). **Rav Naḥman said:** This principle is derived not from the verse in Hosea, but **from here: “And your heart is lifted and you forget the Lord”** (Deuteronomy 8:14). **And the Rabbis say** that this principle is derived **from here: “And they will have eaten and been sated and fattened, and they will turn** to other gods” (Deuteronomy 31:20).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי בָּעֵית אֵימָא מֵהָכָא: ״וַיִּשְׁמַן יְשׁוּרוּן וַיִּבְעָט״. אָמַר רַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר נַחְמָנִי אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹנָתָן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁחָזַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא וְהוֹדָה לוֹ לְמֹשֶׁה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְכֶסֶף הִרְבֵּיתִי לָהֶם וְזָהָב עָשׂוּ לַבָּעַל״.
> **Translation:** **And if you wish, say** instead that it is derived **from here: “And Jeshurun grew fat and kicked”** (Deuteronomy 32:15). **Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani said** that **Rabbi Yonatan said: From where** in the Torah is it derived that **the Holy One, Blessed be He, ultimately conceded to Moses** that the reason for the sin of the Golden Calf was indeed the riches lavished upon Israel? **As it is stated: “And I gave them an abundance of silver and gold, which they used for the Ba’al”** (Hosea 2:10).

*Amar Rabbi Oshaya: mashal le-adam she-hayesa lo parah chushash u-va'alat eivarim* — Rabbi Oshaya offers a parable: a man had a thin, bony cow, with its limbs visibly protruding. *He'echilah karshinin* — he fed it a rich grain normally reserved for humans. *Ve-hayesa meva'etet bo* — and it began to kick and rebel against him. *Amru lo: mi garam lach she-tihei meva'etet bi, ela karshinin she-he'echaltich* — they said to it: what caused you to kick against your master? It was the fine grain he fed you.

*Amrei debei Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba amar Rabbi Yochanan: mashal le-adam echad she-hayah lo bein she-richatzo ve-sakho ve-he'echilo ve-hishkihu, ve-tala kis be-tzavaro, ve-hoshivo al petach shel zonot* — and in the school of Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba, Rabbi Yochanan offered a famous parable: a man had a son. He bathed him, anointed him with oil, fed him, gave him drink, and hung a purse full of money around his neck — and then seated him right at the entrance to a brothel. *Mai ya'aseh oto ben she-lo yecheta?* — What do you want from this son? How could he possibly not sin? You have given him all the opportunity, cultivated every appetite, provided every resource, and placed him in the most tempting of circumstances.

*Amar Rav Acha brei de-Rav Huna amar Rav Sheshet: hainu de-amri inshi: malei kerisei zanei bisha* — and this is what people say: a full stomach brings bad sin. *K'mar'itam va-yisba'u, sav'u va-yaram libam, al ken shechechuni* — when they grazed and were sated, they were sated and their heart grew haughty, therefore they forgot Me.

*Rav Nachman amar me-i-hacha: ve-ram levavecha ve-shachachta et Hashem Elokecha* — Rav Nachman cites: your heart will grow haughty and you will forget the Lord your God. *Rabanan amri me-i-hacha: ve-achal ve-sava ve-dashen u-fana* — the Rabbis cite: they ate and were satisfied and grew fat and turned away. *Ve-i bayit eima me-i-hacha: va-yishman Yeshurun va-yivat* — and if you wish, from here: Jeshurun grew fat and kicked.

*Amar Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani amar Rabbi Yonatan: minayin she-chazar Hakadosh Baruch Hu ve-hoda lo le-Moshe?* — and from where do we know that God went back and agreed with Moses, conceding the force of this argument? *Shene'emar: ve-chesef hirbeiti lahem ve-zahav asu la-Ba'al* — as it is written: I gave them much silver and gold, and they made of the gold an offering to the Baal. God Himself acknowledges: I gave them the wealth; they used it for sin. The admission is God's own.

It is striking how much of this passage functions as a model of prayer that is forthright, even confrontational. We were presented earlier with Hannah's model: not the prayer of pure self-abnegation and begging, but a prayer with a claim, a *ta'anah*, a demand. Here that model is deepened: Moses, Hannah, and Elijah all pray *al Hashem* — against God — pointing at the divine and saying: this too is Your doing. The tradition holds up these figures — most dramatically, the tradition of Rabbi Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev, the great advocate for Israel before the heavenly court — as models of how prayer can operate. God, here is my argument; You must answer.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אוֹשַׁעְיָא: מָשָׁל לְאָדָם שֶׁהָיְתָה לוֹ פָּרָה כְּחוּשָׁה וּבַעֲלַת אֵבָרִים, הֶאֱכִילָהּ כַּרְשִׁינִין וְהָיְתָה מְבַעֶטֶת בּוֹ. אָמַר לָהּ: מִי גָּרַם לִיךְ שֶׁתְּהֵא מְבַעֶטֶת בִּי — אֶלָּא כַּרְשִׁינִין שֶׁהֶאֱכַלְתִּיךְ.
> **Translation:** Similarly, **Rabbi Oshaya said:** This is **comparable to a person who had a lean, but large-limbed cow.** At one point, **he fed it lupines,** a choice food, **and** soon thereafter the cow **was kicking him. He said to** the cow: **Who caused you** to begin **kicking me if not the lupines I fed you?** Here, too, the sin was caused by an abundance of good.

## Moses's Prayer for Israel: Holding God Accountable and Defending the People



### God's initial response: Go down, you have lost your status because I'm angry at Israel

*Vayidaber Hashem el Moshe: lech red* — and God spoke to Moses: go, go down. *Mai lech red? Amar Rabbi Elazar: amar lo Hakadosh Baruch Hu le-Moshe, red mi-gedulatecha* — what does "go down" mean? Rabbi Eleazar explains: God said to Moses, descend from your greatness. *Klum natati lecha gedulah ela bishvil Yisrael?* — Did I give you this stature for any reason other than for the sake of Israel? *Ve-achshav Yisrael chatu, ata lama li?* — Now that Israel has sinned, what do I need you for?

This is a striking *midrash*. The plain reading of the verses presents God as still favoring Moses and willing to make him into a great nation in Israel's place. The *midrash* inverts this entirely: God's primary concern was always for Israel. Moses's leadership and stature exist solely in service of the people. When the people sin and God is angry with them, Moses's own position becomes precarious — because his position was never about Moses.

### Moses's moment of weakness and then recovery: Only when God asks him to let go does he find his strength

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיְדַבֵּר ה׳ אֶל מֹשֶׁה לֶךְ רֵד״. מַאי ״לֶךְ רֵד״? אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר לוֹ הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְמֹשֶׁה: מֹשֶׁה, רֵד מִגְּדוּלָּתְךָ! כְּלוּם נָתַתִּי לְךָ גְּדוּלָּה אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל יִשְׂרָאֵל. וְעַכְשָׁיו יִשְׂרָאֵל חָטְאוּ — אַתָּה לָמָּה לִי? מִיָּד תָּשַׁשׁ כּוֹחוֹ שֶׁל מֹשֶׁה, וְלֹא הָיָה לוֹ כֹּחַ לְדַבֵּר. וְכֵיוָן שֶׁאָמַר ״הֶרֶף מִמֶּנִּי וְאַשְׁמִידֵם״, אָמַר מֹשֶׁה: דָּבָר זֶה תָּלוּי בִּי! מִיָּד עָמַד וְנִתְחַזֵּק בִּתְפִלָּה, וּבִקֵּשׁ רַחֲמִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates upon additional aspects of the sin of the Golden Calf. It is stated: **“And the Lord said to Moses: Go and descend,** for your people whom you have lifted out of the land of Egypt have been corrupted” (Exodus 32:7). **What is** the meaning of **“go and descend”? Rabbi Elazar said: The Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Moses: Moses, descend from your greatness. Isn’t it only for the sake of Israel,** so that you may serve as an emissary, **that I granted you prominence; and now that Israel has sinned, why do I** need **you?** There is no need for an emissary. **Immediately, Moses’ strength waned and he was powerless to speak** in defense of Israel. **And once** God **said** to Moses: **“Leave Me be, that I may destroy them”** (Deuteronomy 9:14), **Moses said** to himself: If God is telling me to let Him be, it must be because **this matter is dependent upon me. Immediately Moses stood and was strengthened in prayer, and asked** that God have **mercy** on the nation of Israel and forgive them for their transgression.

*Miyad tashash kocho shel Moshe, ve-lo haya lo koach le-daber* — immediately Moses's strength was weakened, and he could not respond. He was, in a sense, dismissed. Having been told that he had no standing apart from the people, he could not find the words to speak in their defense. *Ve-keivan she-amar: heref mimeni ve-ashmidem* — but then when God said: leave Me alone and I will destroy them. *Amar Moshe: davar zeh talui bi* — Moses thought: ah, I see this matter depends upon me. If God needs me to let go, then if I do not let go, I can hold Him. *Miyad amad ve-nitchazek bi-tefillah, bikesh rachamim* — immediately he stood and strengthened himself in prayer, and sought compassion on Israel's behalf.

### King & Counselor

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָשָׁל לְמֶלֶךְ שֶׁכָּעַס עַל בְּנוֹ, וְהָיָה מַכֵּהוּ מַכָּה גְּדוֹלָה. וְהָיָה אוֹהֲבוֹ יוֹשֵׁב לְפָנָיו, וּמִתְיָרֵא לוֹמַר לוֹ דָּבָר. אָמַר הַמֶּלֶךְ: אִלְמָלֵא אוֹהֲבִי זֶה שֶׁיּוֹשֵׁב לְפָנַי — הֲרַגְתִּיךָ. אָמַר: דָּבָר זֶה תָּלוּי בִּי. מִיָּד עָמַד וְהִצִּילוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara says: This is **comparable to a king who became angry at his son** who had sinned against him, **and beat him,** administering **a severe beating.** At that moment, **a well-wisher** of the king **was sitting before him** and witnessed the entire event, **and was afraid to say anything to** the king about the excessive beating. Meanwhile, **the king said** to his son: **Were it not for this well-wisher of mine who is sitting before me, I would have killed you.** Upon hearing this, the king’s friend **said** to himself: This is clearly a sign that **this matter,** rescuing the son from the hands of his father, **is dependent upon me. Immediately he stood and rescued him** from the king.

*Mashal le-melech she-ka'as al beno, ve-haya makehu makah gedolah, ve-haya ohavo yoshev lefanav u-mitsta'er lomar lo davar* — like a king who grew angry at his son and was striking him vigorously, while the king's favored counselor sat right before him, in anguish but afraid to say anything. The king was punishing his son — who was the counselor to speak? *Amar ha-melech: ilmalei ohavi ze she-yoshev lefanai, hargaticha* — and then the king said: were it not that my friend is sitting right here before me, I would have killed you.

The counselor understood: the king was sending him a message that was not at all hidden. You care about what I say. My presence matters to you. *Miyad amad ve-hetzilo* — immediately the counselor intervened and saved the son.

So too with Moses: *heref mimeni* — let go of Me — was God's signal that Moses's hold could make a difference. Moses had initially been weakened, thinking he had no standing. But once he understood that God was inviting his intervention — indeed, that God *wanted* to be held — he found his strength.

### Grab Divine Collar

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְעַתָּה הַנִּיחָה לִּי וְיִחַר אַפִּי בָהֶם וַאֲכַלֵּם וְאֶעֱשֶׂה אוֹתְךָ לְגוֹי גָּדוֹל וְגוֹ׳״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֲבָהוּ: אִלְמָלֵא מִקְרָא כָּתוּב, אִי אֶפְשָׁר לְאוֹמְרוֹ. מְלַמֵּד שֶׁתְּפָסוֹ מֹשֶׁה לְהַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, כְּאָדָם שֶׁהוּא תּוֹפֵס אֶת חֲבֵירוֹ בְּבִגְדוֹ, וְאָמַר לְפָנָיו: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, אֵין אֲנִי מַנִּיחֲךָ עַד שֶׁתִּמְחוֹל וְתִסְלַח לָהֶם.
> **Translation:** In an additional aspect of the sin of the Golden Calf, God told Moses: **“Now leave Me be, that My wrath will be enraged against them and I will consume them; and I will make of you a great nation”** (Exodus 32:10). Explaining this verse, **Rabbi Abbahu said: Were the verse not written** in this manner, **it would be impossible to utter** it, in deference to God. The phrase: Leave Me be, **teaches that Moses grabbed the Holy One, Blessed be He, as a person who grabs his friend by his garment** would, **and he said before Him: Master of the Universe, I will not leave You be until You forgive and pardon them.**

*Amar Rabbi Abahu: ilmale mikra katuv i efshar le-omro* — Rabbi Abahu said: were this not written in a verse, one could not say such a thing. *Melamed she-tafas Moshe le-Hakadosh Baruch Hu ke-adam she-tofes et chaveiro bi-vigdo, ve-amar lefanav: Ribbono shel Olam, ein ani manichacha ad she-timchol ve-tislach lahem* — this teaches that Moses grabbed hold of God like a person who grabs his friend by his cloak and said: Master of the Universe, I will not let You go until You forgive and pardon them.

The image is generated by the verb *heref mimeni* — let go of Me — as if Moses in his prayer was physically holding on. The *Gemara* acknowledges the audacity of the image: were it not written in the text, one could not utter it. Yet the text requires it. And this image speaks to something real about the power of prayer: prayer can hold God, can compel divine mercy, can refuse to release the divine until forgiveness comes. Moses's prayer saves the entire Jewish people, and the *Gemara* uses this image — Moses grabbing God by the collar — to convey the force that genuine prayer can have.

### Moses's refusal of replacement: God offers to make him a great nation; Moses insists on Israel

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְאֶעֱשֶׂה אוֹתְךָ לְגוֹי גָדוֹל וְגוֹ׳״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, וּמָה כִּסֵּא שֶׁל שָׁלֹשׁ רַגְלַיִם אֵינוֹ יָכוֹל לַעֲמוֹד לְפָנֶיךָ בִּשְׁעַת כַּעְסֶךְ. כִּסֵּא שֶׁל רֶגֶל אֶחָד — עַל אַחַת כַּמָּה וְכַמָּה!
> **Translation:** In the same verse, God promised Moses: **“And I will make of you a great nation.”** What was Moses’ response? **Rabbi Elazar said: Moses said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, if a chair with three legs,** the collective merit of the three forefathers, **is unable to stand before You in Your moment of wrath, all the more so** that **a chair with one leg,** my merit alone, will be unable to withstand your wrath.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֹא עוֹד אֶלָּא שֶׁיֵּשׁ בִּי בּוֹשֶׁת פָּנִים מֵאֲבוֹתַי, עַכְשָׁיו יֹאמְרוּ: רְאוּ פַּרְנָס שֶׁהֶעֱמִיד עֲלֵיהֶם. בִּקֵּשׁ גְּדוּלָּה לְעַצְמוֹ, וְלֹא בִּקֵּשׁ עֲלֵיהֶם רַחֲמִים.
> **Translation:** **Moreover, but I have** a sense of **shame before my forefathers. Now** they will **say: See** this **leader that** God **placed over** Israel. **He requested greatness for himself but did not pray for** God to have **mercy upon them** in their troubled time.

*Ve-e'eseh otcha le-goy gadol* — I will make you into a great nation. *Amar Rabbi Eleazar: amar Moshe lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu: Ribbono shel Olam, u-mah kisei shel shalosh raglayim eino yachol la'amod lefanecha be-sha'at ka'ascha* — Moses responded: Master of the Universe, if a three-legged chair cannot stand before You when You are angry — meaning Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the three patriarchs whose merit supports Israel — *kisei shel regel achat, al achat kama ve-chama* — how much more so will a one-legged chair fail? If You make me into a nation, there will be only my own merit to stand upon, and how much more will they be in trouble when they anger You.

*Ve-lo od ela she-yesh bi boshet panim me-avotai* — and furthermore, Moses says, I will be shamed before my forefathers. *She-me-achshav yomru: re'u parnas she-he'emid aleihem, bikesh gedulah le-atzmo ve-lo bikesh aleihem rachamim* — for they will say: look at this leader God placed over them — he was perfectly content to be elevated and given greatness for himself, and did not see that his primary role was to seek mercy for the people.

Moses refuses the exchange utterly. His stature is for the people, not for himself — precisely as God had said. And having grasped this, Moses can argue back to God with the same logic God had used against him: if my status is entirely for Israel's sake, then making me into a great nation in their place is no exchange at all. It is a betrayal of the very purpose for which I was elevated.

## Moses's Extended Argument Before God: Legalistic Prayer and Divine Concession



### Prayer Makes Ill

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיְחַל מֹשֶׁה אֶת פְּנֵי ה׳״, אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁעָמַד מֹשֶׁה בִּתְפִלָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, עַד שֶׁהֶחֱלָהוּ. וְרָבָא אָמַר: עַד שֶׁהֵפֵר לוֹ נִדְרוֹ. כְּתִיב הָכָא: ״וַיְחַל״, וּכְתִיב הָתָם: ״לֹא יַחֵל דְּבָרוֹ״, וְאָמַר מָר: הוּא אֵינוֹ מֵיחֵל, אֲבָל אֲחֵרִים מְחִלִּין לוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Torah continues: **“And Moses beseeched [*vayḥal*] before the Lord”** (Exodus 32:11). Many interpretations were given for this uncommon term, *vayḥal*: **Rabbi Elazar said:** It **teaches that Moses stood in prayer before the Holy One, Blessed be He, until it made him ill [***heḥelahu***]** from overexertion. **And Rava said:** Moses stood in prayer **until he nullified His vow,** as the term *vayḥal* alludes to nullification of an oath. **Here it is written *vayḥal*, and there** referring to vows, **it is written: “He shall not nullify [*lo yaḥel*] his word”** (Numbers 30:3). **And** with regard to vows, **the Master said: He** who vowed **cannot nullify** his vow, **but others,** the court, **can nullify** his vow **for him.** Here, it is as if Moses nullified the Lord’s vow to destroy Israel.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁמָּסַר עַצְמוֹ לְמִיתָה עֲלֵיהֶם. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְאִם אַיִן מְחֵנִי נָא מִסִּפְרְךָ״.
> **Translation:** **And Shmuel said:** The term *vayḥal* **teaches that** Moses **gave his life,** from the term *ḥalal*, a dead person, **for** Israel, **as it is stated: “And if not, erase me, please, from Your book”** (Exodus 32:32).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא אָמַר רַב יִצְחָק: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁהֻחֲלָה עֲלֵיהֶם מִדַּת רַחֲמִים.
> **Translation:** **Rava,** also interpreting this verse, **said** that **Rav Yitzḥak said:** The term *vayḥal* **teaches that he caused the Divine Attribute of Mercy to take effect [*heḥela*] upon them.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן אָמְרִי: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם! חוּלִּין הוּא לְּךָ מֵעֲשׂוֹת כַּדָּבָר הַזֶּה.
> **Translation:** **And the Rabbis say** that this term constitutes the essence of Moses’ claim: **It teaches that Moses said before the Holy One Blessed be He: Master of the Universe! It is a sacrilege [*ḥullin*] for You to do something like this.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיְחַל מֹשֶׁה אֶת פְּנֵי ה׳״, תַּנְיָא, רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר הַגָּדוֹל אוֹמֵר: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁעָמַד מֹשֶׁה בִּתְפִלָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא עַד שֶׁאֲחָזַתּוּ אֲחִילוּ. מַאי אֲחִילוּ? אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אֵשׁ שֶׁל עֲצָמוֹת. מַאי אֵשׁ שֶׁל עֲצָמוֹת? אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: אִשָּׁתָא דְגַרְמֵי.
> **Translation:** And another interpretation of the verse, **“And Moses beseeched [*vayḥal*] before the Lord.” It was taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi Eliezer the Great says: This** term **teaches that Moses stood in prayer before the Holy One, Blessed be He, until he was overcome by *aḥilu*.** Even the Sages were unfamiliar with this term. Therefore, the Gemara asks: **What is** the meaning of ***aḥilu*? Rabbi Elazar,** an *amora* of Eretz Yisrael, **said** that *aḥilu* is **fire in the bones.** However, this expression was familiar in Eretz Yisrael but not in Babylonia. They asked in Babylonia: **What is** the disease that they called **fire of the bones? Abaye said** that is a disease known in Babylonia as ***eshta degarmei*,** which in Aramaic means **fire of the bones;** in other words, a fever.

*Va-yechal Moshe et penei Hashem Elokav* — and Moses importuned the face of the Lord his God. *Amar Rabbi Eleazar: melamed she-amad Moshe bi-tfillah lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu ad she-hechelohu* — Rabbi Eleazar explains: this teaches that Moses stood in prayer before God until he *hechelohu*. Rashi interprets *hechelohu* as *hiftsir bo* — until he overwhelmed God, importuned Him relentlessly with prayer. The Steinsaltz commentary, however, understands *hechelohu* in its more literal sense as "made Him ill," from the root *choleh* — as if Moses prayed so insistently that God was, as it were, made ill by the importuning.

Multiple interpretations of the word *va-yechal* are then presented. *Rav Sheshet amar: va-yechal* relates to *lo yachel devaro* — he shall not violate his word. *Aval amar Mar: hu eino mechal, aval acherim mechilin lo* — a person cannot annul his own vow, but others can annul it for him. Thus *Rava* derives: Moses convened a court and released God from His vow — the vow being God's word that He would destroy Israel, which, once spoken by God, constituted a form of oath. Moses assembled a *beit din* and, through the proper legal mechanism of releasing a vow, annulled it.

*Amar Rabbi Eleazar: u-Shmuel amar, melamed she-masar atzmo le-misah aleihem* — Shmuel offers yet another reading: Moses was prepared to die for them. *Ve-im ayin, mecheini na mi-sifr'echa* — and if not, erase me from Your book, meaning: kill me. This connects *va-yechal* to *chalal*, a corpse. *Amar Avram amar Rabbi Yitzchak: melamed she-hechila aleihem midat ha-rachamim* — another interpretation: he caused the attribute of compassion to descend upon the people. *Ve-rabanan amri: melamed she-amar Moshe lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu: chulin hu lecha me'asot kadavar hazeh* — and the Rabbis say: Moses said to God, it would be a desecration for You to do such a thing, invoking the language of *chulin*, which like *chalilah* means something that would profane the divine name.

*Tanya: Rabbi Elazar Hagadol omer: melamed she-amad Moshe bi-tfillah lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu ad she-achazo achilu* — it is taught in a *baraita*: Rabbi Elazar the Great said: this teaches that Moses stood in prayer before God until he was seized with *achilu*. *Mai achilu? Amar Rabbi Elazar: esha de-atzmot* — what is *achilu*? Rabbi Eleazar says: a burning of the bones, a fever within the bones. *Amar Abaye: eshta de-garmei* — Abaye translates this into Aramaic: a burning of the bones. It was apparently an idiomatic expression for a particular kind of deep internal fever. The image is striking: Moses's intensity of prayer was such that it brought on a physical condition — a burning within his very bones.

### Releasing God from His vow: The halakhic mechanism of annulling a divine oath through a Beis Din

The mechanism Rava identifies deserves attention. When God declared that He would destroy Israel, that declaration constituted a binding oath. An individual cannot annul his own oath, but a *beit din* of three can release him. Moses therefore convened such a court and utilized this formal legal mechanism to annul God's vow. This is not merely a rhetorical flourish; it is presented as a real *halachic* device applied to the divine speech itself. Prayer here takes the form of a legal proceeding — Moses is not simply pleading but operating within the structure of Jewish law to neutralize God's threatened decree.

### Patriarchs' Oath

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״זְכֹר לְאַבְרָהָם לְיִצְחָק וּלְיִשְׂרָאֵל עֲבָדֶיךָ אֲשֶׁר נִשְׁבַּעְתָּ לָהֶם בָּךְ״. מַאי ״בָּךְ״? אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, אִלְמָלֵא נִשְׁבַּעְתָּ לָהֶם בַּשָּׁמַיִם וּבָאָרֶץ, הָיִיתִי אוֹמֵר: כְּשֵׁם שֶׁשָּׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ בְּטֵלִים — כָּךְ שְׁבוּעָתְךָ בְּטֵלָה. וְעַכְשָׁו שֶׁנִּשְׁבַּעְתָּ לָהֶם בְּשִׁמְךָ הַגָּדוֹל, מָה שִׁמְךָ הַגָּדוֹל חַי וְקַיָּים לְעוֹלָם וּלְעוֹלְמֵי עוֹלָמִים — כָּךְ שְׁבוּעָתְךָ קַיֶּימֶת לְעוֹלָם וּלְעוֹלְמֵי עוֹלָמִים.
> **Translation:** As Moses continues his prayer, he says: **“Remember Abraham, Isaac and Israel Your servants, to whom You swore in Your name”** (Exodus 32:13). **What is** the meaning of **in Your name? Rabbi Elazar said: Moses said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, had You sworn to them by the heavens and the earth, I would say: Just as the heavens and the earth will** ultimately **be no more, so too Your oath will be null and void. Now that You swore to them by Your great name, just as Your name lives and stands for all eternity, so too does Your oath live and stand for all eternity.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַתְּדַבֵּר אֲלֵיהֶם אַרְבֶּה אֶת זַרְעֲכֶם כְּכוֹכְבֵי הַשָּׁמָיִם וְכׇל הָאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת אֲשֶׁר אָמַרְתִּי״, הַאי ״אֲשֶׁר אָמַרְתִּי״, ״אֲשֶׁר אָמַרְתָּ״ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ!
> **Translation:** In this verse, Moses continues: **“And You said to them: I will make your offspring as numerous as the stars of heaven, and all this land of which I have spoken** I will give to your offspring that they shall inherit it forever.” The Gemara clarifies a puzzling phrase in this verse. **That** phrase **of which I have spoken, it should** have said: **Of which You have spoken,** as Moses is referring to God’s promise to the forefathers.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: עַד כָּאן דִּבְרֵי תַלְמִיד, מִכָּאן וְאֵילָךְ — דִּבְרֵי הָרַב. וְרַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר נַחְמָנִי אָמַר: אֵלּוּ וָאֵלּוּ דִּבְרֵי תַלְמִיד, אֶלָּא כָּךְ אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, דְּבָרִים שֶׁאָמַרְתָּ לִי ״לֵךְ אֱמוֹר לָהֶם לְיִשְׂרָאֵל״ בִּשְׁמִי, הָלַכְתִּי וְאָמַרְתִּי לָהֶם בִּשְׁמֶךָ. עַכְשָׁו מָה אֲנִי אוֹמֵר לָהֶם?
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Elazar said: To this** point, the verse cites **the words of the student,** Moses; **from this point,** and all this land of which I have spoken, the verse cites **the words of the Master,** God. **And Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani said: These and those are the words of the student;** Moses spoke the entire verse. **Rather, Moses said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, those matters which You told me to go and say to Israel in My name, I went and told** it **to them in Your name.** I have already told Israel of God’s promise to the forefathers. **Now what do I say to them?**


*Zechor le-Avraham le-Yitzchak u-le-Yisrael avadecha, asher nishbata lahem bach* — remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel Your servants, to whom You swore by Yourself. *Mai bach?* — what does it mean "by Yourself"? *Amar Rabbi Eleazar: amar Moshe lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu, Ribbono Shel Olam: ilmale nishbata lahem be-shamayim u-va-aretz, hayiti omer: keshem she-shamayim va-aretz beteilim kach shevuatecha beteila* — Rabbi Eleazar explains: Moses said to God: Master of the Universe, if You had sworn to them by heaven and by earth, I would have said — just as heaven and earth are ultimately finite and will one day cease, so too Your oath can be annulled. *Ve-achshav she-nishbata lahem be-shimcha ha-gadol* — but now that You swore to them by Your own great name, *mah shimcha ha-gadol chai ve-kayam le-olam u-le-olmei olamim, kach shevuatecha kayama le-olam u-le-olmei olamim* — just as Your name lives and endures forever, so Your oath must stand forever. You cannot back out.

This is a legal argument of considerable sophistication. The force of an oath depends on what is invoked as its guarantee. An oath by heaven and earth is limited by the finitude of heaven and earth; when they cease, the oath ceases with them. But an oath sworn by God's own eternal name admits of no such limitation. The name is eternal, and therefore the oath is eternal. Moses is holding God to the terms of the very oath God chose to swear.

*Ve-teidaber aleihem: arbe et zar'achem ke-chochvei ha-shamayim ve-chol ha-aretz ha-zot asher amarti* — And You spoke to them: I will multiply your seed like the stars of heaven, and all this land that I said I will give. *Ve-nachalu le-olam.* And they shall inherit it forever.

*Ha-i asher amarti: asher amarta mi ba'ei lei?* — The phrase *asher amarti* — "that I said" — is grammatically puzzling. If Moses is addressing God, it should say "that You said." *Amar Rabbi Eleazar: ad kan divrei talmid, mikan ve-eilach divrei ha-rav* — Rabbi Eleazar explains: up to this point are the words of the student — Moses speaking to God. From here onward it shifts into the words of the teacher — meaning it is a quotation, Moses quoting God's own earlier speech, in which God said "I said this land." So the shift from second to first person is intentional: Moses is quoting God's own words back to Him.

*Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani amar: elu va-elu divrei talmid* — Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani disagrees: both parts of the verse are Moses's words. How then does one explain "that I said"? *Ela kach amar Moshe lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu, Ribbono shel Olam: devarim she-amarta li, lech emor lahem le-Yisrael bishmi, halachti va-amarti lahem bishmecha, achshav mah ani omer lahem?* — This is what Moses said: the things You told me to go and tell them in Your name — I went and told them in Your name. Now what am I supposed to tell them? He places himself in the middle: You sent me as Your messenger to promise them the land; what am I to say now that You are threatening to destroy them? *Asher amarti* — "that I said" — means: that I told them on Your behalf. Moses made himself the guarantor of the message, and now the message must be honored.

### Moses's pragmatic argument: The nations will say God is weak if He destroys Israel after the miracles and conquest

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, עַכְשָׁיו יֹאמְרוּ אוּמּוֹת הָעוֹלָם תָּשַׁשׁ כֹּחוֹ כִּנְקֵבָה, וְאֵינוֹ יָכוֹל לְהַצִּיל. אָמַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְמֹשֶׁה: וַהֲלֹא כְּבָר רָאוּ נִסִּים וּגְבוּרוֹת שֶׁעָשִׂיתִי לָהֶם עַל הַיָּם. אָמַר לְפָנָיו: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, עֲדַיִין יֵשׁ לָהֶם לוֹמַר: לְמֶלֶךְ אֶחָד יָכוֹל לַעֲמוֹד, לִשְׁלשִׁים וְאֶחָד מְלָכִים אֵינוֹ יָכוֹל לַעֲמוֹד.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Elazar said: Moses** phrased it that way because he **said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, now the nations of the world will say that His strength weakened like a female and He is unable to rescue** the nation of Israel. **The Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Moses: And did** the nations of the world **not already see the miracles and the mighty** acts **that I performed on behalf of** Israel **at the** Red **Sea?** Moses **said before Him: Master of the Universe, they can still say:** The Lord **can stand** up **to a single king** like Pharaoh and defeat him, but **He is unable to stand** up **to the thirty-one kings** in the land of Canaan.

*Va-yomru mi-bilti yacholet Hashem* — they will say, because God was not able. *Yachol Hashem mi-ba'ei lei* — it should say "able," not *yacholet*, which is the feminine form. *Amar Rabbi Eleazar: amar Moshe lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu, Ribbono shel Olam: achshav yomru umot ha-olam, tashash kocho ke-nekevah, ve-eino yachol le-hatzilam* — Rabbi Eleazar explains: Moses said to God: Master of the Universe, now the nations will say Your strength has waned like a woman's — and the *Gemara* has used this feminine image in several places — and You are not able to save them. The feminine form *yacholet* is the textual hook.

*Amar Hakadosh Baruch Hu le-Moshe: velo kvar rau nissim u-gevurot she-asiti lahem al ha-yam?* — God responded: did the nations not already see the miracles and mighty acts I performed for them at the Sea? *Ve-Moshe amar: adayin yesh lahem lomar, melech echad yachol la'amod, u-shloshim ve-echad melachim eino yachol la'amod* — and Moses responded: they could still say — one king, Pharaoh, You were able to overcome; but thirty-one kings of the land of Canaan, that You cannot manage. Yes, what You did at the sea was remarkable, but it does not answer the question of whether You can deliver on the full promise of the land. They will say Your power is limited.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״מִבִּלְתִּי יְכֹלֶת ה׳״: יָכוֹל ה׳ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara moves to a discussion of additional prayers offered by Moses. Moses said that if God fails to bring the Jewish people into Eretz Yisrael, the nations of the world will say: **“The Lord did not have the ability [*yekholet*]** to bring this people into the land which He swore to them, and He killed them in the desert” (Numbers 14:16). The Gemara examines this verse closely: The verse should not have utilized the term *yekholet*, an abstract feminine noun, but rather, **it should have said: “The Lord** was not **able [*yakhol*],”** a masculine verb.

### God concedes to Moses: 'I have forgiven them according to your words'

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁחָזַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא וְהוֹדָה לוֹ לְמֹשֶׁה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיֹּאמֶר ה׳ סָלַחְתִּי כִּדְבָרֶיךָ״. תָּנֵי דְּבֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל: כִּדְבָרֶיךָ, עֲתִידִים אוּמּוֹת הָעוֹלָם לוֹמַר כֵּן.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan said: From where** is it derived that **the Holy One, Blessed be He, ultimately conceded to Moses? As it is said: “And the Lord said: I have forgiven according to your word”** (Numbers 14:20). The Sages **of the school of Rabbi Yishmael taught: According to your word,** it will be, as indeed **in the future the nations of the world will say this.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַשְׁרֵי תַלְמִיד שֶׁרַבּוֹ מוֹדֶה לוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara concludes: **Happy is the student whose teacher concedes to him** as the Lord conceded to Moses.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְאוּלָם חַי אָנִי״, אָמַר רָבָא אָמַר רַב יִצְחָק: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאָמַר לוֹ הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְמֹשֶׁה: מֹשֶׁה, הֶחֱיִיתַנִי בִּדְבָרֶיךָ.
> **Translation:** Explaining the next verse, **“Nevertheless, as I live,** and the glory of the Lord fills the entire world” (Numbers 14:21), **Rava said** that **Rav Yitzḥak said:** This **teaches** that **the Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Moses: Moses, you have given Me life with your words.** I am happy that on account of your arguments, I will forgive Israel.

*Amar Rabbi Yochanan: minayin she-chazar Hakadosh Baruch Hu ve-hodah lo le-Moshe?* — Rabbi Yochanan asks: from where do we know that God went back and conceded to Moses? *Shene'emar: va-yomer Hashem salachti ki-dvarecha* — as it says: and God said, I have forgiven them according to your words. God's forgiveness comes explicitly *ki-dvarecha* — because of what Moses said. Whether it was the argument from the patriarchs' oath, the argument from the nations' perception, or everything Moses had argued — God responds by granting forgiveness.

*Tana devei Rabbi Yishmael: ki-dvarecha, atidim umot ha-olam lomar ken* — the school of Rabbi Yishmael taught: *ki-dvarecha* means: the nations of the world would indeed have said this, exactly as Moses predicted, if God had destroyed Israel. The nations' slander was a real possibility that Moses's argument successfully prevented.

*Ashrei talmid she-rabbo modeh lo* — praised is the student whose teacher concedes to him. This is the closing comment on the exchange — and it is said with something approaching wonder. Moses prevailed in argument before God, and God said: I have conceded.

*Ve-ulam chai ani — amar Rava amar Rabbi Yitzchak: u-melamed she-amar lo Hakadosh Baruch Hu le-Moshe: hecheyitani bi-dvarecha* — Rava said in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak: God said to Moses: *chai ani*, I have lived — you have given Me life through your words. Rashi understands this as meaning that the nations will not say God lacks power; but there is also a deeper resonance: when we pray properly, when we hold God accountable and argue our case before Heaven, we enable God to do what God wishes to do in this world. Our prayer, in a sense, gives life to the divine. It enables God's presence and God's will to be actualized in the world. This is the profound image with which this section closes.

## The Structure of Prayer: Praise Before Request



### The ruling of Rav Simai: One must always praise God before making requests

*Rav Simai: le-olam yesader adam shivcho shel Hakadosh Baruch Hu ve-achar kach yitpalel* — Rav Simai rules: one should always first arrange the praise of God and only afterward pray — meaning, only afterward make one's requests. Here the word *yitpalel* is used in the sense of *bakasha*, petition, in contrast to its earlier use in the sense of praising. This reflects the structure of the *Shemoneh Esrei*: the first three blessings are praise, and only then does one move into the middle section of petitions.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דָּרַשׁ רַבִּי שִׂמְלַאי: לְעוֹלָם יְסַדֵּר אָדָם שִׁבְחוֹ שֶׁל הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, וְאַחַר כָּךְ יִתְפַּלֵּל. מְנָלַן? — מִמֹּשֶׁה, דִּכְתִיב: ״וָאֶתְחַנַּן אֶל ה׳ בָּעֵת הַהִיא״. וּכְתִיב: ״ה׳ אֱלֹהִים אַתָּה הַחִלֹּתָ לְהַרְאוֹת אֶת עַבְדְּךָ אֶת גׇּדְלְךָ וְאֶת יָדְךָ הַחֲזָקָה אֲשֶׁר מִי אֵל בַּשָּׁמַיִם וּבָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר יַעֲשֶׂה כְמַעֲשֶׂיךָ וְכִגְבוּרוֹתֶיךָ״. וּכְתִיב בָּתְרֵיהּ ״אֶעְבְּרָה נָּא וְאֶרְאֶה אֶת הָאָרֶץ הַטּוֹבָה וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** Based on Moses’ prayers, **Rabbi Simlai taught: One should always set forth praise of the Holy One, Blessed be He, and then pray** for his own needs. **From where do we** derive that one should conduct himself in this manner? **From Moses, as it is written** in his prayer: **“And I beseeched the Lord at that time”** (Deuteronomy 3:23). **And** immediately afterward in his prayer, **it is written: “Lord, God, You have begun to show Your servant Your greatness and Your strong hand, for what God is there in the heavens or on earth who can perform deeds such as Yours and Your might”** (Deuteronomy 3:24)? Here, Moses began with praise of God, **and it is** only **thereafter** that **it is written: “Please, let me pass over and see the good land** that is beyond the Jordan, that good hill country and the Lebanon” (Deuteronomy 3:25). Only after his praise did Moses make his personal request.

### Moses Example


*Me-nalan mi-Moshe* — how do we know this from Moses? *De-chtiv: va-etchanan el Hashem* — as it is written: and I importuned the Lord. How did Moses pray? First: *Hashem Elokim, ata hachilota leharot et avdecha et godlecha ve-et yadcha ha-chazakah, asher mi El ba-shamayim u-va-aretz asher ya'aseh ke-ma'asecha u-che-gevurotecha* — Lord God, You have begun to show Your servant Your greatness and Your mighty hand; who is there in all the heavens and the earth who can do as Your deeds and Your mighty acts? This is pure praise. Only after this praise does Moses move to his petition: *ve-chtiv batreih: ebrah na ve-ereh et ha-aretz ha-tovah* — and it is written afterward: let me cross over and see this beautiful land.

Moses first praised God and only then made his request. This is the model from which we derive the structure of our own prayer: praise precedes petition, corresponding to the three opening blessings before the petitionary middle section of the daily *Amidah*. The *Gemara* will continue from here.
