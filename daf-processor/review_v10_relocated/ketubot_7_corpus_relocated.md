## Blister Case



### Intro: Sex on Shabbat

The sugya on daf 7b continues the discussion of *bi'ah rishonah* — first-time sexual relations — on Shabbat, focusing on the concern that breaking the hymen may constitute a prohibited act. The Gemara picks up toward the bottom of 6b with the case of *hamefis mursa b'Shabbos*: one who lances a blister on Shabbat.

### Blister Halakha

The ruling is as follows: *im la'asos lah peh* — if the person's intent is to create an opening in the blister — *chayav*, he is liable. But *im l'hotzi mimenu leicha* — if his intent is merely to extract the pus — *patur*, he is exempt. The question that naturally arises is: for what exactly is one liable when making an opening? This is not an act of wounding in the conventional sense.

### Rashi: Opening = Boneh


Rashi explains: *im niskavin la'asos lah peh, shemiskayvein l'yafosah, lihayos lah pesach zeh l'yom achar, chayav shehu mesakein pesach, v'chayav mishum boneh* — if one intends to create an opening so that the blister will have a fixed aperture going forward, this constitutes fixing an opening, and he is liable on account of *boneh*, the prohibited labor of building. Rashi cites the verse describing the creation of Chava — *vayiven es hatzela* — as the prototype for this category, the same source he invoked earlier in the discussion about first-time intercourse. The parallel is noted, though it remains conceptually curious why creating a small drainage opening in a blister should rise to the level of *boneh*. The act seems to operate on an entirely different scale from the concerns animating the earlier discussion, and it is not immediately obvious why fashioning such an aperture — rather than simply extracting the pus — is the detail that triggers liability. In this sense the *la'asos lah peh* case is perhaps better understood as analogous to making a *kli*, fashioning a functional vessel or aperture, than to anything resembling a conventional act of building.

What is equally striking is what the Gemara does not say here. When one intends only to extract the pus and is therefore exempt, the natural expectation would be that the Gemara explains the exemption simply by noting that this act involves pus rather than blood — since *chavala*, wounding, is fundamentally defined by the extraction of blood. That the Gemara does not rest on this obvious distinction, and instead reaches for a different line of reasoning, is itself surprising, and may reflect an assumption that the *leicha* in the blister contains some blood after all — a point the Gemara's actual answer, discussed in the next section, will make clearer.

### Pakid vs. Akir

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵתִיב רַבִּי אַמֵּי: הַמֵּפִיס מוּרְסָא בְּשַׁבָּת, אִם לַעֲשׂוֹת לָהּ פֶּה — חַיָּיב. וְאִם לְהוֹצִיא מִמֶּנָּה לֵיחָה —
> **Translation:** **Rav Ami raised an objection** from a mishna (*Eduyyot* 2:5): With regard to **one who drains an abscess on Shabbat, if** his intent is **to create** a permanent **opening** so that the abscess will dry, **he is liable** to receive punishment for performing an action similar to the prohibited labor of building on Shabbat. **However, if** he created the opening **to remove pus,**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פָּטוּר. הָתָם, פְּקִיד וַעֲקִיר. הָכָא, פְּקִיד וְלָא עֲקִיר.
> **Translation:** **he is exempt.** In this case, exempt means permitted *ab initio*. Ostensibly, intercourse with a virgin is comparable to creating an opening in an abscess. Why then, would intercourse with a virgin be forbidden on Shabbat? The Gemara rejects the proof: **There,** the pus in the abscess **is pooled** in one place **and** completely **removed** from the vessels in the flesh. In creating the opening, he creates nothing enduring. **Here,** however, in the case of the ruptured hymen, even according to the opinion that the blood **is pooled, it is not** completely **removed** from the blood vessels in the flesh. Through intercourse, the blood is removed from its place, which constitutes an aspect of a prohibited labor.

The Gemara then turns to the second half of the ruling — the exemption when one intends only to extract the pus — and considers how this relates to the earlier discussion about blood released during first intercourse. The Gemara's answer is: *hasam pakid v'akir, hacha pakid v'lo akir* — in the blister case, the pus is both stored and already fully uprooted from the flesh, contained entirely outside the skin. In the case of the hymen, the blood may be stored behind it, but it is not yet uprooted from within the skin. When the hymen breaks, blood is still being released from within the skin — it is bleeding in the full sense.

This answer is somewhat puzzling. One might have expected the Gemara simply to say that the blister case involves pus, not blood, and that the prohibition of *chavala* — wounding — is fundamentally defined by the extraction of blood. The Gemara's distinction between *pakid v'akir* and *pakid v'lo akir* seems to assume that the pus in the blister may contain some blood, which is why the answer focuses on degrees of uprootedness rather than on the substance itself. One might also ask what the Gemara's question was in the first place: the physical question of whether hymenal blood is truly comparable to blood drawn through a wound is not obviously answered by a case about pus in a blister. The Gemara's conclusion is simply that the blister case involves material already fully outside the skin, while the hymenal case does not.

### Pattur Aval Mutar

A further point arises regarding the category of *patur* in the blister case. As established at the beginning of Maseches Shabbos, *patur* on Shabbat ordinarily means *patur aval assur* — exempt from liability but still forbidden. There are, however, three exceptions where *patur* means *patur aval mutar* — exempt and actually permitted. This blister case is one of them, on account of the element of *tza'ar*, physical suffering, involved. Rashi confirms this reading.

## Amoraim Debate



### Rav Ami Permits


With that digression resolved, the Gemara returns to the central question of first-time intercourse on Shabbat. *Rav Ami sharah l'meiva batchilah b'Shabbos* — Rav Ami permitted *bi'ah rishonah* on Shabbat. The debate among the *Tannaim* and earlier *Amoraim* on this question evidently continued into later generations. Upon hearing Rav Ami's ruling, the rabbis of the academy challenged him: *ha lo kesiva kesuva* — but the *ketuva* has not yet been written! The assumption behind this objection is that if the wedding took place on Friday afternoon or Friday night, there would not have been time to write the *ketuva* before Shabbat. And a husband and wife are not permitted to live together without a *ketuva* — indeed, the Gemara will later characterize such an arrangement as resembling *be'as znus*, because without the protections of the *ketuva* the wife lacks the commitment and security that sanctify the relationship and guard against capricious divorce.

### Ketuva & Collateral

Rav Ami's response was: *atfuso matalten* — in the case under discussion, the woman had been given movable property as collateral to hold until the *ketuva* could be formally written. This ensures that even before the document is drawn up, she has concrete security for her eventual collection in the event of divorce. This exchange signals a broader theme of Maseches Ketubot: it is not merely the act of *nisuin* itself — the *chuppah*, the *sheva brachot* — that constitutes a fully sanctioned marriage. The protections of the *ketuva* are themselves a constitutive element of a committed marital relationship, providing reciprocal obligations and security for both parties.

### Other Authorities

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי אַמֵּי שְׁרָא לְמִיבְעַל בַּתְּחִלָּה בְּשַׁבָּת. אָמְרִי לֵיהּ רַבָּנַן: וְהָא לָא כְּתִיבָא כְּתוּבָּתָהּ! אֲמַר לְהוּ: אַתְפְּסוּהָ מִטַּלְטְלִין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara relates: **Rav Ami permitted** one **to engage in intercourse** with his virgin wife for the **first** time **on Shabbat. The Sages said to him: But her marriage contract is not** yet **written.** Engaging in conjugal relations with one’s wife without a marriage contract is considered an act of promiscuity. **He said to them: Have her seize** a portion of her husband’s **movable property** equivalent to the value of her marriage contract, and that will serve as a deposit until he writes the marriage contract. He may then engage in sexual relations with her.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב זְבִיד שְׁרָא לְמִיבְעַל בַּתְּחִלָּה בְּשַׁבָּת. אִיכָּא דְּאָמְרִי: רַב זְבִיד גּוּפֵיהּ בְּעַל בַּתְּחִלָּה בְּשַׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara relates: **Rav Zevid permitted** one **to engage in intercourse** with his virgin wife for the **first** time **on Shabbat. There are** those **who say: Rav Zevid himself engaged in intercourse** with his virgin wife for the **first** time **on Shabbat.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב יְהוּדָה שְׁרָא לְמִיבְעַל בַּתְּחִלָּה בְּיוֹם טוֹב. אָמַר רַב פַּפִּי מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא: לָא תֵּימָא בְּיוֹם טוֹב דִּשְׁרֵי, הָא בְּשַׁבָּת אֲסִיר, דְּהוּא הַדִּין דַּאֲפִילּוּ בְּשַׁבָּת נָמֵי שְׁרֵי, וּמַעֲשֶׂה שֶׁהָיָה כָּךְ הָיָה.
> **Translation:** It was further related: **Rav Yehuda permitted** one **to engage in intercourse** with his virgin wife for the **first** time **on a Festival. Rav Pappi said in the name of Rava: Do not** infer and **say:** It is **on a Festival that** it is **permitted,** but **on Shabbat** it is **prohibited,** similar to actions involving food preparation, which are permitted on Festivals and prohibited on Shabbat, **as the same is true that even on Shabbat it is permitted. And** the reason Rav Yehuda issued his ruling with regard to a Festival is due to the fact that **the incident that took place, took place in this way.**

The Gemara then records additional authorities who permitted *bi'ah rishonah* in similar circumstances. *Rav Zvid sharah l'meiva betechila b'Shabbos* — Rav Zvid likewise permitted it, and some report that he personally did so at his own wedding. *Rav Yehuda sharah l'meiva betechila b'Yom Tov* — Rav Yehuda permitted it on Yom Tov.

### Rav Pappi vs. Pappa

At this point the Gemara records a significant dispute between two authorities, each transmitting a ruling in the name of Rava. Rav Pappi in the name of Rava held: *lo teima b'Yom Tov desharei ha b'Shabbos assur* — do not suppose that Rava made a distinction between Yom Tov and Shabbat. Rather, *hu hadin d'afilu b'Shabbos nami sharei* — the same ruling applies even on Shabbat. The case simply happened to arise on Yom Tov. Rav Pappa in the name of Rava, however, held precisely the opposite: *b'Yom Tov sharei b'Shabbos assur* — Rava specifically permitted *bi'ah rishonah* on Yom Tov but not on Shabbat. The question is immediately apparent: whether the issue is *petach* or *chavala*, the physical concern is identical on Yom Tov and Shabbat. What could be the basis for a distinction?

### Mitoch & Shaveh

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב פָּפָּא מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא אָמַר: בְּיוֹם טוֹב שְׁרֵי, בְּשַׁבָּת אֲסִיר. אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב פַּפִּי לְרַב פָּפָּא: מַאי דַּעְתָּיךְ — מִתּוֹךְ שֶׁהוּתְּרָה חַבּוּרָה לְצוֹרֶךְ, הוּתְּרָה נָמֵי שֶׁלֹּא לְצוֹרֶךְ? אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה, מוּתָּר לַעֲשׂוֹת מוּגְמָר בְּיוֹם טוֹב, דְּמִתּוֹךְ שֶׁהוּתְּרָה הַבְעָרָה לְצוֹרֶךְ, הוּתְּרָה נָמֵי שֶׁלֹּא לְצוֹרֶךְ!
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa said in the name of Rava:** According to Rav Yehuda, **on a Festival** it is **permitted, on Shabbat** it is **prohibited. Rav Pappi said to Rav Pappa: What is your thinking?** Is it: **Since** causing **a wound was permitted** on a Festival when performed **for** the **purpose** of food preparation, it **was also permitted** when **not** performed **for** the **purpose** of food preparation? **If that is so,** it would be **permitted to prepare incense [*mugmar*] on a Festival** due to the following reason: **Since kindling** a fire **was permitted** on a Festival when performed **for** the **purpose** of food preparation, it **was also permitted** when **not** performed **for** the **purpose** of food preparation.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ, עָלֶיךָ אָמַר קְרָא: ״אַךְ אֲשֶׁר יֵאָכֵל לְכׇל נֶפֶשׁ״ — דָּבָר הַשָּׁוֶה לְכׇל נֶפֶשׁ.
> **Translation:** Rav Pappa **said to him:** It is **about your** assertion that **the verse states** with regard to a Festival: **“Save that which every person must eat,** that alone may be done by you” (Exodus 12:16), indicating **a matter that is equal for every person.** Incense is burned only by those who are particularly delicate. It is not equally utilized by everyone, and therefore it is not permitted. Intercourse, on the other hand, is universally practiced.


Rav Pappa explains to Rav Pappi: *mitoch shehutra chabura letzorech hutra nami shelo letzorech* — since wounding (*chavala*) is permitted on Yom Tov for a purpose, namely *shechitah* for food as taught at the beginning of Maseches Beitzah, it is therefore permitted even in a context that is not directly related to food preparation. The concern about *bi'ah rishonah*, on this view, is primarily a concern of *chavala* rather than *petach*, and on Yom Tov a specific leniency applies through the principle of *mitoch*.

Rav Pappa then challenges this reasoning: *aval ela me'ata mutar la'asos mugmar b'Yom Tov* — if *mitoch* works that broadly, one should similarly be permitted to burn incense on Yom Tov, since fire (*havara*) is permitted on Yom Tov for cooking, and by *mitoch shehutra havara letzorech hutra nami shelo letzorech*, burning for any purpose should follow. The implicit premise of the objection is that burning incense is in fact *assur* on Yom Tov. The answer given is: *alecha amar kra, ach asher ye'achel lechol nefesh* — the verse says "only that which shall be eaten by every soul," indicating that only something that is *davar hashaveh lechol nefesh* — universally beneficial, something that every person appreciates — may be performed on Yom Tov outside of direct food preparation. Burning incense benefits some people and repels others; it does not meet this threshold.

This Gemara is a central *sugya* for understanding the two distinct models of Yom Tov leniency. One model, the blanket *mitoch*, tends to apply to fire-making and carrying — two *melachot* that are somewhat conceptually marginal: carrying is described by the *Rishonim* as a *melacha geru'a* because it is not inherently creative, and fire, though the one *melacha* explicitly mentioned in the Torah (*lo teva'aru bechol moshvoteichem beyom haShabbat*), has an opinion among the *Tannaim* that one is not *chayav misa* for it on Shabbat. By contrast, other *melachot* require something analogous to the permitted act of eating — something *shaveh lechol nefesh*, a pleasure or need that is both universal and bodily. This Gemara insists on this second model. Under this model, the distinction between sex and burning incense is clear: sexual relations are a basic human need and pleasure, universal in scope, whereas burning incense is not.

### Sex vs. Incense

Tosafot elaborates on the *mitoch* principle, explaining: *perush l'tzorecho shel nefesh, ubilvad sheyihiyeh tzorech hana'at hayom o tzorech kiyum mitzva b'Yom Tov kigone hotza'at katan u'l'molo v'Sefer Torah likros bo* — it must serve some legitimate purpose, whether for personal enjoyment or for fulfilling a *mitzva* on Yom Tov, such as carrying a child to circumcise him or carrying a *Sefer Torah* for reading. Tosafot does not require *shaveh lechol nefesh* for the *mitoch* of carrying. This reinforces the point that the two models operate differently: carrying exemplifies the blanket *mitoch* model, which requires only some minimal *tzorech* rather than anything analogous to eating, while the present *sugya* — which introduces the language of *mitoch* but ultimately insists on *shaveh lechol nefesh* — represents a distinct and more demanding model that governs *melachot* other than carrying and fire-making.

It is worth pausing on why the blanket *mitoch* is limited to carrying and fire specifically. As noted above in connection with the *Shaveh* discussion, both are *melachot* that occupy a somewhat marginal conceptual position — a point that makes it all the more striking that *mitoch* applies to them in its unrestricted form, while *chavala*, a *melacha* that arises squarely in the food-preparation context of *shechitah*, still requires *shaveh lechol nefesh* to be extended beyond that context. The Gemara, in other words, is not applying a simple rule about which *melachot* are central or peripheral; it is insisting that outside of the blanket *mitoch* category, the analogy to eating must be substantive — the act must be something that every person benefits from in a bodily, immediate way.

The Gemara's resolution — that *bi'ah rishonah* on Yom Tov is permitted because *chavala* in that context is *shaveh lechol nefesh* while incense-burning is not — is itself worth examining. It is true that sexual relations in general are a universal human pleasure. One might raise the point that first-time intercourse, from the woman's perspective particularly, is not the pleasurable experience the category implies, and that the *chavala* component is not itself pleasurable at all. The Gemara, it must be said, is approaching this entirely from the man's perspective. Nevertheless, the principle being invoked is that within the context of a pleasure that is universally *shaveh lechol nefesh*, a *chavala* that is necessary to enable that pleasure is subsumed under the leniency.

### Deer Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרָבָא לְרַב אָשֵׁי: אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה, נִזְדַּמֵּן לוֹ צְבִי בְּיוֹם טוֹב, הוֹאִיל וְאֵינוֹ שָׁוֶה לְכׇל נֶפֶשׁ, הָכִי נָמֵי דַּאֲסִיר לְמִשְׁחֲטֵיהּ? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אֲנָא דְּבַר (הַ)צוֹרֶךְ לְכׇל נֶפֶשׁ קָאָמֵינָא, צְבִי צָרִיךְ לְכׇל נֶפֶשׁ הוּא.
> **Translation:** **Rav Aḥa, son of Rava, said to Rav Ashi: If that is so, if a deer happened to** come into **his** possession **on a Festival, since** it is **not** a food that is **equal for every person,** would the ruling **also** be **that** it is **prohibited to slaughter it?** Rav Ashi **said to him: I said a matter that** is **a need for every person,** and **deer** meat **is a matter that** although difficult to acquire, is **a need for every person.** Incense, even when available, is not universally utilized.

The Gemara then presents a further challenge: *nizdamen lo tzvi b'Yom Tov, ho'il v'eino shaveh lechol nefesh, hachi nami d'assur l'mishchatei* — would you say one is forbidden to slaughter a deer on Yom Tov because venison is a rare delicacy, not something universally enjoyed? The response is: *ana davar hatzarich lekhol nefesh ka'amina* — the rule of *shaveh lechol nefesh* applies to things that are *necessary* for all people, not merely enjoyed by all. The underlying logic seems to be that since deer is food, it falls under the general category of eating, and the leniency for food preparation on Yom Tov does not require that every individual enjoy that particular food. The *shaveh lechol nefesh* requirement operates to extend Yom Tov leniency to things that are *not* food-related, by analogy to food; once something is food, the ordinary rules of Yom Tov cooking apply regardless of the specific delicacy involved.

## R. Yochanan & Lore



### Hora'ah Halakha

The Gemara records: *hora Rabbi Yochanan b'Tzaidan, assur lavo betechila b'Shabbat* — Rabbi Yochanan ruled in Tzidon that *bi'ah rishonah* on Shabbat is forbidden. The Gemara immediately questions whether a ruling to forbid can properly be called a *hora'ah* at all. *Umi ika hora'ah le'issur* — is there such a thing as authoritative adjudication in the direction of prohibition?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יַעֲקֹב בַּר אִידִי, הוֹרָה רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בְּצַיְידָן: אָסוּר לִבְעוֹל בַּתְּחִלָּה בַּשַּׁבָּת. וּמִי אִיכָּא הוֹרָאָה לְאִיסּוּר?
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Ya’akov bar Idi said: Rabbi Yoḥanan issued a ruling in** the city of **Tzaidan: It is prohibited to engage in intercourse** with one’s virgin wife for the **first** time **on Shabbat.** The Gemara asks: **And is there a ruling issued to prohibit** an action? Typically, that language is utilized in reference to a firmly established ruling. A stringent ruling can be issued even based on uncertainty. In contrast, a lenient ruling can be issued only if the matter is clearly established by means of tradition or the reasoning of the Sage issuing the ruling.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִין, וְהָתְנַן: הוֹרוּהָ בֵּית הִלֵּל שֶׁתְּהֵא נְזִירָה עוֹד שֶׁבַע שָׁנִים אֲחֵרוֹת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Yes,** that language is used with regard to a stringent ruling, as **didn’t we learn** the following in a mishna (*Nazir* 19b): When Queen Helene’s son went to war, she took a vow to be a nazirite for seven years, and she fulfilled that vow for the duration of her stay in the Diaspora? When she immigrated to Eretz Yisrael, **Beit Hillel issued a ruling that she shall be a nazirite for seven additional years.** Apparently, rulings are issued to prohibit an action as well.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי נָמֵי, כִּי הָא דְּתַנְיָא: חוּט הַשִּׁדְרָה שֶׁנִּפְסַק בְּרוּבּוֹ, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי. רַבִּי יַעֲקֹב אוֹמֵר: אֲפִילּוּ נִיקַּב. הוֹרָה רַבִּי כְּרַבִּי יַעֲקֹב. אָמַר רַב הוּנָא: אֵין הֲלָכָה כְּרַבִּי יַעֲקֹב.
> **Translation:** **And alternatively,** this is **similar** to **that which is taught** in a *baraita*: **If a majority of the spinal cord** of an animal **is severed,** the animal is a *tereifa*; **this is the statement of Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi. **Rabbi Ya’akov says: Even** if the spinal cord is **perforated** but otherwise intact, the animal is a *tereifa*. The *baraita* continues: **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **issued a ruling in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Ya’akov.** Here too, the term: Issue a ruling, is employed with regard to a stringent ruling. **Rav Huna said:** Despite the fact that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi ruled in accordance with his opinion, **the *halakha* is not in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Ya’akov,** but rather it is in accordance with the opinion of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi. This is one version of this discussion.

### Matir Authority

Rashi's explanation is illuminating. He writes: *mi kari issur hora'ah mishum dechol adam matzi lehachmir, she'afilu min hasafek she'ein lo barur lo hu ba ve'oser* — calling something forbidden does not demonstrate rabbinic authority because anyone can be strict, and even one who is uncertain about the law can simply declare something forbidden without any real knowledge or tradition behind the ruling. *Aval hamatir somech al shmu'ato o sevara chochmato vehi hora'ah* — but one who rules permissively must be relying either on a received tradition or on the strength of his own scholarly judgment, and that is what constitutes genuine *hora'ah*. The ability to say *mutar* is the true mark of judicial authority.

This principle is illustrated by a famous anecdote involving the Noda BiYehuda. A *shochet* from a neighboring town would make the long journey to ask his *treifa* questions, but eventually stopped coming. When he resumed, he explained that he had found a simpler method: upon reading the verse *uvasar basadeh treifa lo socheilu lakelev tashlichun oto*, he realized he could simply throw questionable meat to the dog — if the dog ate it, it was *treifa*; if not, it was kosher. The Noda BiYehuda asked why he had then returned. The *shochet* replied: "The dog was too much of a *machmir*." The point is well taken: being strict requires no expertise.

### Rebbe Examples


The Gemara offers two examples of *hora'ah le'issur* to complicate this principle. First: *horu Beis Hillel shetei nezirut sheva shanim acherot* — Beis Hillel ruled that Heleni HaMalka, having fulfilled her vow of *nezirut* in *chutz l'aretz*, had to repeat the full seven years upon entering Eretz Yisrael. While this ruling is strict, it is a genuine *chiddush* — the ruling that her vow did not count requires real knowledge. Second: *chut hashidra shenifsak berubo, divrei Rabbi; Rabbi Yaakov omer afilu nikev; hora Rebbe ke-Rabbi Yaakov* — Rebbe first ruled that the spinal cord must be severed in its majority to render an animal a *treifa*, then reversed himself and ruled, like Rabbi Yaakov, that even perforation suffices. The latter case is more directly relevant because Rebbe's reversal could be understood as mere stringency rather than authoritative ruling. The Gemara accepts that such language can be used even for a strict ruling. *Amar Rav Huna: ein halacha ke-Rabbi Yaakov* — and in fact we do not rule like Rabbi Yaakov; the cord must be severed in its majority.

### R. Yochanan Forbidden

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק מַתְנֵי הָכִי: אָמַר רַבִּי אֲבָהוּ, שָׁאַל רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל בֶּן יַעֲקֹב דְּמִן צוּר אֶת רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בְּצַיְידָן, וַאֲנָא שְׁמַעִי: מַהוּ לִבְעוֹל בַּתְּחִלָּה בַּשַּׁבָּת, וַאֲמַר לֵיהּ: אָסוּר. וְהִלְכְתָא: מוּתָּר לִבְעוֹל בַּתְּחִלָּה בַּשַּׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak taught this** alternative version of the ruling of Rabbi Yoḥanan. **Rav Abbahu said: Rabbi Yishmael ben Ya’akov, who is from Tyre, asked Rabbi Yoḥanan in Tzaidan, and I heard** the exchange: **What is** the *halakha* with regard **to engaging in intercourse** with one’s virgin wife for the **first** time **on Shabbat? And he said to** him: It is **prohibited.** The Gemara concludes: **And the *halakha* is** that it is **permitted to engage in intercourse** with one’s virgin wife for the **first** time **on Shabbat,** and one need not be concerned lest he cause a wound, create an opening, or initiate bleeding.

The Gemara then presents the full account. Rabbi Abbahu reports: *sha'al Rabbi Yishmael ben Yaakov d'min Tzur et Rabbi Yochanan b'Tzaidan, ve-ana shemi'ah* — Rabbi Yishmael the son of Yaakov from Tzur asked Rabbi Yochanan in Tzidon whether *bi'ah rishonah* on Shabbat is permitted, and Rabbi Abbahu overheard the response. *Ve-amar lei assur* — Rabbi Yochanan ruled it forbidden. Nevertheless: *ve-hilchata b'tzlachot mutar liv'ol bitchilah b'Shabbat* — the final *halakha* is that it is permitted.

It is worth noting, as Tosafot observes, that Rabbi Yochanan is an *Amora* of Eretz Yisrael, while most of the *Amoraim* who ruled permissively throughout this *sugya* appear to be Babylonian. The *hilcheta* here follows the Babylonian *Amoraim*. Tosafot at the end of this passage adds: *ve-hilchata mutar liv'ol bitchilah b'Shabbat, v'lo chayshinan shema yishchot ben of* — we do not also worry that the groom will come to perform *shechitah* for the wedding meal and accidentally violate another *melacha* in the process. *V'aha samchinan l'me'evad seuda b'Shabbat* — and it is on this basis that the Ashkenazic custom developed to marry on Friday afternoon and hold the wedding *seuda* on Shabbat, a practice that also had the practical benefit of allowing the costs of Shabbat hospitality to serve double duty as the wedding meal.

## Sheva Brachot



### Rav Huna Teaching

The *sugya* now transitions from *bi'ah rishonah* to the laws of *birchat chatanim* — the wedding blessings. *Amar Rabbi Chelbo amar Rav Huna amar Rabbi Abba bar Zavda amar Rav: achas betulah v'achas almanah, te'unah bracha* — whether the bride is a *betulah* or an *almanah* (meaning a woman marrying for the second time), a *bracha* is required. The term *birchat chatanim* is the language the Gemara uses throughout; it does not use the phrase *sheva brachot*, in part because the Geonic period later added the *borei pri hagefen* to bring the count to seven, and the Gemara's set consists of six blessings. Notably, while *ein birchat chatanim pachot m'asarah* — the *Mishnah* in Megillah establishes that *birchat chatanim* requires a *minyan* of ten — there is no real sustained treatment of *birchat chatanim* in the *mishnayot* of *Masechet Ketubot* itself; it is the *Gemara* here that introduces and develops the laws of these blessings, including the question of the duration for which they are recited.

The teaching uses the striking formulation *te'unah bracha* — she requires a *bracha*, literally, the *bracha* is incumbent upon her. This language, centering the woman as the subject to whom the blessing attaches, will become conceptually significant as the discussion develops.

### Duration Rules

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חֶלְבּוֹ אָמַר רַב הוּנָא אָמַר רַבִּי אַבָּא בַּר זַבְדָּא אָמַר רַב: אַחַת בְּתוּלָה וְאַחַת אַלְמָנָה טְעוּנָה בְּרָכָה. וּמִי אָמַר רַב הוּנָא הָכִי? וְהָאָמַר רַב הוּנָא: אַלְמָנָה אֵינָהּ טְעוּנָה בְּרָכָה! לָא קַשְׁיָא: כָּאן בְּבָחוּר שֶׁנָּשָׂא אַלְמָנָה, כָּאן בְּאַלְמוֹן שֶׁנָּשָׂא אַלְמָנָה.
> **Translation:** § **Rabbi Ḥelbo said** that **Rav Huna said** that **Rabbi Abba bar Rav Zavda said** that **Rav said: Both a virgin and a widow** who marry **require** that the **benediction** of the grooms be recited. The Gemara asks: **Did Rav Huna say that? But didn’t Rav Huna say: A widow does not require** that **a benediction** be recited? The Gemara answers: This is **not difficult. Here,** where Rav Huna said that a widow requires a benediction, it is **with regard to a bachelor who married a widow. There,** where Rav Huna said she does not require a benediction, it is **with regard to a widower who married a widow.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַלְמוֹן שֶׁנָּשָׂא אַלְמָנָה לָא? וְהָאָמַר רַב נַחְמָן, אָמַר לִי הוּנָא בַּר נָתָן, תָּנָא: מִנַּיִן לְבִרְכַּת חֲתָנִים בַּעֲשָׂרָה — שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּקַּח עֲשָׂרָה אֲנָשִׁים מִזִּקְנֵי הָעִיר וַיֹּאמֶר שְׁבוּ פֹה וַיֵּשֵׁבוּ״. וּבוֹעַז אַלְמוֹן שֶׁנָּשָׂא אַלְמָנָה הֲוָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And does a widower who married a widow not** require a benediction to be recited? **But didn’t Rav Naḥman say: Huna bar Natan said to me** that it was **taught: From where** is it derived **that the benediction of the grooms** is recited **in** a quorum of **ten** men? It **is as it is stated** with regard to Boaz, who married Ruth: **“And he took ten men of the Elders of the city and said: Sit you here, and they sat”** (Ruth 4:2). **And** when **Boaz** married Ruth, **he was a widower marrying a widow.** As that is the primary source for the obligation to recite the benediction, apparently the benediction is recited even in that case.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״אֵינָהּ טְעוּנָה בְּרָכָה״ דְּאָמַר רַב הוּנָא — אֵינָהּ טְעוּנָה בְּרָכָה כׇּל שִׁבְעָה, אֲבָל יוֹם אֶחָד טְעוּנָה בְּרָכָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **What is** the meaning of: **Does not require a benediction, that Rav Huna stated?** It means that **she does not require a benediction all seven** days of the wedding celebration, **but** everyone agrees that for **one day, she requires** that **a benediction** be recited.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא הָא דְּתַנְיָא: שָׁקְדוּ חֲכָמִים עַל תַּקָּנַת בְּנוֹת יִשְׂרָאֵל שֶׁיְּהֵא שָׂמֵחַ עִמָּהּ שְׁלֹשָׁה יָמִים. בְּמַאי: אִי בְּבָחוּר, הָאָמְרַתְּ שִׁבְעָה! אִי בְּאַלְמוֹן, הָאָמְרַתְּ יוֹם אֶחָד?
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **However, that which is taught** in a *baraita*, that **the Sages were assiduous in seeing to the well-being of Jewish women,** ensuring **that** the groom **will rejoice with her three days,** and that is why they established that a widow is married on Thursday, **with regard to what** circumstance is the *baraita* speaking? **If** it is **with regard to a bachelor** who marries a widow, **didn’t you say** he celebrates **seven** days; why then did the Sages see to a mere three-day celebration? **If** it is **with regard to a widower** who marries a widow, **didn’t you say** he celebrates for **one** day? Why then did the Sages see to a three-day celebration?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: בְּאַלְמוֹן — יוֹם אֶחָד לִבְרָכָה, וּשְׁלֹשָׁה לְשִׂמְחָה. וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: בְּבָחוּר — שִׁבְעָה לִבְרָכָה, וּשְׁלֹשָׁה לְשִׂמְחָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers that this can be resolved in several manners. **If you wish, say: In** the case of **a widower** marrying a widow, there is **one day for benediction and three days for celebration.** The wedding is scheduled on Thursday to facilitate a three-day celebration. **And if you wish, say** instead: **In** the case of **a bachelor** marrying a widow, there are **seven days for benediction and** there are **three days for celebration,** during which he must refrain from going to work.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵיתִיבִי: מְבָרְכִין לִבְתוּלָה שִׁבְעָה וּלְאַלְמָנָה יוֹם אֶחָד. מַאי לָאו, אֲפִילּוּ אַלְמָנָה שֶׁנִּשֵּׂאת לְבָחוּר! לֹא, לְאַלְמוֹן. אֲבָל לְבָחוּר מַאי, שִׁבְעָה? אִי הָכִי לִיתְנֵי: מְבָרְכִין לִבְתוּלָה שִׁבְעָה, וּלְאַלְמָנָה שֶׁנִּשֵּׂאת לְבָחוּר שִׁבְעָה, וּלְאַלְמָנָה יוֹם אֶחָד?
> **Translation:** **The Gemara raises an objection** from a *baraita*: **One recites a benediction for a virgin** who marries for **seven** days **and for a widow** who marries for **one** day. **What, is it not even** in the case of **a widow who is married to a bachelor,** that one recites the benediction for one day? The Gemara answers: **No,** it is only in the case of a widow who is married **to a widower** that the benediction is recited for one day. The Gemara asks: **However,** one may then infer that in the case of a widow who is married **to a bachelor, what** is the *halakha*? The blessing is recited **seven** days? **If so, let** the *tanna* **teach** the *baraita*: **One recites a benediction for a virgin** who marries for **seven** days, **and for a widow** who marries **a bachelor seven** days, **and for a widow** marrying a widower for **one day.** Why was the middle case omitted?

The Gemara challenges Rav Huna's ruling by citing a contradictory statement attributed to him: *almanah einah te'unah bracha* — for a widow, no *bracha* is required. The Gemara resolves this with the distinction: *kan b'bachur shenasa almanah, kan b'alman shenasa almanah* — in one case a bachelor married a widow, in the other a widower married a widow. The principle that emerges is that if for either member of the couple this is a first marriage, the full seven days of *sheva brachot* are observed; if for both it is a second marriage, only one day is required.

It is also important to note the conceptual distinction between the *brachot* recited under the *chuppah* and the *brachot* recited at the meals during the week. The *Rambam* rules that if the *sheva brachot* were not recited at the time of *nisuin*, they may be recited many days later — but one cannot make up for *sheva brachot* missed at the meal by reciting them at a different time within the week. The *brachot* under the *chuppah* are attached to the act of *nisuin* itself, while the meal *brachot* belong to the ongoing celebration.

The Gemara further addresses a *braita* that teaches: *she'kedu chachamim al takanas benos Yisrael she'yesame'ach imah gimel yamim* — the Sages enacted that a husband should rejoice with his wife for three days, which was achieved by the practice of marrying an *almanah* on Thursday, yielding Thursday, Friday, and Shabbat together. The Gemara asks: if it is a first marriage for him, do you not say seven days of *sheva brachot*? And if it is a second marriage for him, do you not say only one day? What then is the meaning of "three days"? The Gemara offers two resolutions: either it is a case of a second marriage for both, where the *bracha* requirement is one day but *simcha* continues for three; or it is a first marriage for him, where *brachot* extend for seven days but the three days refers to a separate rabbinic expectation of sustained *simcha* and companionship.

### Gemara Resolution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִילְּתָא פְּסִיקְתָּא קָתָנֵי, דְּלֵיכָּא בְּתוּלָה דְּבָצְרָה מִשִּׁבְעָה, וְלֵיכָּא אַלְמָנָה דְּבָצְרָה מִיּוֹם אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** Although the *tanna* could have included that case in the *baraita*, **he taught categorical matters.** He preferred to avoid entering into detail, **as there is no virgin** for **whom** the benediction is recited **fewer than seven** days, **and there is no widow** for **whom** the benediction is recited for **less than one day.** However, there are circumstances where even for a widow the benediction is recited for more than one day.

The Gemara then raises a further challenge from a *braita*: *mevarchin b'betulah sheva ule'almanah yom echad* — we bless for seven days for a *betulah* and one day for an *almanah*. The *braita* makes no mention of the man's status. Does this not imply that the distinction is entirely based on the woman's status — a widow who marries a bachelor would still receive only one day?

The Gemara rejects this: *lo, kan b'alman shenasa almanah* — the *braita* refers only to the case where the man is also a widower. If a bachelor marries a widow, the ruling would be seven days. The *braita*'s formulation is *milta psikta katani* — it states things in absolute terms: a *betulah* never has fewer than seven days, and an *almanah* never has fewer than one day, but sometimes an *almanah* also receives seven. The Gemara's answer is admittedly not entirely satisfying, as the *braita* does seem to ground the distinction entirely in the woman's status. And it is worth pausing to note that what the Gemara has done here is actually quite radical. The Gemara arrived at this reframing through the need to reconcile contradictory teachings, not from any programmatic agenda, but the effect is significant: the entire framing of the *braita* — like the language of *te'unah bracha* throughout — orients around the woman's identity as a *betulah* or *almanah*, treating her status as the sole determinant of what celebration she is owed. The Gemara, by introducing the man's prior marital history as the operative variable, fundamentally shifts the basis of the law — a shift whose conceptual implications will be worth examining directly.

### Status Shift

This observation leads to an important conceptual observation. The original framing of these laws — much like the *ketuva* system itself — orients entirely around the woman's status: is she a *betulah* or not, is her *ketuva* 200 *zuz* or 100 *zuz*? In that framework, the degree of communal celebration is entirely a function of what type of woman is being married. The Gemara's reframing, by introducing the man's prior marital status as the determining variable, represents a significant shift: the duration of *sheva brachot* now reflects the *simcha* of the couple as a couple — the degree to which this is a new beginning for either of them — rather than the personal status of the bride alone.

This distinction carries conceptual weight. The *sheva brachot* are a communal celebration, which is why a *minyan* is required. The extent to which the community continues to celebrate corresponds to the degree of novelty and joy for both parties. If for both it is a second marriage, the communal celebration is more abbreviated. If for either it is a first, the full week of celebration is warranted. The three days of *simcha* in the *braita*, by contrast, belong to the couple themselves — a period of time spent together at the outset of their shared life, regardless of prior marital history.

## Minyan Requirement



### Minyan Sources

Having established that *birchat chatanim* requires a *minyan*, the Gemara now examines the sources for this requirement. The *Mishnah* in Megillah states it as a rule alongside other rabbinic acts requiring ten men, but what is the textual or exegetical basis?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוּפָא, אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן: אָמַר לִי הוּנָא בַּר נָתָן, תָּנָא: מִנַּיִן לְבִרְכַּת חֲתָנִים בַּעֲשָׂרָה — שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּקַּח עֲשָׂרָה אֲנָשִׁים מִזִּקְנֵי הָעִיר וַיֹּאמֶר שְׁבוּ פֹה״. וְרַבִּי אֲבָהוּ אָמַר, מֵהָכָא: ״בְּמַקְהֵלוֹת בָּרְכוּ אֱלֹהִים ה׳ מִמְּקוֹר יִשְׂרָאֵל״.
> **Translation:** § Apropos the source for the benediction of the grooms, the Gemara discusses **the** matter **itself. Rav Naḥman said: Huna bar Natan said to me** that it was **taught: From where** is it derived **that the benediction of the grooms** is recited **in** a quorum of **ten** men? It is **as it is stated: “And he took ten men of the Elders of the city and said: Sit you here,** and they sat” (Ruth 4:2). **And Rabbi Abbahu said** that the source is **from here: “In assemblies [*mak’helot*], bless God, the Lord, from the source of Israel”** (Psalms 68:27). This verse indicates that a congregation [*kahal*], which contains at least ten men, blesses God when reciting a benediction related to the source of Israel, i.e., conjugal relations, which will lead to the birth of Jewish children.

### Ruth 4 Source

*Amar Rav Nachman amar lei Huna bar Nasan: tana, minayin l'virchat chatanim b'asarah? Shene'emar, vayikach asarah anashim mizeknei ha'ir vayomer shevu po vayeisheivu* — Rav Nachman, citing a tradition from Huna bar Nasan, grounds the requirement in Ruth 4:2, where Boaz took ten elders of the city and said, "Sit here," and they sat. The blessings given to Boaz and Ruth by the assembled elders and people at the gate serve as the prototype for *birchat chatanim*.

### Makor Imagery


Rabbi Abbahu derives the *minyan* requirement from a different verse: *Bemakhelos barchu Elokim Hashem mimakor Yisrael* — "In the assemblies, bless God, the Lord, from the *makor* of Israel" (Psalms 68:27). *Makor* here points to matters connected with marriage and reproduction, and *bemakhelos* implies a gathering — hence a *minyan* of ten. One might initially read *makor* simply as "source" or "wellspring" — something like the womb, the source of life — and on that reading the verse would connect the praise of God to the realm of fertility and family building, the whole idea of *boneh bayis ne'eman*, building an enduring household. The implication for *birchat chatanim* would then be that the *kehila*, the assembly, gathers to bless a union oriented toward that future. But as the Gemara will go on to clarify, the precise meaning of *makor* turns out to be more specific and more surprising than this initial reading suggests.

### Makor Meaning

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַב נַחְמָן, בְּהַאי קְרָא דְּרַבִּי אֲבָהוּ מַאי דָּרֵישׁ בֵּיהּ? מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ לְכִדְתַנְיָא, הָיָה רַבִּי מֵאִיר אוֹמֵר: מִנַּיִן שֶׁאֲפִילּוּ עוּבָּרִים שֶׁבִּמְעֵי אִמָּן אָמְרוּ שִׁירָה עַל הַיָּם — שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״בְּמַקְהֵלוֹת בָּרְכוּ אֱלֹהִים ה׳ מִמְּקוֹר יִשְׂרָאֵל״. וְאִידַּךְ? אִם כֵּן, לֵימָא קְרָא ״מִבֶּטֶן״. מַאי ״מִמְּקוֹר״ — עַל עִסְקֵי מְקוֹר.
> **Translation:** **And what does Rav Naḥman derive from this verse** from **which Rabbi Abbahu** derived that *halakha*? **He requires** the verse **to** derive **that which is taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi Meir would say: From where** is it derived **that even fetuses in their mother’s womb recited** the **song at the** Red **Sea?** It is **as it is stated** in the chapter of Psalms that describes the exodus from Egypt: **“In assemblies, bless God, the Lord, from the source of Israel.”** Even those fetuses that were still in the source, i.e., the womb, joined the assemblies in blessing God. **And the other** Sage, Rabbi Abbahu says: **If that** is the meaning, **let the verse say: From the belly of Israel. What is** the meaning of the term **“source”?** Clearly, it is referring **to matters** related to **the source** of Israel, i.e., the benediction of the grooms, which must be recited in a congregation, a quorum of ten.

The Gemara asks what each authority does with the verse cited by the other. Rav Nachman uses the verse from Psalms for a different *drasha* altogether, as cited in a *braita*: *haya Rabbi Meir omer minayin she'afilu ubarim she-bimei imanim amru shira al ha-yam* — Rabbi Meir asks how we know that even fetuses in their mothers' wombs burst forth in *shira* at the crossing of the *Yam Suf*. The answer is from this very verse: *Hashem mimakor Yisrael* — praise arose from the *makor*, the womb, meaning from within the bodies of the Israelite women. On this reading, *makor* means womb, the source of fetal life.

Rabbi Abbahu responds: *im ken limra kra mibeten* — if the verse meant only to indicate the womb, it should have used the word *beten*. The use of *makor* instead points to something more specific: *al iskei makor* — related to matters of the *makor* itself. And *makor*, as is clear from the verse *es mekora hera* used in the context of marital law, refers not to the womb but to the genitals themselves.

This is a striking and theologically significant point. If *birchat chatanim* finds its scriptural anchor in the verse *Hashem mimakor Yisrael*, and *makor* there refers to the genitals rather than the womb, then the blessings are oriented toward the sexual union of husband and wife rather than toward fertility and children. Indeed, a close reading of the text of the *sheva brachot* bears this out: there is no explicit reference to children in the blessings themselves. The *brachot* speak of the creation of *Adam* and *Chava*, of the joining of *chatan* and *kallah*, of the joy each finds with the other — but not explicitly of procreation. The implicit theme of *birchat chatanim*, understood in light of this *drasha*, is the sanctification of the sexual union itself, set in the context of creation and covenant. The *bracha* of *birchat eirusin* makes this even more explicit: *assar lanu es ha-arusos ve-hittir lanu es ha-nessuos* — He forbade for us the *arusos* and permitted for us the *nessuos* — the blessing is directly about the permissibility of marital sex.

### Pirsum Requirement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי אֲבָהוּ, בְּהַאי קְרָא דְּרַב נַחְמָן מַאי דָּרֵישׁ בֵּיהּ? הָהוּא מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ לְמִידְרַשׁ: עַמּוֹנִי וְלֹא עַמּוֹנִית, מוֹאָבִי וְלֹא מוֹאָבִית. דְּאִי סָלְקָא דַעְתָּךְ לִבְרָכָה, לָא סַגִּיא דְּלָאו זְקֵנִים.
> **Translation:** **And what does Rabbi Abbahu derive from this verse** from **which Rav Naḥman** derived his *halakha*? **He requires** the verse stating that Boaz assembled ten men in order **to teach** that the Torah prohibition with regard to marrying members of the nations of Ammon and Moab is limited to a male **Ammonite and not a female Ammonite,** and to a male **Moabite and not a female Moabite, as, if it would enter your mind** that Boaz gathered the men only **to** recite **a benediction,** would it **not** have been **sufficient** if they **were not Elders?** From the fact that he convened a quorum of Elders, apparently it was to engage in halakhic discourse and to issue a halakhic ruling.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִידַּךְ: אִי סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ לְמִידְרַשׁ, לָא סַגִּיא דְּלָאו עֲשָׂרָה! אִין — לְפַרְסוֹמֵי מִילְּתָא, וּכְדַאֲמַר לֵיהּ שְׁמוּאֵל לְרַב חָנָא בַּגְדָּתָאָה: פּוֹק וְאַיְיתִי לִי בֵּי עַשְׂרָה, וְאֵימָא לָךְ בְּאַנְפַּיְיהוּ: הַמְזַכֶּה לְעוּבָּר קָנָה. וְהִלְכְתָא — הַמְזַכֶּה לְעוּבָּר לֹא קָנָה.
> **Translation:** **And the other** Sage, Rav Naḥman, would reject that proof. **If it would enter your mind** that he gathered the men **in order to teach** a *halakha*, would it **not** have been **sufficient** if they **were not ten?** The Gemara answers: **Yes,** in fact a quorum of ten is not necessary to issue a halakhic ruling. Nevertheless, Boaz convened ten Elders **to publicize the matter, as Shmuel said to Rav Ḥana of Baghdad: Go and bring me an assembly of ten** men **and I will say to you before them** a *halakha* that I seek to disseminate: With regard to **one who transfers ownership** of an object **to a fetus,** the fetus **acquires** it, although it has not yet entered the world. Boaz too assembled ten Elders to publicize the matter. Apropos the *halakha* that Shmuel publicized, the Gemara rules: **And the *halakha*** is: With regard to **one who transfers ownership** of an object **to a fetus,** the fetus **does not acquire** it.

The Gemara then returns to the question of what Rav Nachman does with the verse in Ruth 4. On the surface, the ten elders there were needed to formalize the legal transaction — the redemption and the *chalitzah*-like proceeding — not to recite *brachot*. Rabbi Abbahu therefore says that what Rav Nachman needs the verse for is a different *drasha* entirely: *lemidrash amoni velo amonit moavi velo moavit* — to publicize the ruling that the prohibition on Moabites and Ammonites applies only to males, not females, and this is why Boaz needed elders present before he could marry Ruth.

The Gemara then asks: if the ten were needed for the *drasha*, why specifically elders rather than any ten people? And if needed for the *bracha*, why elders rather than any *minyan*? The Gemara's answer is: *lefirsumei milta* — to publicize the matter. The *zekeinim* serve as a *beis din* for the legal ruling; the ten are needed for *pirsum*, public announcement. This is illustrated by the story of Shmuel who told Rav Chanan: *puk veaisi li bei asarah, ve-ima lach b'anpaihu: hamizakeh le'ubar kanah* — go gather ten people, and I will declare in their presence that a transfer of property to a fetus is legally effective. The need for ten here is specifically to publicize an innovative ruling. (The Gemara adds parenthetically: *ve-hilchata hamizakeh le'ubar lo kanah* — the final ruling does not, in fact, follow this view.)

## Brachot Location



### Chatanim Brachot

A *braita* teaches: *mevarchin birchat chatanim beveis chatanim* — the *birchat chatanim* is recited in the house of the *chatanim*, meaning at the wedding venue or in the context of the *nisuin* itself.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מְבָרְכִין בִּרְכַּת חֲתָנִים בְּבֵית חֲתָנִים. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: אַף בְּבֵית הָאֵרוּסִין מְבָרְכִין אוֹתָהּ.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: **One recites the benediction of the grooms in the house of the grooms,** when the bride enters into the wedding canopy. **Rabbi Yehuda said: One recites it even in the house of the betrothal,** at the time of the betrothal.

### R. Yehuda Eirusin


Rabbi Yehuda, however, says: *af beveis ha'irusin mevarchin osah* — one also recites the blessings at the *eirusin*, the betrothal. This is not merely a question of physical location but of timing: Rabbi Yehuda holds that *birchat chatanim* — what we would understand as the blessings associated with *nisuin* — is recited not only at the time of *nisuin* but already at *kidushin*, which could precede the *nisuin* by months or years.

### Abaye Explanation

Abaye explains: *uviYehuda shanu, mipnei shemisyached imah* — this applies specifically in Yehuda, where the practice was that after *kidushin*, even before *nisuin*, the couple would spend time alone together in the bride's father's house in order to grow comfortable with one another. Since seclusion could lead to sexual relations — not as an explicit intention but as a natural result — special rules applied there.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: וּבִיהוּדָה שָׁנוּ, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמִּתְיַיחֵד עִמָּהּ.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said: And** the Sages **taught** the statement of Rabbi Yehuda **in Judea because** there the custom was **that** the groom **be secluded with** his betrothed, leading to the concern lest he engage in conjugal relations with her. Therefore, the blessing is recited already at that stage.

### Rashi: Kallah Forbidden


Rashi explains the necessity for *birchat chatanim* at this stage as follows: *kallah belo beracha asurah le-ba'alah keniddah* — a bride without having had *birchat chatanim* is forbidden to her husband like a *niddah*. On this view, the *sheva brachot* function as a *matir* — they are what renders marital sex permissible. Without them, the sexual act is forbidden. Therefore, if the couple in Yehuda might have sexual relations during the period of *eirusin*, the blessings must be recited beforehand to avoid a forbidden act.

### Tosafot Nuance

Tosafot's explanation differs somewhat. Tosafot writes: *u-lefi she-pe'amim ba aleha shelo leshem chuppah, osim beracha kedei shetehei kallah be-beracha* — since on occasion the husband would come to her not in the context of the formal *chuppah*, they recite the *bracha* so that the *kallah* should have the *bracha* in place. Tosafot does not go so far as Rashi in saying she is forbidden like a *niddah* without it; the concern is simply that there should be a *bracha* before any sexual act occurs. Other *rishonim* take yet a different angle, suggesting that perhaps such an encounter might itself be elevated into a *chuppah*, so the *bracha* is recited in anticipation of that possibility.

What makes this striking is what it implies about the *sheva brachot* as a whole. All the rich language of communal joy — *mesame'ach chatan ve-kallah*, *od yishama be-arei Yehuda* and so on — would, on this reading, be recited years before the couple actually begins living together, and the immediate operative reason for doing so would be simply to permit them to have sex. That is, to put it plainly, a somewhat surprising result. The deeper question of what this reveals about the nature of *birchat chatanim* — whether it functions primarily as a *matir* for the physical act or as a celebration of the couple's shared life — is taken up in the next section.

### Sex vs. Joy

This discussion raises a deeper conceptual question about the nature of *sheva brachot*. The blessings speak of *simcha*, of *chatan* and *kallah* rejoicing together, of *menucha* and *bayit ne'eman* — the language is that of marital joy and communal celebration. Yet here, the Gemara seems to root the necessity of the blessings specifically in the potential for sexual relations. The language of *te'unah bracha* — she requires a *bracha* — and the comparison to *niddah* point toward an understanding of *birchat chatanim* as a *matir*, a ritual permission-granting act, rather than simply a communal celebration. A parallel might be drawn to *birchat hanehnin* — blessings recited before deriving physical pleasure — where the act of blessing frames and sanctifies a physical experience rather than merely marking a social occasion. Whether *birchat chatanim* functions primarily as such a *matir* for marital sex, or primarily as a celebration of the couple's new life together, is a question the Gemara gestures toward without fully resolving.

### Two Brachot

A final *braita* clarifies: *mevarchin birchat chatanim b'veis chatanim u'virchat eirusin b'veis ha'eirusin* — the *birchat chatanim* is recited at the *nisuin*, and a separate *birchat eirusin* is recited at the *eirusin*. This is striking, because while *birchat chatanim* appears in the *Mishnah* in Megillah and has clear Tannaitic grounding, the idea of a distinct *birchat eirusin* has no explicit earlier source. The *Gemara* will open tomorrow's discussion with precisely this question — what is the *birchat eirusin*, and where does it come from?
