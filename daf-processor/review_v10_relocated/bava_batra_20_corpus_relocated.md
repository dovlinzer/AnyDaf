## Maysivay to Shmuel



### Shmuel's Wafer Principle

We pick up four lines from the top of 20a with a *maysivay* challenge to a ruling attributed to Shmuel. On the previous *daf*, Rav Tuvi bar Kisna reported in the name of Shmuel that a wafer placed in a window does not reduce the size of that window. The *halachic* context is the laws of *ohel* and *tumah*: when a corpse lies in one room, *tumah* passes into an adjacent room only if the connecting window is at least a *tefach* in height and width. If the window measures exactly a *tefach* but a wafer sitting in it would reduce the effective opening to less than a *tefach*, one might think the window is now too small to transmit *tumah*. Shmuel's ruling is that the wafer does not count toward reducing the window's size, because the wafer is intended to be eaten and will be removed. The principle underlying Shmuel's position is that only something one genuinely intends to leave in place permanently can be considered part of the wall structure; an object one plans to remove does not diminish the window's legal dimensions.

Tosafot notes that this discussion is brought here because it will connect to the broader conversation about vegetation near walls — whether certain growth benefits or damages a wall — which ties into our *mishnah*'s topic of required distancing from walls.

### Tosefta Objects List

The *maysivay* challenge to Shmuel comes from a *Tosefta* in *Ohalos*. The *Tosefta* lists a series of objects that, if placed in a window, *do* diminish its size — meaning they are treated as part of the wall for purposes of measuring the *tefach* threshold. These are:

Grass (*asavim*) that was uprooted and placed in the window, or grass that grew on its own through gaps in the stones. Small rags (*metlanyot*) that lack three by three fingerbreadths in area — it is important to note that the measurement here is *shalosh al shalosh etzbaot*, fingerbreadths, not *tefachim*; we will encounter a four by four *tefachim* standard shortly and the grammatical distinction is critical. A rag smaller than three by three fingerbreadths is not *mekabel tumah*, susceptible to ritual impurity, and that is precisely the qualification at work throughout this list. A hanging limb or flesh (*ha'eiver vehabasar hamedulalin bivheimah uvchayah*) — a limb that has been partially torn from an animal but is still partially attached. A live bird resting in the window (*of sheshachan bechalon*). A non-Jew seated in the window: though a non-Jew is rabbinically a source of *tumah*, he is not *mekabel tumah* on a Torah level. A baby born in the eighth month (*ben shmonah*): the *Gemara* held that such an infant would not survive, and so it has the status of a stone — while still alive it is not classified as a person who is *mekabel tumah*. Salt (*melach*), since salt is not a food in itself and therefore not *mekabel tumah*. A pottery vessel (*kli cheres*) — ordinarily pottery is *mekabel tumah*, but only through its opening, so the case here requires that the opening faces away from the room containing the corpse. And a *Sefer Torah*, which is striking: a *Sefer Torah* is not *mekabel tumah* simply because it is a book, and a book does not meet any of the criteria of a *kli* that renders an object susceptible to impurity.

The *Tosefta* concludes: *kulam mematin bechalon* — all of these diminish the size of the window. Because none of them is *mekabel tumah*, they are treated as part of the wall, and the *tefach* is measured from the top of these objects to the top of the window opening.

## Temporary Objects



### Why They Don't Minimize

The *Tosefta* continues with a contrasting list: *aval hasheleg vehabarad vehagalid vehakefor vehamayim* — snow, hail, frost, and water. For all of these, *kulam ein mematin bechalon*: they do not diminish the size of the window.

The reason is not that these substances are *mekabel tumah* — that consideration would not apply straightforwardly, since random rainwater not set aside for drinking has not been designated as food and therefore lacks that status. Rather, the reason these items do not count is that they will not remain. They melt, flow away, or otherwise naturally dissipate. Even though they are not *mekabel tumah*, they fail the other requirement: physical permanence. An object in a window can only reduce the effective opening if it will stay there.

This now creates the central tension with Shmuel's position. The *Tosefta* seems entirely unconcerned with whether a person *plans* to remove an object — it only asks whether the object is *mekabel tumah* and whether it will physically remain on its own. By contrast, Shmuel's principle was precisely about intent to remove: the wafer does not count because you will take it to eat it. Yet the *Tosefta*'s list includes many objects that one would naturally want to remove, and it rules that they *do* minimize the window. The *Gemara* must now work through this tension, going through the list case by case.

## Maysivay to Shmuel (II)



### Grass, Rags, Animals

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אוֹ שֶׁעָלוּ מֵאֲלֵיהֶן. כֵּיוָן דְּקָשׁוּ לְכוֹתֶל – שָׁקֵיל לְהוּ! אָמַר רַבָּה: בְּכוֹתֶל חוּרְבָּה. רַב פָּפָּא אָמַר: אֲפִילּוּ בְּכוֹתֶל יִישּׁוּב – בְּבָאִין חוּץ לִשְׁלֹשָׁה לַחַלּוֹן.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* teaches: **Or** grass **that grew on its own** also reduces the dimensions of the window. The Gemara asks: But **since** the grass **damages the wall,** the owner **will remove it.** Therefore, it should not serve as a barrier to impurity. **Rabba says:** This is referring **to a wall of a ruin,** whose structural integrity is insignificant, and therefore the owner will not trouble himself to remove the grass. **Rav Pappa says:** The *baraita* may even be referring **to a wall** in **a settled** house, and it is referring to a case **where** the grass **comes from three** handbreadths **beyond** the **window.** In other words, the grass does not grow on the window but takes root some distance away, and from there it reaches the window. The homeowner is not particular about this grass and will not uproot it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַטְלוֹנִיּוֹת – חֲזוּ לֵיהּ לִקְרִיעָה דִלְבוּשָׁא! בִּסְמִיכְתָּא. חֲזוּ לְאוּמָּנָא! בְּרִיסַּקָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara further asks: Why do **scraps of fabric** reduce the dimensions of the window? After all, **they are fit for** patching **a tear in a garment.** The Gemara answers: This is referring **to thick** scraps, which are unsuitable for patching. The Gemara challenges: Nevertheless, they are **fit for a bloodletter** to wipe up the blood at the point of incision. The Gemara answers: It is referring **to sackcloth,** which scratches the skin, and would not be used for that purpose.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי בְּרִיסַּקָּא, ״שֶׁאֵין בָּהֶן שָׁלֹשׁ עַל שָׁלֹשׁ״?! ״שֶׁאֵין בָּהֶן אַרְבָּעָה עַל אַרְבָּעָה״ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ! כְּעֵין רִיסַּקָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If** it is referring **to sackcloth,** why does the *baraita* state **that it is not three by three** fingerbreadths? It **should have** said that **it is not four by four** handbreadths. Rough woven material of the kind used for sacks rather than clothes is susceptible to impurity only if its area measures at least four by four handbreadths. The Gemara answers: It is not actual sackcloth; rather, it is **like sackcloth,** i.e., it is stiff, and will therefore not be used by a bloodletter, but is woven like regular clothing.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָאֵבֶר וְהַבָּשָׂר הַמְדוּלְדָּלִין בִּבְהֵמָה וּבְחַיָּה – עָרְקָא וְאָזְלָא! בִּקְשׁוּרָה.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* teaches: **And a limb or flesh dangling from an animal or a beast** reduces the dimensions of a window. The Gemara asks: According to the opinion of Shmuel, why should this be so? After all, the animal can **arise and escape,** and therefore it should not be considered as part of the window. The Gemara answers: This is referring **to** an animal that is **tied** in place.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁחֵיט לַהּ! בִּטְמֵאָה. מְזַבֵּין לַהּ לְגוֹי! בִּכְחוּשָׁה. פָּסֵיק, שָׁדֵי לַהּ לִכְלָבִים! כֵּיוָן דְּאִיכָּא צַעַר בַּעֲלֵי חַיִּים, לָא עָבֵיד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: But the owner of the animal will take it and **slaughter it.** The Gemara answers: It is referring **to** an animal that is **non-kosher** and will not be slaughtered. The Gemara challenges: Even so, **he** will take it and **sell it to a gentile.** The Gemara responds: It is referring **to a lean** animal, which no one will buy. The Gemara continues: Even if the animal does not move, there is a use for the part that is dangling, **since he** can **cut it off** and **throw it to the dogs.** The Gemara answers: **Since there is suffering to an animal** if he cuts it off, **he will not do** that.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעוֹף שֶׁשָּׁכַן בַּחַלּוֹן. פָּרַח וְאָזֵיל! בְּקָשׁוּר. שָׁחֵיט לֵיהּ! בְּטָמֵא. מְזַבֵּין לֵיהּ לְגוֹי! בִּקְלָנִיתָא.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* further teaches: **And a bird resting in the window** reduces its dimensions. The Gemara challenges: But it will **fly away,** and therefore it should not be considered as part of the window. The Gemara answers: This is referring to a bird **that is tied** in place. The Gemara further challenges: But the owner will take it and **slaughter it.** The Gemara answers: This is referring **to a non-kosher bird,** which he will not slaughter. The Gemara continues: Even if it is non-kosher **he** will take it and **sell it to a gentile.** The Gemara responds: It is referring **to a *kelanita*,** a type of bird that is so bony that no one would purchase it to consume it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יָהֵיב לֵיהּ לְיָנוֹקָא! בִּמְסָרֵט. קְלָנִיתָא לָא מְסָרְטָא! כְּעֵין קְלָנִיתָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: But even so, **he** can **give it to a child** to play with, so why does it reduce the dimensions of the window? The Gemara answers: It is referring to a bird **that scratches.** The Gemara challenges: But **a *kelanita* does not scratch.** The Gemara answers: The *baraita* is referring to a type of bird that is **like a *kelanita*** in that it is bony, but is inclined to scratch people.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְגוֹי שֶׁיָּשַׁב בַּחַלּוֹן. קָאֵי וְאָזֵיל! בְּכָפוּת. אָתֵי חַבְרֵיהּ, שָׁרֵי לֵיהּ! בִּמְצוֹרָע. אָתֵי מְצוֹרָע חַבְרֵיהּ, שָׁרֵי לֵיהּ! אֶלָּא בַּחֲבוּשֵׁי מַלְכוּת.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* further states: **And a gentile sitting in the window** reduces its dimensions. The Gemara asks: But the gentile will **arise and leave,** so why does he reduce the dimensions of the window? The Gemara answers: This is referring **to** someone **who is tied** in place. The Gemara continues: **Another** person **will come** and **release him.** The Gemara answers: This is referring **to a leper,** whom people are afraid to touch. The Gemara challenges: **Another leper will come** and **release him. Rather,** this is referring **to a prisoner of the monarchy.** Since he is confined as a punishment, others are afraid to release him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבֶן שְׁמֹנֶה הַמּוּנָּח בַּחַלּוֹן. אָתְיָא אִמֵּיהּ, דָּרְיָא לֵיהּ! בְּשַׁבָּת; דְּתַנְיָא: בֶּן שְׁמֹנֶה הֲרֵי הוּא כְּאֶבֶן, וְאָסוּר לְטַלְטְלוֹ בְּשַׁבָּת. אֲבָל אִמּוֹ שׁוֹחָה עָלָיו וּמְנִיקָתוֹ, מִפְּנֵי הַסַּכָּנָה.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* teaches: **And** a child **born** after **eight** months of pregnancy **who is placed in the window** reduces its dimensions. The Gemara challenges: Perhaps **his mother will come and remove him** from there. The Gemara answers: This is referring **to Shabbat,** when it is prohibited to move this child, **as it is taught** in a *baraita*: A child **born** after **eight** months **is like a stone** with regard to the *halakhot* of set-aside [*muktze*], **and** therefore **it is prohibited to move him; but his mother may bend over** the child **and nurse him, due to the danger** that failure to nurse will cause her to fall ill.

The *Gemara* takes up each item in the *Tosefta* and asks why, given Shmuel's principle, we would not expect the owner to remove it.

Beginning with grass: *veha'asavim chazu livheimah* — grass is fit to feed to one's animal, so one would presumably want to take it out of the window. The *Gemara* answers: *be'afrazta* — we are speaking of a poisonous variety that cannot be fed to animals. As for grass that grew up on its own through the wall, the *Gemara* notes that since such growth damages the wall — *keivan dekashi lekotel* — the owner would certainly want to remove it. This, incidentally, is why the *Tosefta* is being brought here: the damage that grass causes to walls is the very issue our *mishnah* addresses. Rava answers that we are speaking of a wall in a desolate, ownerless area (*bekotel churva*) where nobody is concerned about maintaining the wall and nobody plans to remove the grass. Rav Pappa goes further and says it applies even to a wall in a settled area (*afilu bekotel yishuv*), because the grass is growing from the ground three *tefachim* away — just as our *mishnah* requires — and is simply bending over and falling into the window from that safe distance, causing no damage, and therefore giving no reason to remove it.

Moving to rags: a rag smaller than three by three fingerbreadths can still serve as a patch for a torn garment, so why would someone leave it in the window? One might wonder how a rag too small to be *mekabel tumah* can still be worth using as a patch — the answer is that not being significant enough for a person to make a point of storing away does not mean one would refuse to use it if it happened to be at hand. The *Gemara* answers: *b'smichta* — it is a thick, heavy rag that would not work well as a patch. But it could still be used by a blood-letter to wipe away blood after bloodletting. The *Gemara* answers: *b'riska* — it is rough and scratchy material, unsuitable for use on skin. The *Gemara* then raises a further challenge: if we are dealing with sackcloth (*briska*), then the threshold for being *mekabel tumah* is not three by three fingerbreadths but four by four *tefachim* — a much larger standard. So the *Tosefta* cannot be discussing sackcloth. The *Gemara* resolves: *k'ein briska* — not sackcloth itself, but a scratchy material like it, made of ordinary cloth. The upshot is that we have a tiny scrap of rough cloth with no practical use whatsoever, which is why it will be left in the window.

The *Gemara* then turns to the hanging limb: *v'ha'eiver v'habasar hamiduldalin bivheimah uvchayah* — if a limb is hanging from an animal that is standing in the window, surely the animal itself will wander off. The *Gemara* answers: *bikshurah* — the animal is tied down. But even so, one would eventually slaughter the animal. The *Gemara* answers: *bitmeiah* — it is a non-kosher animal, a mule for instance. But one would sell it to a non-Jew. The *Gemara* answers: *bichshusha* — it is so weak and emaciated that no one would want to buy it. But why not simply cut off the hanging limb and throw it to a dog as food? The *Gemara* answers: *keivan d'ika tza'ar ba'alei chayim lo avid* — since doing so would cause the animal pain, one would not do it. It is worth noting that the *Gemara* does not say it is forbidden; the general ruling of the *poskim*, beginning with the *Trumas Hadeshen*, is that *tza'ar ba'alei chayim* is permitted when it serves a human purpose. Here the point is simply that this would not be one's practical intention. The result is a highly contrived scenario: an emaciated, non-kosher animal tied in a window with a partially dangling limb, where no plan for removal exists.

For the bird: *parach v'azil* — it will fly away. *Bikshur* — it is tied down. The word *sheshachan* in the *Tosefta* — describing a bird that has settled or alighted in the window — does not naturally suggest a bird that has been captured and bound, yet that is precisely the scenario the *Gemara* requires in order to explain why it would not simply fly away. *Shachit lei* — but one would slaughter and eat it. *Betame* — it is a non-kosher bird. *Mazbenei lei lenochri* — one would sell it to a non-Jew. *Bekalanisa* — we are dealing with a *kalanisa*, a bird that Rashi identifies as very thin and of no culinary value, so no one would want to buy it. *Yahvei lei lyanuka* — but one might give it to a child as a pet. *Bimsarit* — it is a scratchy, abrasive bird and no one would want it as a pet. The *Gemara* then notes that a *kalanisa* by definition does not scratch — *kalanisa lo masreta* — so the resolution is: *keein kalanisa*, something like a *kalanisa*, a similarly worthless but scratchy bird. One is left with a tied-up, unsellable, uneatable, too-scratchy-to-be-a-pet bird sitting in the window.

The *Gemara* turns to the non-Jew: *kai ve'azil* — he will get up and leave. *Bekafut* — he is bound. *Atei chavreia vesharei lei* — his friend will come and untie him. *Bimetzora* — he has a communicable disease and no one will approach him. *Atei metzora chavreia vesharei lei* — even so, a fellow sufferer might come and free him. *Ela bechavushei malchut* — the final resolution: he is being held prisoner by the government and is chained there under governmental authority, so no one may free him. We have arrived at the scenario of a government prisoner in a window.

### Salt to Sefer Torah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֶלַח – חַזְיָא לֵיהּ! בִּמְרִירְתָּא. חַזְיָא לְעוֹרוֹת! דְּאִית בַּהּ קוֹצֵי.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* teaches: **Salt** reduces the dimensions of a window. The Gemara challenges: **It is fit** for use and people will remove it from there. The Gemara answers: This is referring **to bitter** salt, which is not used as a seasoning. The Gemara challenges: Nevertheless, **it is fit for** tanning **hides.** The Gemara responds: It is referring to salt **that has thorns** mixed with it, and therefore it will not be used for tanning.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֵּיוָן דְּקַשְׁיָא לְכוֹתֶל, שָׁקְלָא! דְּיָתְבָא אַחַסְפָּא. חַסְפָּא גּוּפָא תֵּיחוּץ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: Even so, **since** this salt **is damaging to the wall, he will remove it** from there. The Gemara answers: This is referring to a case **where it sits on a shard** of earthenware, and consequently it does not damage the wall. The Gemara states: If it is resting on earthenware, **let** the **shard itself serve as a barrier** against the spreading of the impurity. Why, then, is the salt mentioned?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּלֵית בַּהּ שִׁיעוּרָא; כְּדִתְנַן: חֶרֶס – כְּדֵי לִיתֵּן בֵּין פַּצִּים לַחֲבֵירוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: This is referring to a case **where** the shard **does not have** the sufficient **measure** for ritual impurity, and is therefore considered insignificant. **As we learned** in a mishna (*Shabbat* 82a): One who carries a shard of **earthenware** on Shabbat is liable only if it is **equivalent** in size to that which is used **to place between one pillar and another** when they are piled on the ground, to strengthen the pillars.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּלִי חֶרֶס – חֲזֵי לֵיהּ! דְּמִיטַּנַּף. חֲזֵי לְאוּמָּנָא! דִּמְנַקַּב.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* teaches: **An earthenware vessel** reduces the dimensions of a window. The Gemara challenges: But it is **fit for one** to use; therefore, it is likely to be removed from the window. The Gemara answers: This is referring to a case **where** the earthenware **is dirty.** The Gemara challenges: Even so, **it is fit for a bloodletter** to collect the blood. It would not matter to him if the earthenware were dirty. The Gemara answers: It is referring to a case **where it is perforated** and therefore unfit for that use.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה – חֲזֵי לְמִקְרָא! בְּבָלוּי. וְהָא בָּעֵי גְּנִיזָה! שָׁם תְּהֵא גְּנִיזָתָהּ.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* teaches: **A Torah scroll** reduces the dimensions of a window. The Gemara challenges: But it **is fit for reading;** therefore, it might be removed. The Gemara answers: This is referring to a Torah scroll **that is worn out** and unfit for reading. The Gemara challenges: **But** one is **required** to place the Torah scroll in **a repository** for unusable sacred books; therefore, he will certainly remove it to be stored away. The Gemara answers: This is referring to one who determines that **its repository will be there.** In other words, it was placed in the window with the intent of storing it there in its worn-out state.

Turning to salt: *chazi lei* — salt is useful, so one would remove it. *Bemarirata* — it is bitter salt with no culinary use. *Chazi le'oros* — one could still use it for tanning leather. *De'it bei kotzei* — it is filled with thistles and therefore unusable for that purpose as well. *Keivan dekashei lekotel shaklei lei* — but salt damages a wall, so one would remove it to protect the wall. *Deyaspa chaspa* — it is sitting on a pottery shard and not in direct contact with the wall, so it does no damage.

The *Gemara* then asks: but the pottery shard itself — *chaspa gufa teichatz* — would also be a partition between the salt and the wall, and so it should independently count as something that diminishes the window size. The *Gemara* answers: *deleis bei shiura* — the pottery shard is so small it has no *halachic* significance. The *Gemara* cites the *mishnah* in *Shabbos*: the minimum significant size of a pottery shard is one large enough to be placed between stacked beams to keep them from warping. This shard is smaller than that. Tosafot raises a difficulty here: we already encountered a rag smaller than three by three fingerbreadths — also not normally significant for *Shabbos* purposes — that nonetheless *does* diminish the window. Why should the pottery shard be any different? Tosafot resolves this by suggesting the pottery shard is so thin in profile that it could not itself reduce the opening to less than a *tefach*, which is why the *Tosefta* does not mention it. The *mishnah* in *Shabbos* is cited only to explain why one would not bother removing the shard — and since neither the shard nor the salt has any use, neither will be removed.

Moving to the pottery vessel (*kli cheres*): *chazi lei* — it is fit for use. *Demitneif* — it has become soiled and is disgusting. *Chazi leumna* — a blood-letter could still use it as a vessel for collecting blood. *Deminkav* — it has a hole in it, rendering it useless even for that purpose.

Finally, the *Sefer Torah*: surely of all things, one would remove it from a window. *Chazi lemikra* — it is fit for reading. *Beviloi* — it is completely worn out. *Veha ba'ei gnizah* — but even a worn-out *Sefer Torah* requires proper burial in a *genizah*; one cannot simply leave it in a window. The *Gemara* answers: *shama gnizaso* — that location in the window is itself its place of resting, effectively its *genizah*. The details of what constitutes a valid *genizah* and whether an open window truly qualifies are questions one could explore at length, but that is the *Gemara*'s resolution.

Stepping back, what is remarkable about the *Gemara*'s treatment of this entire *Tosefta* is that virtually every item on the list would naturally be removed, and in each case the *Gemara* has constructed an elaborate and highly unusual circumstance to explain why removal is not planned. The *pshat* of the *Tosefta* runs squarely against Shmuel's position — intent to remove seems irrelevant to whether something diminishes the window — yet the *Gemara* expends considerable effort constructing these *okimtot* to preserve Shmuel's ruling.

## Salt/Fat Mechitza



### Rav's Mechitza Rule

Rav taught: *bechol osin mechitza chutz memelach verevav* — one may construct a *mechitza* for *Shabbos* purposes from any material, except salt and fat (*revav*). The context here is not the *mechitzos* familiar from *beit knesset* usage but rather *mechitzos* that create a *reshus hayachid* or serve purposes of *eiruv* or *sukkah*. Salt and fat are excluded because they lack permanence: a pile of salt will scatter and fat will dissolve. Even if they currently have a recognizable form, they will not maintain it, and so they cannot function *halachically* as a wall.

### Shmuel vs. Salt Types

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַב: בַּכֹּל עוֹשִׂין מְחִיצָה, חוּץ מִמֶּלַח וּרְבָב. וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: אֲפִילּוּ מֶלַח. אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: וְלָא פְּלִיגִי – הָא בְּמֶלַח סְדוֹמִית, הָא בְּמֶלַח אִיסְתְּרוֹקָנִית.
> **Translation:** § With regard to the *halakha* of the *baraita* referring to salt, the Gemara cites that which **Rav says: One can construct a barrier** to delineate a private domain on Shabbat or to block the spreading of ritual impurity **with anything except for salt and fat,** as salt crumbles and fat melts in the heat. **And Shmuel says: Even salt** can be used as a barrier. **Rav Pappa said: And they do not disagree,** as **this** ruling of Shmuel is referring to **Sodomite salt,** which is like stone and can be used as a barrier, and **that** ruling of Rav is referring **to *isterokanit* salt,** which is taken from the sea and is composed of grains.

Shmuel disagrees: *afilu melach* — even salt may be used for a *mechitza*. Rav Pappa resolves the apparent dispute: *lo peligi* — they do not actually disagree. *Ha b'melach Sedomis ha b'melach Istronknis* — one is speaking of *melach Sedomis*, mineral salt, which is hard and retains its form, especially when cut from the ground; the other is speaking of *melach Istronknis*, sea salt, which is granular and does not hold its shape. Rav's prohibition applies to sea salt, while Shmuel's permission applies to mineral salt.

### Salt Mounds & Beam

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַשְׁתָּא דְּאָמַר רַבָּה: עוֹשֶׂה אָדָם שְׁנֵי צִבּוּרֵי מֶלַח וּמַנִּיחַ עֲלֵיהֶם קוֹרָה, שֶׁהַמֶּלַח מַעֲמֶדֶת אֶת הַקּוֹרָה וְהַקּוֹרָה מַעֲמֶדֶת אֶת הַמֶּלַח; אֲפִילּוּ מֶלַח אִיסְתְּרוֹקָנִית – וְלָא פְּלִיגִי: הָא דְּאִיכָּא קוֹרָה, הָא דְּלֵיכָּא קוֹרָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara adds: **And now that Rabba said:** If **a person makes two piles of salt** at the opening to an alleyway **and places** a cross **beam on top of them,** so **that the salt supports the** cross **beam and the** cross **beam supports the salt** by weighing it down and compressing it, he can use this beam to render it permitted to carry in the alleyway on Shabbat, one can say that **even *isterokanit* salt** can be used as a barrier. **And** even so, Rav and Shmuel **do not disagree: This** ruling of Shmuel is referring to a case **where there is** a cross **beam** to weigh the salt down, and **that** ruling of Rava is referring to a case where **there is no** cross **beam.**

The *Gemara* adds a further nuance in the name of Rabbah: *oseh adam shenei tziburei melach umaniyach aleihem korah* — a person may construct two mounds of salt and place a beam across them. The principle at work is mutual reinforcement: the salt supports the beam, and the beam by its weight presses down and keeps the salt in place. In this configuration even sea salt (*melach Istronknis*) can serve as the posts of a *tzuras hapethach* for an *eiruv* or *mavoi*. Thus the final resolution: *ha d'ika korah ha d'leika korah* — the distinction between the positions is simply whether a beam is resting on top of the salt or not.

This discussion of salt and walls flows naturally from the earlier treatment of salt in the window context, which is what prompted the digression.

## Mills & Ovens



### Normal & Donkey Mills

The *mishnah* now turns to objects that must be kept at a distance from a wall: *marchikin es harechayim gimel min hashechev shehein daled min harechev* — one must distance a mill three *tefachim* from the *shechev*, the base of the mill, which translates to four *tefachim* from the *rechev*, the grinding component. The base protrudes more widely than the grinding part, so something three *tefachim* from the wider base is four *tefachim* from the narrower grinding element above it.

Similarly: *v'shel chamor gimel min ha'istrobiel shehein daled min hakelat* — a donkey mill, so called because donkeys were sometimes employed to turn it, though it is a smaller and more portable device, must likewise be kept three *tefachim* from its base (*istrobiel*), which is four *tefachim* from its grinding component (*kelat*). The structural geometry is parallel.

### Vibrations vs. Sound

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַרְחִיקִין אֶת הָרֵיחַיִם שְׁלֹשָׁה מִן הַשֶּׁכֶב, שֶׁהֵן אַרְבָּעָה מִן הָרֶכֶב וְכוּ׳. מַאי טַעְמָא? מִשּׁוּם טִירְיָא. וְהָא תַּנְיָא: וְשֶׁל חֲמוֹר – שְׁלֹשָׁה מִן הָאִיסְטְרוֹבֵיל שֶׁהֵן אַרְבָּעָה מִן הַקֶּלֶת; הָתָם מַאי טִירְיָא אִיכָּא? אֶלָּא מִשּׁוּם קָלָא.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that **one must distance a mill** from a neighbor’s wall by a distance of **three** handbreadths **from the lower stone** of the mill, **which is four** handbreadths **from the upper stone** of the mill. The Gemara asks: **What is the reason** that one must distance a mill from the property of his neighbor? It is **due to the vibrations** it causes. The Gemara asks: **But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita*: **And** the measure for distancing a mill **on a base** is **three** handbreadths **from the lower millstone [*ha’isterobil*], which is four** handbreadths **from the mouth [*hakelet*],** where the wheat is fed in? But **there, what vibrations are there? Rather,** the reason for the distancing is **due to the noise** generated by the mill.

The *Gemara* asks *mai ta'ama* — what is the reason for distancing a mill from the wall? *Mishum tiraya* — because of the vibrations. When a heavy mill operates close to a wall, the vibrations destabilize it. The *Gemara* then challenges: but by the donkey mill, the same law applies — *mai tiraya ika?* — yet a lighter, more portable mill would not produce significant vibrations. *Ella mishum kala* — rather, the reason for the donkey mill is noise. The sound waves emanating from a mill operating very close to a wall cause damage to the wall. The two rationales — vibration for the large mill, sound for the small one — both lead to the same required distance.

### Tannur & Kirah

*Vechen tannur shalosh min hakalya shehein arba min hasafa* — and similarly, an oven (*tannur*) must be three *tefachim* from its base (*kalya*), which is four *tefachim* from its mouth (*safa*). The *tannur* was shaped roughly like a large pot, wider at the base and narrower at the opening. Rashi understands this as a vessel wide at the bottom and narrow at the top, like an inverted beaker, where the three *tefachim* are measured from the wide base and the four from the narrower mouth. Tosafot suggests a pot-belly shape — narrow at top and bottom, widest in the middle — where the three *tefachim* are from the protruding middle section and the four from the mouth.

### Abaye's Inference

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֶת הַתַּנּוּר – שְׁלֹשָׁה מִן הַכִּלְיָא, שֶׁהֵן אַרְבָּעָה מִן הַשָּׂפָה. אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ, כִּלְיָא דְּתַנּוּר טֶפַח; נָפְקָא מִינַּהּ לְמִקָּח וּמִמְכָּר.
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches: **And** there must be a distance of **three** handbreadths **from the** protruding **base** of **an oven** until the wall, **which is four** handbreadths **from the** narrow **upper rim** of the oven. **Abaye said: Learn from** the mishna that **the base of an oven** is **a handbreadth** wider than its rim. The practical **difference** of this observation is with respect **to buying and selling,** i.e., a buyer should know that this is the proper ratio for the dimensions of an oven.

Abaye draws a practical inference: *shema mina kalya de-tannur tefach* — we learn from these measurements that the base (or for Tosafot, the protruding middle section) of a *tannur* extends one *tefach* beyond the mouth. The arithmetic is straightforward: three *tefachim* from one point equals four from another, meaning the difference of one *tefach* represents the protrusion of the base. Abaye notes the practical implication: *nafka mina le-mekkach u-mimkar* — this matters for commercial transactions. If one orders or purchases an oven and it is delivered without the full extent of that base protrusion, the buyer has grounds for complaint.

With that, the *mishnah*'s discussion of objects requiring distancing from a wall comes to a close. The gemara that follows this *mishnah* contains the pivotal dispute between Abaye and Rava over whether a person who arrives first at a boundary must still position himself with anticipation of a future neighbor's needs, and the central debate between Rabbi Yose and the *Chachamim* — *al ha-mazzik le-harchik et atzmo o al ha-nizzak le-harchik et atzmo* — whether it is the one causing damage or the one liable to be damaged who bears the obligation to distance himself — a question that sits at the heart of this entire *perek*, and which we have treated in full in the preceding discussion.

## Home Oven Safety



### First Story Oven

The *mishnah* now addresses oven safety within a home: *lo ya'amid adam tannur be-toch ha-bayit ella im ken yesh al gabav govah arba amot* — a person may not install an oven inside a house unless there are four *amot* — approximately six feet — between the top of the oven and the ceiling. The concern operates on two levels: fire safety in general, and specifically the damage that rising heat and steam can cause to the ceiling, which is simultaneously the floor of the story above where a neighbor may be living.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ לֹא יַעֲמִיד אָדָם תַּנּוּר בְּתוֹךְ הַבַּיִת, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן יֵשׁ עַל גַּבָּיו גּוֹבַהּ אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת. הָיָה מַעֲמִידוֹ בַּעֲלִיָּיה – צָרִיךְ שֶׁיְּהֵא תַּחְתָּיו מַעֲזִיבָה שְׁלֹשָׁה טְפָחִים. וּבַכִּירָה – טֶפַח.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **A person may not set up an oven inside a house unless there is a space four cubits high above it,** i.e., between the top of the oven and the ceiling, to avoid burning the ceiling, which serves as the floor of the residence above. If **one was setting up** an oven **in the upper story, there must be a plaster floor beneath it,** which serves as the ceiling of the lower story, at least **three handbreadths** thick, so that the ceiling below does not burn. **And** in the case **of a stove** the plaster floor must be at least one **handbreadth** thick.

### Second Story Oven


*Haya ma'amido be-aliya* — if the oven is being installed on an upper story, *tzarich she-yehei tachtav ma'aziva shelosha tefachim* — there must be three *tefachim* of plaster (*ma'aziva*) in the floor beneath it. This serves to insulate the lower neighbor's ceiling from the heat of the oven above, preventing structural damage or fire. The four-*amot* clearance above the oven remains applicable on the upper story as well.

### Kirah Requirement

*U've-kirah tefach* — for a *kirah*, a smaller oven, it is sufficient to have one *tefach* of plaster beneath it. Rashi explains that because the *kirah* is smaller, it does not produce as large a fire, and the reduced requirement follows accordingly. Though one might ask whether this should also affect the four-*amot* clearance required above — the *mishnah* does not say so explicitly.

### Liability Debate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִם הִזִּיק – מְשַׁלֵּם מַה שֶּׁהִזִּיק. רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: לֹא אָמְרוּ כׇּל הַשִּׁיעוּרִין הָאֵלּוּ, אֶלָּא שֶׁאִם הִזִּיק – פָּטוּר מִלְּשַׁלֵּם.
> **Translation:** **And if** he **causes damage** in any case, **he pays** compensation for that **which he damaged. Rabbi Shimon says: They said all of these measurements** to teach **only that if he causes damage he is exempt from paying,** as he took all reasonable precautions.

*Ve-im hizzik meshalem mah she-hizzik* — even if one has complied with all of these specifications, if damage results one remains liable. *Rabbi Shimon omer*: the specifications were given precisely in order to define exemption — *lo amru kol ha-shi'urim ha-elu ella she-im hizzik patur mi-leshalem* — if one followed all the required measures and damage still occurred, one is exempt from payment.

This disagreement touches on a fundamental question in *Bava Kamma*. Normally there is an obligation of *shemirah* — watchfulness — over one's fire, and liability follows from a failure of that watchfulness. Rabbi Shimon's position is natural: compliance with the prescribed standards constitutes adequate *shemirah*, and therefore exempts the owner. The *Tanna Kamma*'s position is more nuanced. One way to understand it is that these specifications, while legally sufficient to permit operating the oven, do not actually constitute full *shemirah* against all possible damage. True *shemirah* might require far greater clearance — twenty feet, or more — but demanding that would make ordinary domestic life impossible. So the *halacha* adopts a middle position: the standards define what is sufficient for the purpose of permitted use, but they do not remove the underlying liability that attaches to fire. A parallel exists in *Bava Kamma* with respect to animals in *reshus harabim*: several *Rishonim* explain that an animal's natural conduct in a public domain does not trigger liability precisely because imposing liability would make ordinary use of public space untenable. Here the tension is similar but unresolved in the permissive direction — we allow the oven but do not eliminate the liability.

## Shops Under Storage



### Bakery & Dye Shop Ban

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֹא יִפְתַּח אָדָם חֲנוּת שֶׁל נַחְתּוֹמִין וְשֶׁל צַבָּעִין תַּחַת אוֹצָרוֹ שֶׁל חֲבֵירוֹ, וְלֹא רֶפֶת בָּקָר. בֶּאֱמֶת בַּיַּיִן הִתִּירוּ, אֲבָל לֹא רֶפֶת בָּקָר.
> **Translation:** The mishna continues: **A person may not open a bakery or a dye shop beneath the storeroom of another, and** he **may not** establish **a cattle barn** there, as these produce heat, smoke, and odors, which rise and damage the items in the storeroom. The mishna comments: **In truth,** the *halakha* is that in the case **of** a storeroom of **wine** the Sages rendered it **permitted** to set up a bakery and a dye shop beneath, as the heat that rises does not damage the wine. **But** they did **not** render it permitted to establish **a cattle barn,** because its odor damages the wine.

*Lo yiftach adam chanut shel nachtomim veshel tzeva'im tachas otzaro shel chavero* — a person may not open a bakery or a dyeing shop beneath another's storehouse. The fumes, smoke, and heat rising from these operations will damage whatever the upper neighbor is storing — grain, straw, fruit, and similar goods.

### Cowshed Prohibition

*Velo refet bakar* — nor may one operate a cowshed beneath a storehouse. The odor from cattle is sufficiently penetrating and damaging that it is always forbidden, regardless of what is stored above.

### Wine Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ וְהָתַנְיָא: בַּתַּנּוּר אַרְבָּעָה, וּבַכִּירָה שְׁלֹשָׁה! אֲמַר אַבָּיֵי: כִּי תַּנְיָא הָהִיא – בִּדְנַחְתּוֹמִין, דְּתַנּוּר דִּידַן כִּי כִּירָה דְנַחְתּוֹמִין.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara asks: **But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita* that in the case of **an oven** the plaster floor must be **four** handbreadths thick, **and with regard to a stove** it must be **three?** By contrast, the mishna says that the plaster floor beneath and oven and a stove must be three handbreadths and one handbreadth thick, respectively. **Abaye said: When that** *baraita* **is taught** it is **with regard to** ovens and stoves **of bakers.** Since they bake all day long, their implements get very hot. The **oven** discussed **in our** mishna is similar to **a baker’s stove,** which is why in both cases a distance of three handbreadths is required.

*Be'emet beyayin hitiru aval lo refet bakar* — in truth, when the storehouse contains wine, the prohibition on bakeries and dye shops is lifted, because the warmth and fumes from those operations actually improve wine rather than harming it. The cowshed prohibition, however, remains in force even for wine storage, since the smell from cattle spoils the wine.

A *tanna* adds: *beyayin hitiru mipnei she-mashpicho* — the bakery fumes benefit wine. But *velo refes bakar mipnei she-masricho* — the cowshed still ruins it.

It is worth pausing here to note that the gemara also resolves a tension between our mishnah and a baraita on the required thickness of plaster insulation. Where our mishnah specifies three *tefachim* for a *tannur* and one for a *kirah*, the baraita gives four for a *tannur* and three for a *kirah*. Abaye explains — *ki tanya ha-hi be-nachtomim* — that the baraita is speaking specifically about a baker's oven, a commercial-scale operation considerably larger than a household stove. Indeed, *de-tannur didan ke-kirah de-nachtomim* — our ordinary household *tannur*, which requires three *tefachim*, is comparable in size to a baker's *kirah*, so the different figures are entirely consistent once the scale of the appliance is taken into account.

### Priority Baraita

A *braita* addresses the question of priority: *im hayta refet kodemet le-otzar muttar* — if the cowshed was there first, and the upper neighbor subsequently decided to use the space as a storehouse, the cowshed owner need not relocate. First occupancy confers rights, and the one who arrived later with the storehouse cannot compel the prior occupant to move.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֹא יִפְתַּח חֲנוּת וְכוּ׳. תָּנָא: אִם הָיְתָה רֶפֶת קוֹדֶמֶת לָאוֹצָר – מוּתָּר. בָּעֵי אַבָּיֵי: כִּיבֵּד וְרִיבֵּץ לְאוֹצָר, מַהוּ?
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches that one **may not open a bakery** or a dye shop beneath the storeroom of another, and he may not establish a cattle barn there. A Sage **taught: If the cattle barn preceded the storeroom it is permitted,** i.e., the barn owner is not required to move it. With regard to this point, **Abaye raises a dilemma:** If **he cleaned and sprinkled** the area, i.e., he prepared it for use **as a storeroom** but he has not yet filled it, **what is** the *halakha*? Is it considered a storeroom already, and therefore others may no longer put a cattle barn beneath it, or perhaps the *halakha* is that as long as it is empty he cannot prevent others from establishing a cattle barn?

### Abaye's Questions


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רִיבָּה בַּחַלּוֹנוֹת, מַהוּ? אַכְסַדְרָה תַּחַת הָאוֹצָר, מַהוּ? בָּנָה עֲלִיָּיה עַל גַּבֵּי בֵּיתוֹ, מַהוּ? תֵּיקוּ. בָּעֵי רַב הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: תַּמְרֵי וְרִמּוֹנֵי, מַאי? תֵּיקוּ.
> **Translation:** Similarly, if he **added windows** for ventilation, which demonstrates his intention to use it as a storeroom, **what** is the *halakha*? Likewise, if he establishes **an enclosed veranda beneath the storeroom, what** is the *halakha*? If he **built an upper room on top of his house** for storage, **what** is the *halakha*? None of these questions are answered, and the Gemara declares that they **shall stand** unresolved. The Gemara cites a similar question: **Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua, raises a dilemma:** If he placed **dates and pomegranates** there, **what** is the *halakha*? Is this considered the start of its use as a storeroom or not? No answer was found to this question either, and the Gemara declares: The dilemma **shall stand [*teiku*]** unresolved.

Abaye poses a series of questions about the boundary of this priority rule. *Kibbed veribetz le-otzar mahu* — what if the upper-story owner had begun sweeping and preparing the space for use as a storehouse, making his intention clear, but had not yet actually stored anything there, when the lower-story person moved in a cowshed? Is the storehouse considered to have priority because of the preparatory activity? *Reiba be-chalonos mahu* — what if he went further and made structural alterations, opening up additional windows to provide air and light for storage — is that sufficient to establish priority? *Bana aliya gabei bayis mahu* — or what if the upper story itself was built explicitly for storage purposes but has not yet been put to use? The *Gemara* leaves all of these unresolved: *teiku*.

Rav Huna the son of Rav Yehoshua poses a further question: *tamrei veriminei mai* — what about a storehouse containing dates or pomegranates, which are presumably less sensitive to the smells from below? Does the same prohibition apply? This too is left as *teiku*.

### Yosef & Sheshes

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בֶּאֱמֶת בְּיַיִן הִתִּירוּ וְכוּ׳. תָּנָא: בְּיַיִן הִתִּירוּ – מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּשְׁבִּיחוֹ, וְלֹא רֶפֶת בָּקָר – מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּסְרִיחוֹ. אָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: הַאי דִּידַן, אֲפִילּוּ קוּטְרָא דִּשְׁרָגָא נָמֵי קַשְׁיָא לֵיהּ. אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: וְאַסְפַּסְתָּא כְּרֶפֶת בָּקָר דָּמְיָא.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that **in truth, it is permitted** in the case **of wine** but not in the case of a cattle barn. The Gemara states that a Sage **taught: They permitted** it in the case **of wine because** the heat and the smoke **improve** the wine. **But** they did **not** permit one to establish **a cattle barn, because** a barn **creates a bad odor. Rav Yosef said: This** wine **of ours** spoils quickly, and therefore **even the smoke of a candle also damages it. Rav Sheshet said: And alfalfa [*ve’aspasta*] is considered like a cattle barn** in this regard, because it rots over time and creates a foul odor.

The discussion of wine generates two further rulings from later Amoraim. Rav Yosef observes: *hai didan afilu kutra de-sharga nami kasha lei* — our wine in Babylonia is particularly sensitive, and even the smoke from a single candle is damaging to it. There is therefore no relaxation of the protective rules for wine; if anything, greater care is required.

Rav Sheshes adds: *aspasta ke-refes bakar damya* — *aspasta*, vetch, a coarse fodder used for animal feed, produces an odor so pungent that storing it below a storehouse is treated exactly like operating a cowshed. The prohibition applies with full force.
