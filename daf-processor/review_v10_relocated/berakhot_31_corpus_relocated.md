## Prior Daf Review



### Havdalah Ruling

Before turning to the new *perek*, a brief correction is in order regarding *havdalah* in *shomea tefillah*. Although *shomea tefillah* serves as an opportunity to make up missed insertions — such as *tal umatar livracha* — the ruling is that *havdalah* may not be inserted there. The reason is that *shomea tefillah* accommodates only *bakashot*, personal petitions. Just as one does not recite *mashiv haruach umorid hageshem* within *shmoneh esrei* if it was missed, one does not recite *havdalah* in *shomea tefillah*. Accordingly, if *havdalah* was omitted from *chonen hada'at*, it is skipped entirely within *shmoneh esrei* and recited instead *al hakos*.

### Missed Blessings

A related question concerns one who misses something like *tal umatar livracha* and has already passed *shomea tefillah* but has not yet concluded *shmoneh esrei*. There are two opinions: some hold that one returns to *shomea tefillah*, but the ruling is that one returns to the blessing where the insertion properly belongs — that is, to *birkat hashanim*. This parallels the rule that if *ya'aleh veyavo* is omitted, one returns to *retzei*, the blessing to which it belongs.

### Musaf Obligation

The *gemara* on the previous *daf* raised an interesting question surrounding *musaf*. Some opinions hold that the obligation of *musaf* applies only when there is a *tzibur*, or alternatively that an individual fulfills the obligation through the *tzibur* and need not recite *musaf* independently. The reasoning reflects a fundamental distinction: *musaf* is entirely oriented toward the *korban tzibur*, the communal sacrifice. It is therefore not amenable to a *tefillat nedavah*, which presupposes the framework of personal prayer. Though we rule that even a *yachid* with no *minyan* must recite *musaf*, this does not necessarily undermine the core principle that *musaf* belongs to the domain of communal sacrifice rather than personal prayer. The parameters of what constitutes a *tzibur* for this purpose — whether one's city, one's immediate community — are left undefined, presumably because the ruling was never adopted in practice and there was no need to resolve it halachically.

### Beit Midrash Prayer


A final theme from the previous *daf* deserves mention: the *rabbanim* who chose to pray in the *beit midrash* even though there were thirteen synagogues available to them. This reflects a broader and recurring tension — not only between *talmud Torah* and *tefillah* in general, but between two distinct institutional worlds. The *beit midrash* was a rabbinic domain, governed by the *rabbanim*, whereas the *batei kneisiyot* were organized under a different form of leadership. Something of that structural tension is alluded to in that discussion.

## Koved Rosh & Awe



### Standing with Seriousness

The new *perek* moves beyond questions of the text and timing of *davening* — though those discussions contained within them important questions of *kavanah* and the tension expressed by *lo ta'as tefillatkha keva* — toward a direct examination of the mindset one brings to prayer. The *mishna* opens: *ein omdin lehitpallel ela mitoch koved rosh* — one does not stand to *daven* except from a sense of weightiness of head, of seriousness of purpose.

### Ancient Pious Ones

The *mishna* continues: *chassidim harishonim*, the ancient pious ones, would spend a full *sha'ah* — a full hour, or at minimum a significant period of time — before standing to pray, *kedei sheyachavnu libam l'avihem shebashamayim*, so that they might direct their hearts properly to their Father in Heaven when they rose to *daven*.

### No Interruption

From here the *mishna* turns to a related principle: the mindset of standing before God must be maintained throughout prayer itself. Even if a king greets one during prayer, one does not respond. This stands in direct contrast to the rules learned earlier regarding *shema*, where one may interrupt in certain circumstances to respond to a greeting. During *tefillah* there is no such latitude — *afilu nachash karuch al akevo lo yafsik*, even if a snake is coiled around one's ankle, one does not interrupt. The *gemara* will examine just how far this principle extends, including whether it applies literally in cases of genuine danger.

The *gemara* asks: *minayin*, from where is this requirement derived? Rabbi Elazar cites the verse describing Chana as *marat nafesh* — of anguished soul — positioning her as the paradigm for the proper disposition in prayer. This is a striking derivation. The *gemara* itself immediately challenges it: perhaps Chana is simply different, *dehavat mirira liba tufa*, she was exceptionally bitter because she was unable to bear children — that is a particular circumstance, not a universal model. The word *marah* translated literally means bitter, though "anguished" captures the sense more precisely in English.

### Fear & Awe

Rabbi Yosef bar Rabbi Chanina therefore offers a different verse: *va'ani berov chasdecha avo veitecha, eshtachaveh el heichal kodshecha beyiratecha* — and I, through Your great kindness, will enter Your house; I will bow in Your holy palace, in awe of You. This is closer to the concept of *koved rosh*, since entering prayer with *yirah*, with fear and awe of God, is a more direct parallel than anguish. Again the *gemara* challenges: perhaps David is simply different — he would genuinely anguish himself in his prayers beyond the standard of others, and this verse describes his exceptional practice rather than setting a universal norm.

### Reinterpretation Behadrat


Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi therefore derives the requirement from yet another verse: *hishtachavu l'Hashem behadrat kodesh* — bow down before God in the glory of holiness. The *gemara* reads: *al tikrei behadrat ela becherdat* — do not read it as *behadrat*, in glory, but as *becherdat*, in trembling. This is a directive addressed to all, not a first-person account from a single figure. The *gemara* presses again: who says the wordplay of *hadrat* becoming *cherdat* is compelling? Why not take the plain meaning of the verse?

The practical example offered is that of Rav Yehuda, who would dress himself nicely before praying — *metzayein nafshei vehadar matzlei* — suggesting that standing before God calls for a certain decorum and dignity. However, it is noted that proper dress reflects *kavod*, a sense of standing before the King, but this does not necessarily entail the sense of trembling and awe that the *gemara* is seeking to ground in the verse. There is, then, a distinction — and perhaps a productive tension — between *koved rosh* understood as seriousness of purpose, and *koved rosh* understood as trembling awe. The *gemara*'s various derivations explore both dimensions, moving from Chana's anguish through David's fearful intimacy to the trembling demanded of all.

## Service in Awe



### Prayer as Avodah


The *gemara* finally settles on the verse from which *koved rosh* is definitively derived: *ivdu et Hashem beyirah vegilu bir'adah* — serve God in awe, and rejoice in trembling. Rashi, though the *gemara* itself does not make the connection explicit, links *ivdu* to the concept of *avodah*: *tefillah she-hee lanu b'makom avodah asu osah b'yirah* — prayer, which stands in place of the sacrificial service, should be performed in awe. Whether or not one draws the explicit link to *korbanot*, the core principle is clear: one approaches God in prayer with a sense of awe, and that is what is meant by *koved rosh*.

### Father & King

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵלֵךְ וְאֶסְתַּתֵּר בִּפְנֵי אֶלְקָנָה בַּעֲלִי, וְכֵיוָן דְּמִסְתַּתַּרְנָא מַשְׁקוּ לִי מֵי סוֹטָה, וְאִי אַתָּה עוֹשֶׂה תּוֹרָתְךָ פְּלַסְתֵּר, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״וְנִקְּתָה וְנִזְרְעָה זָרַע״.
> **Translation:** What was Hannah threatening? She said: **I will go and seclude myself** with another man **before Elkana, my husband. Since I secluded myself, they will force me to drink the *sota* water** to determine whether or not I have committed adultery. I will be found innocent, **and** since **You will not make Your Torah false [*pelaster*],** I will bear children. With regards to a woman who is falsely suspected of adultery and drank the *sota* water, the Torah **says:** “And if the woman was not defiled, but was pure, then **she shall be acquitted and she shall conceive”** (Numbers 5:28).

> **Translation:** However, Rabbi Elazar’s opinion **works out well** according **to the one who said** that the verse means: **If she were barren, she will be remembered** by God and granted children. That works out **well. But** according **to the one who said** that the verse means that childbearing will be easier and more successful, i.e., **if she had** previously **given birth with pain,** she now **gives birth with ease,** or if she had previously given birth to **daughters,** she now **gives birth to sons,** or if she had previously given birth to **black** children, considered to be unattractive, she now **gives birth to fair** children, or if she had previously given birth to **short,** weak children, she **gives birth to tall,** strong children, **what can be said?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא: ״וְנִקְּתָה וְנִזְרְעָה זָרַע״ — מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאִם הָיְתָה עֲקָרָה — נִפְקֶדֶת, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל. אָמַר לֵיהּ רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא: אִם כֵּן, יֵלְכוּ כׇּל הָעֲקָרוֹת כּוּלָּן וְיִסְתַּתְּרוּ, וְזוֹ שֶׁלֹּא קִלְקְלָה — נִפְקֶדֶת. אֶלָּא מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאִם הָיְתָה יוֹלֶדֶת בְּצַעַר — יוֹלֶדֶת בְּרֶיוַח, קְצָרִים — יוֹלֶדֶת אֲרוּכִּים, שְׁחוֹרִים — יוֹלֶדֶת לְבָנִים, אֶחָד — יוֹלֶדֶת שְׁנַיִם.
> **Translation:** **As it was taught** in a *baraita* that the *tanna’im* disputed the interpretation of the verse in Numbers: **“Then she shall be acquitted and she shall conceive” teaches that if she was barren, she will be remembered** by God and granted children; this is **the statement of Rabbi Yishmael. Rabbi Akiva said to him: If so, all barren women will go and seclude themselves** with men who are not their husbands, **and any** woman **who did not** commit the **sin** of adultery **will be remembered** by God and granted children. **Rather,** the verse **teaches** that this is merely a promise for greater ease in childbirth; **if she has** previously **given birth with pain,** she now **gives birth with ease,** if she has previously given birth to **short** children, she **gives birth to tall** children, if she has previously given birth to **black** children, she now **gives birth to fair** children, if she has previously given birth to **one** child, she now **gives birth to two** children.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״אִם רָאֹה תִרְאֶה״ — דִּבְּרָה תּוֹרָה כִּלְשׁוֹן בְּנֵי אָדָם.
> **Translation:** According to Rabbi Akiva’s explanation, **what** is derived from the double language uttered by Hannah: ***Im ra’o tireh*? The Torah spoke in the language of men,** meaning that this double language is not extraordinary and nothing may be derived from it. It is common biblical vernacular.

A sharp observation arises from the text of the *mishna* itself, where God is referred to as *avinu shebashamayim*, our Father in Heaven — likely the first instance in the *mishna* of this formulation. Yet the *gemara*'s derivations consistently emphasize distance, awe, and trembling before the divine King. This is the precise tension of *avinu malkenu*, which will be given expression so powerfully on the *yamim nora'im*: the closeness and intimacy of a parent alongside the deference and distance appropriate before a sovereign. One who is both prince and the king's child knows this double relationship well — there is still a certain submission because the father is, after all, the king. The discussion here does lean more heavily on the *malkhenu* dimension, on distance and awe, and it is worth noting that tension explicitly.

## Joy with Awe



### Awe Tempers Joy

The *gemara* now turns to the second half of the same verse: *vegilu bir'adah* — rejoice in trembling. What is the meaning of this paradoxical pairing? Rav Adda bar Matna said in the name of Rabbah: *b'makom gilah sham tehei ra'adah* — in the very place of rejoicing, there should be trembling. Tosafot notes that the plain sense of the verse may refer to the future: when the day of trembling, the *yom hadin*, arrives, those who have served God faithfully will rejoice. But the *gemara* here reads the verse as integrating both modes simultaneously in the present service of God — a joy in the closeness and intimacy with God, alongside the awe and trembling of standing in God's presence. These are not in conflict but are to be held together.

### Abaye's Rebuke

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״בָּעֳנִי אֲמָתֶךָ״. ״אַל תִשְׁכַּח אֶת אֲמָתֶךָ״. ״וְנָתַתָּה לַאֲמָתְךָ״.
> **Translation:** In the oath/prayer uttered by Hannah, she refers to herself as “Your servant” [*amatekha*] three times: **“The affliction of Your maidservant…**and **not forget Your maidservant and will give Your maidservant”** (I Samuel 1:11).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בְּרַבִּי חֲנִינָא: שָׁלֹשׁ אֲמָתוֹת הַלָּלוּ לָמָּה? — אָמְרָה חַנָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, שְׁלֹשָׁה בִּדְקֵי מִיתָה בָּרָאתָ בָּאִשָּׁה, וְאָמְרִי לַהּ: שְׁלֹשָׁה דִּבְקֵי מִיתָה. וְאֵלּוּ הֵן: נִדָּה, וְחַלָּה וְהַדְלָקַת הַנֵּר. כְּלוּם עָבַרְתִּי עַל אַחַת מֵהֶן?! —
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yosei, son of Rabbi Ḥanina, said: Why** are **these three maidservants [*amatot*]** cited in the verse? They are cited to teach that **Hannah said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, You have created three crucibles** potentially leading to **death in a woman,** where she is particularly vulnerable. Alternatively, **some say:** Master of the Universe, You have created **three accelerants of death** in a woman. **They are** mitzvot that, as a rule, pertain to women: Observing the *halakhot* of **a menstruating woman,** separating ***ḥalla*** from dough, **and lighting** Shabbat **candles. Have I ever violated one of them?** Hannah attests to her status as God’s maidservant [ama]. The reference to these three mitzvot is drawn from the etymological similarity between *amatekha*, your maidservant, and *mita*, death.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְנָתַתָּ לַאֲמָתְךָ זֶרַע אֲנָשִׁים״.
> **Translation:** Later in her prayer, Hannah says: **“And You will grant Your servant an offspring of men.”**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״זֶרַע אֲנָשִׁים״? אָמַר רַב: גַּבְרָא בְּגוּבְרִין. וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: זֶרַע שֶׁמּוֹשֵׁחַ שְׁנֵי אֲנָשִׁים, וּמַאן אִינּוּן — שָׁאוּל וְדָוִד. וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: זֶרַע שֶׁשָּׁקוּל כִּשְׁנֵי אֲנָשִׁים, וּמַאן אִינּוּן — מֹשֶׁה וְאַהֲרֹן. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״מֹשֶׁה וְאַהֲרֹן בְּכֹהֲנָיו וּשְׁמוּאֵל בְּקוֹרְאֵי שְׁמוֹ״. וְרַבָּנַן אָמְרִי: ״זֶרַע אֲנָשִׁים״ — זֶרַע שֶׁמּוּבְלָע בֵּין אֲנָשִׁים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is** the meaning of **“an offspring of men”? Rav said:** Hannah prayed for **a man among men,** a son who would be outstanding and exceptional. **And Shmuel said:** This expression means **an offspring who will anoint two men** to royalty. **And who were they? Saul and David. And Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** Hannah prayed that she would bear **an offspring who** would be **the equivalent of two** of the world’s greatest **men. And who were they? Moses and Aaron. As it is stated: “Moses and Aaron among His priests, and Samuel among those who call His name”** (Psalms 99:6). In this verse, Hannah’s son, Samuel, is equated to Moses and Aaron. **And the Rabbis say: “An offspring of men”:** Hannah prayed for **an offspring who would be inconspicuous among men,** that he would not stand out in any way.

Abaye was once sitting before Rabbah and observed that Rabbah was laughing rather heartily — *kahava ka-badach tuva*. This was not during *davening* but apparently in the context of learning. Abaye, though Rabbah's student, offered a pointed remark: *vegilu bir'adah ksiv* — it is written, rejoice in trembling. The implication is that even outside of prayer, in the general conduct of *avodas Hashem*, excessive levity is out of place. Rabbah responded: *ana tfillin manachna* — I am wearing *tefillin*.

### Tefillin Reminder

Rashi explains: the *tefillin* are testimony — *eidus she-memsheles koni u-maruso alai* — that the sovereignty of one's Creator is upon one. Wearing *tefillin* keeps one perpetually in the context of awe. Even in the midst of joy and laughter, the *ra'adah* is maintained because God's presence is constantly signaled by the *tefillin*. Rabbah can laugh at a good moment and maintain the *gilah*, precisely because the *ra'adah* is never absent — it is kept present through the *tefillin* on his arm and head.

### Anguish Model

A parallel incident: Rabbi Yirmiyah was sitting before Rabbi Zeira, and Rabbi Zeira observed that Rabbi Yirmiyah was laughing excessively. Rabbi Zeira rebuked him with the verse *b'chol etzev yiheyeh motar* — with all anguish there shall be abundance — suggesting that a posture of anguish is fitting for one serving God. Rabbi Yirmiyah gave the identical response: *ana tfillin manachna*, I am wearing *tefillin*. The *tefillin*, in this reading, supply not merely the sense of divine sovereignty but also a consciousness of seriousness that extends throughout the day. This raises a broader question: is there an expectation of an ongoing consciousness of God at all times? It seems that at a minimum, one should not lose oneself in excessive levity. For those who do not wear *tefillin* throughout the day, one might suggest that the *kippah* serves a corresponding function as a constant reminder of one's standing before God — though the *gemara* itself does not say this.

### Breaking Glass

Mar, the son of Ravina, made a wedding feast for his son. He observed the *rabbanim* having an excessively good time, laughing and jesting. He brought out a fine goblet worth four hundred *zuzei* and shattered it before them, and they became sorrowful. The point was made: even at one's greatest celebration, a consciousness of seriousness must be maintained.

### Rav Ashi's Practice

Rav Ashi similarly made a wedding feast for his son, observed the *rabbanim* laughing too much, brought out a white glass goblet of great value, and broke it before them, bringing them back to a state of sobriety. Tosafot draws the explicit line: *mikan nahagu leshaber zechuchit benisuin* — from here derives the custom of breaking a glass at a wedding. Notably, this derivation is distinct from the more commonly cited reason of *zecher lechurban*. Here it is not primarily about mourning the destruction of the Temple; it is about the general principle that even in the place of greatest joy, one must not allow oneself to become untethered from the consciousness of standing before God. The two rationales reflect different dimensions of the same underlying concern.

### Mortality Song

At the wedding feast of Mar the son of Ravina, the *rabbanim* asked Rav Hamnuna Zuti to sing for them. He sang: *vai lan demitnan, vai lan demitnan* — woe to us that we will die, woe to us that we will die. When asked for the refrain, he replied: *hei Torah vehei mitzvah demagnu lan* — where is the Torah and where is the *mitzvah* that will protect us? The message conveyed to those assembled: life is short, and its purpose is the service of God through Torah and *mitzvot*. Joy at a wedding is not to be replaced by despair, but it must be tempered by the awareness that *adam le'amal yulad* — a person is born to toil in divine service.

It is worth noting the observations prompted by this passage. The *rabbanim* in question are from the *amora'im* of Bavel, who in many respects were materially comfortable and socially stable. One could argue that precisely when life is going well — when the *rabbanim* of Bavel enjoy relative prosperity and security — the temptation to forget one's seriousness of purpose is greatest. The pointed rejoinder is not that joy itself is wrong, but that one must not lose the thread of purposeful living. The *gemara* here is not demanding constant anguish; it is insisting that even in joy, life must remain framed by the consciousness of one's mission.

What makes this particularly striking is the identity of those summoned to sing. It is the *rabbanim* themselves who call upon Rav Hamnuna Zuti — they are not passive recipients of a rebuke from outside but are the very ones who invite the reminder. There is something telling in that arrangement: the greatest scholars, gathered at a moment of celebration, recognize their own susceptibility to being swept away by the festivities, and so they actively solicit the corrective. The request for a song becomes, in effect, a request to be recalled to seriousness. This is not an external imposition of sobriety but a self-aware act on the part of people who understand that awareness of mortality and purpose must be kept close precisely when joy might otherwise crowd it out — a principle the *gemara* continues to press in its next ruling on laughter itself.

### No Excessive Laughter

Rabbi Yochanan in the name of Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai states — and this ruling is cited in the *Shulchan Aruch* — *assur leadam shimalay sechok piv ba'olam hazeh*, it is forbidden for a person to fill his mouth with laughter in this world. The proof text: *az yimalay sechok pinu uleshonenu rinah* — then our mouths will be filled with laughter and our tongues with song — meaning only at the time of ultimate redemption, when the nations will say that God has done greatly for Israel. Excessive laughter belongs to that future redeemed state; in the present, it is out of place.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי אֲתָא רַב דִּימִי, אֲמַר לֹא אָרוֹךְ וְלֹא גּוּץ, וְלֹא קָטָן וְלֹא אַלָּם, וְלֹא צָחוֹר וְלֹא גִּיחוֹר, וְלֹא חָכָם וְלֹא טִפֵּשׁ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara relates: **When Rav Dimi came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia, **he said** in explanation: Hannah prayed that her son would not be conspicuous among men; **neither too tall nor too short; neither too small nor too fat; neither too white nor too red; neither too smart nor too stupid.**

### Reish Lakish Stringency


It is further related that Reish Lakish, from the moment he heard this teaching from Rabbi Yochanan, took great care never to fill his mouth with laughter. Beforehand, he had been able to do so freely; after hearing the *halacha*, he maintained this stringency for the rest of his life. It is striking that both Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish are from the *amora'im* of Eretz Yisrael, who had considerably less reason to be reminded of the fragility of existence and the weight of *galut* than their counterparts in Bavel. The *gemara* here is not demanding that one go through life miserable. What it is demanding — and what *Shulchan Aruch* begins with in a different key, *shivisi Hashem lenegdi tamid* — is a constant consciousness of standing before God, which naturally limits the degree to which one can abandon oneself to unrestrained levity.

## Prayer Mindset



### Not During Judgment

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אֲנִי הָאִשָּׁה הַנִּצֶּבֶת עִמְּכָה בָּזֶה״, אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ בֶּן לֵוִי: מִכָּאן שֶׁאָסוּר לֵישֵׁב בְּתוֹךְ אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת שֶׁל תְּפִלָּה.
> **Translation:** When Hannah came to the Temple with her son Samuel, she told Eli: “My lord, as your soul lives, my lord, **I am the woman who stood here with you** to pray to the Lord” (I Samuel 1:26). **Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi said: From here** the *halakha* **that it is forbidden to sit within four cubits of** one who is **praying** is derived. As the verse says: “Who stood here with you,” indicating that Eli stood alongside Hannah because she was praying.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אֶל הַנַּעַר הַזֶּה הִתְפַּלָּלְתִּי״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: שְׁמוּאֵל מוֹרֵה הֲלָכָה לִפְנֵי רַבּוֹ הָיָה. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּשְׁחֲטוּ אֶת הַפָּר וַיָּבִיאוּ אֶת הַנַּעַר אֶל עֵלִי״. מִשּׁוּם דְּ״וַיִּשְׁחֲטוּ אֶת הַפָּר״ הֵבִיאוּ הַנַּעַר אֶל עֵלִי?
> **Translation:** Additionally, Hannah’s emphasis in speaking to Eli, **“for this youth I prayed”** (I Samuel 1:27), indicates that she came to protect him from danger. As **Rabbi Elazar said: Samuel was** one who **taught *halakha* in the presence of his teacher.** Hannah wanted to pray that he not be punished by death at the hand of Heaven for his transgression, **as it is stated: “And they slaughtered the cow and they brought the youth to Eli”** (I Samuel 1:25). This verse is puzzling. **Because they slaughtered the cow,** therefore, **they brought the youth to Eli?** What does one have to do with the next?

The *gemara* returns to the state of mind appropriate for *davening*. A *baraita* teaches: *ein omdin lehitpallel lo mitoch din velo mitoch devar halacha ela mitoch halacha pesuka* — one does not stand to pray from being in the middle of judgment, or from being engaged in an active halachic analysis, but only from *halacha pesuka*, a clear and settled ruling. This is distinct from the *mishna*'s emphasis on *koved rosh*, though it serves a related purpose. If one's mind is occupied with adjudicating a *din Torah* or working through a complex halachic question, one's attention is preoccupied — even with something of genuine importance — and that mental state is not the right launching point for *tefillah*.

### Halacha Pesuka

One might ask: what is meant by *halacha pesuka*, and how does it differ from any other learning? The *gemara* provides several concrete examples in response to Abaye's question of *heichi dami halacha pesuka* — what does it look like in practice?

### Niddah Example


The first example, offered by Abaye citing Rabbi Zeira, is the famous ruling at the conclusion of *masechet Niddah*: *benot Yisrael hechmiru al atzman* — Jewish women adopted a stringency upon themselves, such that even if they observe a drop of blood as small as a mustard seed — or smaller — they observe *shivah neki'im*, seven clean days. By Torah law, a woman waits seven days from the onset of bleeding and, if bleeding ceases before the end of the seventh day, goes to the *mikvah*. The status of *zavah* requiring seven clean days applies only if bleeding is observed on three consecutive days during the *zavah* period. The accepted practice, however, is to treat even a single drop of blood as conferring *zavah* status requiring *shivah neki'im*. This settled ruling is an example of *halacha pesuka*.

### Grain Tithe Example

Rava offers a second example, citing the teaching of Rav Hoshaia: *ma'arim adam al tevo'aso umakhnisah bemotz shellah kedei shetehei behemto ochelet upeturah min hama'aser* — a person may arrange his grain cleverly by bringing it into the storage area while still in its chaff, so that his animal may eat from it and be exempt from *ma'aser*. The background requires unpacking. Grain ordinarily becomes obligated in *ma'aser* — *hukva lema'aser* — upon the completion of *miruach*, the smoothing of the threshed and winnowed pile, followed by bringing it into storage. Before that point, one may eat from it in a non-fixed manner (*achilas arai*), including feeding it to animals, without taking *terumos u'ma'asros*. If one reverses the order — bringing the grain into storage before *miruach* and performing *miruach* inside — the grain technically never becomes fully obligated, and the entire pile may be used for animal feed (or eaten in a non-fixed manner), without separating *terumos u'ma'asros* at all. That is the ruling.

### Sacrifice Blood Example

A third example, cited by Rav Huna in the name of Rav Zevid: *hamakiz dam bivehemat kodashim assur behanah u'mo'alin bo* — if one lets blood from a sanctified animal, that blood is forbidden for benefit, and using it constitutes *me'ilah*. The general principle is that the blood designated to be thrown on the *mizbeiach* is exempt from *me'ilah*, as the verse states *ani nesativ lachem al hamizbeiach lechaper* — I have given it to you for atonement — implying that the blood is given over for that purpose and not subject to *me'ilah*. However, if blood is drawn from a sanctified animal for a medical reason — to treat the animal — that blood is not being used for the *mizbeiach* and therefore falls under *me'ilah*. Deriving benefit from it is forbidden, and doing so requires a *korban me'ilah*. That too is an example of *halacha pesuka*.

### Two Approaches

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, אָמַר לָהֶן עֵלִי: קִרְאוּ כֹּהֵן, לֵיתֵי וְלִשְׁחוֹט. חֲזַנְהוּ שְׁמוּאֵל דַּהֲווֹ מְהַדְּרִי בָּתַר כֹּהֵן לְמִישְׁחַט, אֲמַר לְהוּ: לְמָה לְכוּ לְאַהְדּוֹרֵי בָּתַר כֹּהֵן לְמִישְׁחַט? שְׁחִיטָה בְּזָר כְּשֵׁרָה! אַיְיתוּהוּ לְקַמֵּיהּ דְּעֵלִי. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מְנָא לָךְ הָא? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מִי כְּתִיב ״וְשָׁחַט הַכֹּהֵן״?! ״וְהִקְרִיבוּ הַכֹּהֲנִים״ כְּתִיב, מִקַּבָּלָה וְאֵילָךְ מִצְוַת כְּהוּנָּה, מִכָּאן לַשְּׁחִיטָה שֶׁכְּשֵׁרָה בְּזָר.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** this is what happened: **Eli said to** those who brought the offering: **Call a priest; he will come and slaughter** the offering. **Samuel saw them looking for a priest to slaughter** the animal. **He said to them: Why do you** need **to look for a priest to slaughter** it? **Slaughter** of an offering performed **by a non-priest is valid. They brought him before Eli** to clarify his statement. Eli **said to him: How do you know this?** Samuel **said to him: Is it written** in the Torah: **And the priest shall slaughter** indicating that the offering may only be slaughtered by a priest? **It is written: “And the priests shall offer,”** only **from** the stage of **receiving** the blood in the bowls **and onward is it a mitzva** incumbent upon **priests** alone. **From here** the *halakha* that **slaughter by a non-priest is acceptable** is derived.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מֵימָר שַׁפִּיר קָא אָמְרַתְּ, מִיהוּ מוֹרֶה הֲלָכָה בִּפְנֵי רַבָּךְ אַתְּ, וְכׇל הַמּוֹרֶה הֲלָכָה בִּפְנֵי רַבּוֹ חַיָּיב מִיתָה. אָתְיָא חַנָּה וְקָא צָוְוחָה קַמֵּיהּ: ״אֲנִי הָאִשָּׁה הַנִּצֶּבֶת עִמְּכָה בָּזֶה וְגוֹ׳״. אֲמַר לַהּ: שִׁבְקִי לִי דְּאֶעֶנְשֵׁיהּ, וּבְעֵינָא רַחֲמֵי, וְיָהֵיב לָךְ רַבָּא מִינֵּיהּ. אֲמַרָה לֵיהּ: ״אֶל הַנַּעַר הַזֶּה הִתְפַּלָּלְתִּי״.
> **Translation:** Eli **said to** Samuel: **You have spoken well** and your statement is correct, but **nevertheless, you are** one who **issued a halakhic ruling in the presence of your teacher, and anyone who issues a halakhic ruling in the presence of his teacher,** even if the particular *halakha* is correct, **is liable for death** at the hand of Heaven for showing contempt for his teacher. **Hannah came and shouted before him: “I am the woman who stood here with you** to pray to the Lord;” do not punish the child who was born of my prayers. **He said to her: Let me punish him, and I will pray** for mercy, that the Holy One, Blessed be He, **will grant you** a son who will be **greater than** this one. **She said to him: “For this youth I prayed”** and I want no other.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְחַנָּה הִיא מְדַבֶּרֶת עַל לִבָּהּ״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בֶּן זִמְרָא: עַל עִסְקֵי לִבָּהּ. אָמְרָה לְפָנָיו: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, כׇּל מַה שֶּׁבָּרָאתָ בָּאִשָּׁה, לֹא בָּרָאתָ דָּבָר אֶחָד לְבַטָּלָה: עֵינַיִם לִרְאוֹת, וְאׇזְנַיִם לִשְׁמוֹעַ, חוֹטֶם לְהָרִיחַ, פֶּה לְדַבֵּר, יָדַיִם לַעֲשׂוֹת בָּהֶם מְלָאכָה, רַגְלַיִם לְהַלֵּךְ בָּהֶן, דַּדִּים לְהָנִיק בָּהֶן. דַּדִּים הַלָּלוּ שֶׁנָּתַתָּ עַל לִבִּי לָמָּה? לֹא לְהָנִיק בָּהֶן?! תֵּן לִי בֵּן, וְאָנִיק בָּהֶן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara continues to deal with Hannah’s prayer. It is said: **“And Hannah spoke on her heart.”** Several interpretations are offered to explain her use of the phrase “on her heart” instead of the common phrase to her heart (Maharsha). **Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Yosei ben Zimra:** Hannah spoke to God **concerning matters of her heart. She said before Him: Master of the Universe,** of **all** the organs **You created in a woman, You have not created one in vain.** Every organ fulfills its purpose; **eyes to see, ears to hear, a nose to smell, a mouth to speak, hands with which to perform labor, feet with which to walk, breasts with which to nurse.** If so, **these breasts that You placed upon my heart, to what** purpose did You place them? Was it **not** in order **to nurse with them? Grant me a son and I will nurse with them.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בֶּן זִמְרָא: כׇּל הַיּוֹשֵׁב בְּתַעֲנִית בְּשַׁבָּת קוֹרְעִים לוֹ גְּזַר דִּינוֹ שֶׁל שִׁבְעִים שָׁנָה. וְאַף עַל פִּי כֵן חוֹזְרִין וְנִפְרָעִין מִמֶּנּוּ דִּין עוֹנֶג שַׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** Tangentially, the Gemara also cites an additional statement that **Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Yosei ben Zimra: Anyone who sits** in observance of a **fast on Shabbat,** his merit is great and **they tear up** and repeal **his sentence of seventy years;** because everyone is enjoying himself and a feast is prepared, it is more difficult to fast on Shabbat than on any other day. **Nevertheless, they then hold him accountable** for failing to fulfill **the *halakha* of delight of Shabbat**.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי תַּקַּנְתֵּיהּ? אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: לִיתֵּיב תַּעֲנִיתָא לְתַעֲנִיתָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is his remedy** to atone and avoid punishment? **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said: He must sit** in observance of another **fast** on a weekday to atone **for the fast** on Shabbat.

Having established the content of *halacha pesuka*, the *gemara* notes: *rabbanan avdi kemasnishin* — the students of the *yeshiva* acted in accordance with the *mishna*, focusing on *koved rosh*, seriousness and awe. *Rav Ashi avid ke-braisa* — Rav Ashi acted in accordance with the *baraita*, standing to *daven* from *halacha pesuka*. The fact that some followed the *mishna* and others the *baraita* suggests these are not mutually exclusive alternatives or competing rulings, but rather different paths to achieving the same disposition. It is worth pausing to note that the *gemara* takes a brief digression at this point — introduced by the mention of Rabbi Elazar in the name of Rabbi Yosei bin Zimra — touching on the case of one who fasts on *Shabbos* due to a *ta'anit chalom*, a disturbing dream that requires immediate fasting even on *Shabbos*. The *gemara* observes that such a fast, though difficult precisely because everyone else is engaged in the pleasure of *oneg Shabbos*, has the power to annul even a severe heavenly decree — and *Rashi* explains that the decree may have stood since one's youth. Yet because one has thereby neglected *oneg Shabbos*, there remains a residual problem, and the remedy offered by Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak is to fast again in response to having fasted — a fast for the fast. The *gemara* then returns from this digression to the primary discussion of *tefillah* and its models, which, as the structure of this *perek* makes clear, is centrally concerned with *kavana* and the figures — *Chana* foremost among them — who serve as models of how one ought to pray.

### Framing Service


The question arises: why are the examples of *halacha pesuka* so complex? These are not simple rulings like "one must pray three times a day." One could speculate that for the *rabbanim* spending their days in serious study, something too elementary does not engage the mind in the same way. A ruling with a degree of complexity — but one that resolves clearly into a firm bottom line — may be precisely what focuses the *talmid chacham* before prayer.

But there may be a deeper point as well. *Halacha pesuka* is not merely a psychological focusing device. It may represent a positive framing of what *davening* is. To engage with *halacha* immediately before *tefillah* is to make a statement that one's life is directed by God's law, that the details of one's conduct are governed by divine concern. That framing — of a life lived under halachic discipline and purpose — is precisely the right disposition from which to approach God in prayer. It is not merely that one's mind is focused; it is that one presents oneself before God as someone engaged in purposeful, God-directed service. One student's formulation captures this well: when the connection between *halacha* study and *tefillah* is made explicit, one's learning becomes integrated into one's sense of divine service rather than remaining a separate intellectual exercise. The *tefillah* is then not an interruption of learning but its natural culmination.

It is worth noting a contemporary parallel: in the *siddur*, after *birkat haTorah*, there is the custom of reciting a passage of Torah. The prayer most commonly associated with preparation for *shmoneh esrei* at *mincha* — where the structure allows for something to be inserted before *shmoneh esrei*, unlike *shacharis* and *ma'ariv* which are fixed around *geulah* and *tefillah* — is *ashrei*. *Ashrei yoshvei veitekha* is perhaps more an embodiment of the spirit of the *mishna*'s *koved rosh* than of *halacha pesuka* strictly speaking, but the practice points to the same underlying concern: one does not simply walk into *shmoneh esrei* cold.

## Forbidden States



### Negative States List

The *baraita* continues with an expanded list: *ein omdin lehitpallel lo mitoch atzvut* — one does not stand to *daven* from sadness, *velo mitoch atzlut* — nor from laziness, *velo mitoch sechok* — nor from having just been engaged in excessive laughter, *velo mitoch sicha* — nor from idle conversation, *velo mitoch kalut rosh* — nor from levity, *velo mitoch dvarim betelim* — nor from frivolous talk. Many of these overlap with what came before; together they paint a picture of the mental states that are incompatible with *tefillah*.

### Simcha Shel Mitzvah

The positive counterpart: *ela mitoch simcha shel mitzvah* — one should stand from a joy of *mitzvah*. This is striking, and seemingly in some tension with the emphasis on awe, trembling, and *koved rosh* that has dominated the discussion until now. The *baraita* here introduces a different register: not awe and weight, but joy.

### Rashi's Reading

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: חַנָּה הֵטִיחָה דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״וְתִתְפַּלֵּל עַל ה׳״, מְלַמֵּד שֶׁהֵטִיחָה דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה.
> **Translation:** After explaining the uncommon expression, on her heart, the Gemara cites an additional statement in the matter of Hannah. **And Rabbi Elazar said: Hannah spoke impertinently toward** God **on High. As it is stated: “And she prayed onto the Lord,”** as opposed to the common phrase: To the Lord. This **teaches that she spoke impertinently toward on High.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אֵלִיָּהוּ הֵטִיחַ דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְאַתָּה הֲסִבֹּתָ אֶת לִבָּם אֲחֹרַנִּית״. אָמַר רַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר רַבִּי יִצְחָק: מִנַּיִן שֶׁחָזַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא וְהוֹדָה לוֹ לְאֵלִיָּהוּ,
> **Translation:** **And** on a similar note, **Rabbi Elazar said** that **Elijah spoke impertinently toward** God **on High** as well in his prayer at Mount Carmel, **as it is stated:** “Answer me, Lord, answer me, that this people will know that You are the Lord, God, **and You have turned their hearts backward”** (I Kings 18:37), claiming that God caused Israel to sin. On this topic, **Rabbi Shmuel bar Rabbi Yitzḥak said: From where do we know that the Holy One, Blessed be He, ultimately conceded to Elijah** that he was correct?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּכְתִיב: ״וַאֲשֶׁר הֲרֵעֹתִי״.
> **Translation:** **As it is written** in a future prophecy: “In that day, says the Lord, I will assemble the lame, and I will gather those who are abandoned and **those with whom I have dealt in wickedness”** (Micah 4:6). God states that He caused Israel to act wickedly.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חָמָא בְּרַבִּי חֲנִינָא: אִלְמָלֵא שָׁלֹשׁ מִקְרָאוֹת הַלָּלוּ נִתְמוֹטְטוּ רַגְלֵיהֶם שֶׁל שׂוֹנְאֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל.
> **Translation:** Similarly, **Rabbi Ḥama, son of Rabbi Ḥanina, said: Had it not been for these three verses, the legs of the enemies of Israel,** a euphemism for Israel itself, **would have collapsed, as** Israel would have been unable to withstand God’s judgment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חַד דִּכְתִיב: ״וַאֲשֶׁר הֲרֵעֹתִי״, וְחַד דִּכְתִיב: ״הִנֵּה כַחֹמֶר בְּיַד הַיּוֹצֵר כֵּן אַתֶּם בְּיָדִי בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל״, וְחַד דִּכְתִיב: ״וַהֲסִרֹתִי אֶת לֵב הָאֶבֶן מִבְּשַׂרְכֶם וְנָתַתִּי לָכֶם לֵב בָּשָׂר״.
> **Translation:** **One** is the verse just mentioned in which **it is written: “Those whom I have dealt in wickedness.” And one** is the verse in which **it is written: “Behold, like clay in the potter’s hand, so are you in My hand, house of Israel”** (Jeremiah 18:6). **And one** is the verse in which **it is written:** “And I will give you a new heart and a new spirit I will place within you, **and I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and I will give you a heart of flesh”** (Ezekiel 36:26). These three verses indicate that God influences a person’s decisions, and therefore one does not have sole responsibility for his actions.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב פָּפָּא אָמַר מֵהָכָא: ״וְאֶת רוּחִי אֶתֵּן בְּקִרְבְּכֶם וְעָשִׂיתִי אֵת אֲשֶׁר בְּחֻקַּי תֵּלֵכוּ״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa said** there is a clearer proof **from here: “And I will place My spirit within you and I will cause you to walk in My statutes,** and you will observe My decrees and do them” (Ezekiel 36:27).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מֹשֶׁה הֵטִיחַ דְּבָרִים כְּלַפֵּי מַעְלָה. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּתְפַּלֵּל מֹשֶׁה אֶל ה׳״ אַל תִּקְרֵי ״אֶל ה׳״, אֶלָּא ״עַל ה׳״.
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Elazar said: Moses** also **spoke impertinently toward** God **on High, as it is stated** in the verse following the sin of those who murmured against God in the desert: **“And Moses prayed to the Lord** and the fire subsided” (Numbers 11:2), and this verse is interpreted homiletically: **Do not read to [*el*] the Lord,** but rather **onto [*al*] the Lord,** which indicates that he spoke impertinently.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁכֵּן דְּבֵי רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר בֶּן יַעֲקֹב קוֹרִין לָאַלְפִין עַיְינִין, וְלָעַיְינִין אַלְפִין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the basis for this interpretation: **As** the Sages **of the school of Rabbi Eliezer ben Ya’akov would** indiscriminately **read *alef* as *ayin* and *ayin* as *alef*** and in this case transforming *el* into *al*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי אָמְרִי, מֵהָכָא: ״וְדִי זָהָב״.
> **Translation:** The Sages of **the school of Rabbi Yannai,** however, **say** proof that Moses spoke impertinently toward God on High is derived **from here,** Moses’ rebuke at the beginning of Deuteronomy: **“And Di Zahav”** (Deuteronomy 1:1). This is an entry in a list of places where Moses had spoken to Israel. As there was no place encountered by that name, it is interpreted as an allusion to another matter.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״וְדִי זָהָב״? אָמְרִי דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי: כָּךְ אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, בִּשְׁבִיל כֶּסֶף וְזָהָב שֶׁהִשְׁפַּעְתָּ לָהֶם לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, עַד שֶׁאָמְרוּ ״דַּי״ — הוּא גָּרַם שֶׁעָשׂוּ אֶת הָעֵגֶל.
> **Translation:** We must clarify: **What is** the meaning of **and Di Zahav?** The Sages of **the school of Rabbi Yannai said that Moses said the following before the Holy One, Blessed be He,** to atone for Israel after the sin of the Golden Calf: **Master of the Universe, because of the gold and silver that you lavished upon Israel** during the exodus from Egypt **until they said enough [*dai*];** it was this wealth **that caused** Israel **to make the** Golden **Calf.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרִי דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי: אֵין אֲרִי נוֹהֵם מִתּוֹךְ קוּפָּה שֶׁל תֶּבֶן אֶלָּא מִתּוֹךְ קוּפָּה שֶׁל בָּשָׂר.
> **Translation:** Establishing a general moral principle, the Sages **the school of Rabbi Yannai said: A lion does not roar** standing **over a basket of straw** from which he derives no pleasure, **but** he roars standing **over a basket of meat,** as he only roars when satiated.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אוֹשַׁעְיָא: מָשָׁל לְאָדָם שֶׁהָיְתָה לוֹ פָּרָה כְּחוּשָׁה וּבַעֲלַת אֵבָרִים, הֶאֱכִילָהּ כַּרְשִׁינִין וְהָיְתָה מְבַעֶטֶת בּוֹ. אָמַר לָהּ: מִי גָּרַם לִיךְ שֶׁתְּהֵא מְבַעֶטֶת בִּי — אֶלָּא כַּרְשִׁינִין שֶׁהֶאֱכַלְתִּיךְ.
> **Translation:** Similarly, **Rabbi Oshaya said:** This is **comparable to a person who had a lean, but large-limbed cow.** At one point, **he fed it lupines,** a choice food, **and** soon thereafter the cow **was kicking him. He said to** the cow: **Who caused you** to begin **kicking me if not the lupines I fed you?** Here, too, the sin was caused by an abundance of good.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חִיָּיא בַּר אַבָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מָשָׁל לְאָדָם אֶחָד שֶׁהָיָה לוֹ בֵּן. הִרְחִיצוֹ וְסָכוֹ, וְהֶאֱכִילוֹ וְהִשְׁקָהוּ, וְתָלָה לוֹ כִּיס עַל צַוָּארוֹ, וְהוֹשִׁיבוֹ עַל פֶּתַח שֶׁל זוֹנוֹת. מַה יַּעֲשֶׂה אוֹתוֹ הַבֵּן שֶׁלֹּא יֶחֱטָא?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara offers another analogy: **Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba said** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** This is **comparable to a person who had a son; he bathed him and anointed him** with oil, **fed him and gave him drink, and hung a purse** of money **around his neck.** Then, he **brought** his son **to the entrance of a brothel. What could the son do to avoid sinning?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב הוּנָא אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: הַיְינוּ דְּאָמְרִי אִינָשֵׁי: מְלֵי כְּרֵסֵיהּ זְנֵי בִּישֵׁי. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״כְּמַרְעִיתָם וַיִּשְׂבָּעוּ שָׂבְעוּ וַיָּרׇם לִבָּם עַל כֵּן שְׁכֵחוּנִי״. רַב נַחְמָן אָמַר: מֵהָכָא: ״וְרָם לְבָבֶךָ וְשָׁכַחְתָּ אֶת ה׳״. וְרַבָּנַן אָמְרִי, מֵהָכָא: ״וְאָכַל וְשָׂבַע וְדָשֵׁן וּפָנָה״.
> **Translation:** On a similar note, **Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Huna, said** that **Rav Sheshet said: That is what people say** in a popular maxim: **Filling his stomach is a type of sin, as it is stated: “When they were fed and became full they were sated, and their hearts were lifted and they have forgotten Me”** (Hosea 13:6). **Rav Naḥman said:** This principle is derived not from the verse in Hosea, but **from here: “And your heart is lifted and you forget the Lord”** (Deuteronomy 8:14). **And the Rabbis say** that this principle is derived **from here: “And they will have eaten and been sated and fattened, and they will turn** to other gods” (Deuteronomy 31:20).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי בָּעֵית אֵימָא מֵהָכָא: ״וַיִּשְׁמַן יְשׁוּרוּן וַיִּבְעָט״. אָמַר רַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר נַחְמָנִי אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹנָתָן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁחָזַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא וְהוֹדָה לוֹ לְמֹשֶׁה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְכֶסֶף הִרְבֵּיתִי לָהֶם וְזָהָב עָשׂוּ לַבָּעַל״.
> **Translation:** **And if you wish, say** instead that it is derived **from here: “And Jeshurun grew fat and kicked”** (Deuteronomy 32:15). **Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani said** that **Rabbi Yonatan said: From where** in the Torah is it derived that **the Holy One, Blessed be He, ultimately conceded to Moses** that the reason for the sin of the Golden Calf was indeed the riches lavished upon Israel? **As it is stated: “And I gave them an abundance of silver and gold, which they used for the Ba’al”** (Hosea 2:10).

Rashi elaborates: *simcha shel mitzvah* means *divrei tanchumim shel Torah*, words of consolation and encouragement from Torah. He offers examples: passages adjacent in the *davening* to redemption themes, such as *ratzon yerei'av ya'aseh*, or *lo yitosh Hashem amo*. The point is that one approaches *tefillah* from a state of joy in one's relationship with God, in the awareness of God's care and presence. *Mitzvah* in this context does not refer to performing an act but to this joyful orientation toward divine closeness. This is a markedly different framing from the *halacha pesuka* of the *baraita* — which may speak more specifically to the *rabbinic* class — and a different framing from the *koved rosh* of the *mishna*. These are not so much competing requirements as they are different valid orientations, available to different people or in different circumstances.

The *gemara* then draws on a striking aggadic tradition to support the legitimacy of speaking — and even pressing — directly to God. *Shekein matzinu beMoshe shehibit al Hashem*: we find this same directness in Moshe himself. The proof-text offered is *shene'emar vayispaleil Moshe el Hashem* — Moshe prayed to God — but the *gemara* reads it through the hermeneutical principle of the school of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov: *shekein debei Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov korin le'alfin ayinin ule'ayinin alfin* — the *aleph* of *el* is read as the *ayin* of *al*, so that the verse yields not "Moshe prayed to God" but "Moshe prayed against God," or over against Him. This is an unusual phonological principle, and the *gemara* acknowledges it is not the strongest proof. It reflects a real dialectal reality in which the guttural *ayin* had merged with *aleph* in certain communities — a phenomenon the *gemara* elsewhere associates specifically with Hellenistic cities where such sounds were lost — and the school of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov exploits that merger to generate an aggadic reading. A stronger source is then brought from the school of Rabbi Yannai, who reads *Vedi Zahav* in the opening of *Devarim* as *dai zahav*, "enough gold," and understands it as Moshe's implicit accusation before God: *kach amar Moshe lifnei Hakadosh Baruch Hu* — *Ribono Shel Olam*, it was *bishvil kesef vezahav shehishpata lahem leYisrael*, because of the excess of silver and gold You poured upon Israel, *ad she'amru dai*, until they said "enough," *hu garam she'asu es ha'eigel* — that is what caused them to make the calf. Moshe, in other words, did not merely petition; he pressed the case back toward its divine source. That this is presented here, in the context of *simcha shel mitzvah* and the proper orientation entering prayer, suggests that genuine relationship with God — the relationship that underlies *simcha shel mitzvah* — includes the boldness of one who stands before someone they truly know.

### Parting from Friends

A parallel principle is offered: *vechen lo yipater adam mechaveiro lo mitoch sicha velo mitoch sechok velo mitoch kalut rosh velo mitoch dvarim betelim, ela mitoch dvar halacha*. When parting from a friend, one should not conclude the encounter with idle talk, laughter, or levity. The parting should involve a *dvar halacha*. The *Hagra* notes that the text is in tension here: we have just spoken of *simcha shel mitzvah* as the positive frame, and now we return to *dvar halacha*. He therefore marks the textual point and suggests making the two consistent — reading *dvar halacha* here as aligned with *simcha shel mitzvah*. Whether or not one accepts that textual emendation, the underlying tension points to the same two orientations that appeared in the context of prayer: the frame of halachic engagement, and the frame of joyful connection to God.

The *gemara* grounds this in a broader observation: *shechen matzinu binevi'im harishonim shesiyimu divreihem bedivrei shevach vetanchumim* — the early prophets, with one or two notable exceptions such as Yirmiyahu, concluded their prophecies on a positive note regarding Israel's relationship with God. *Simcha shel mitzvah* in this context means ending on a note that expresses the joy of that divine relationship, a kind of *berakhah* of closeness with God.

### Devar Torah Custom

The *gemara* provides a practical illustration: Rav Kahana accompanied Rav Shimi bar Ashi all the way from Pumna to Beit Tzanisa in Bavel. Upon arriving, Rav Kahana remarked that the palm trees of Beit Tzanisa must be so ancient they date back to *Adam haRishon*. Rav Shimi responded: *adkartan milta d'Rabbi Yose bar Chanina* — you have reminded me of a teaching of Rabbi Yose bar Chanina. Rabbi Yose bar Chanina had expounded the verse *b'eretz asher lo avar bah ish v'lo yashav adam sham* — a land that no person has passed through and no *adam* has dwelt in — by asking: if no one ever passed through, how could anyone have dwelt? The verse must be using *adam* in the sense of *Adam haRishon*, teaching that any land *Adam haRishon* designated for settlement was settled, and any he did not designate was not — which is why Beit Tzanisa retained its ancient palm trees to this day.

Rav Mordechai similarly accompanied Rav Shimi bar Ashi from Hagronia to Bei Kifi, or some say to Bei Dura. The point in both cases is the custom of accompanying departing guests — a practice with its own halachic context — and of concluding the encounter with words of Torah. The category of *dvar halacha* proves fairly elastic here: what Rav Shimi offers is a piece of rabbinic *aggadah* and textual interpretation, not a practical ruling. This suggests that *dvar halacha* in this context means broadly any substantive Torah teaching. The frame is what matters: that the parting be shaped by Torah rather than by mere small talk.

What is striking about these stories as models of prayer is how assertive they are. The *gemara* has been presenting, in a relatively short span of text, a picture of *tefillah* as something closer to a *ta'anah* — an argument, a claim — than to quiet petition. We saw this already with Chana, and we see it again here in the discussion of Moshe interceding over the *chet ha'egel*. The one who prays is not simply deferring; he or she is engaging God, even pressing God. That this appears at the opening of the *perek* as a governing model for *tefillah* is quite remarkable.

In the world of the *rabbanim*, the natural way to frame a relationship — both in parting and in approaching *tefillah* — is through the medium of Torah. For those outside that world, or in different contexts, *simcha shel mitzvah*, an expression of the joyful relationship with God, serves the same function.

## Kavana Requirement



### Heart to Heaven

The *gemara* now focuses directly on *kavanah*. *Hamispallel tzarich sheyachavein libo lashamayim* — one who prays must direct one's heart to Heaven. *Abba Shaul* frames this as a reciprocal relationship: if one prepares and directs one's heart, God's ears will be attentive. This is the core challenge of *tefillah* as the *gemara* understands it, and it is a formidable one. *Tefillah* is not merely the recitation of words; it is communication, the expression of one's inner state. How does one legislate *kavanah*? The tension between fixed prayer and personal prayer — between the communal structure inherited as *tefillot keneged korbanot* and the deeply interior act of turning one's heart to God — has run through the entire discussion of these chapters, and it is this tension that the present *mishna* now addresses head-on.

### Rabbi Akiva's Practice

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיְדַבֵּר ה׳ אֶל מֹשֶׁה לֶךְ רֵד״. מַאי ״לֶךְ רֵד״? אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר לוֹ הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְמֹשֶׁה: מֹשֶׁה, רֵד מִגְּדוּלָּתְךָ! כְּלוּם נָתַתִּי לְךָ גְּדוּלָּה אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל יִשְׂרָאֵל. וְעַכְשָׁיו יִשְׂרָאֵל חָטְאוּ — אַתָּה לָמָּה לִי? מִיָּד תָּשַׁשׁ כּוֹחוֹ שֶׁל מֹשֶׁה, וְלֹא הָיָה לוֹ כֹּחַ לְדַבֵּר. וְכֵיוָן שֶׁאָמַר ״הֶרֶף מִמֶּנִּי וְאַשְׁמִידֵם״, אָמַר מֹשֶׁה: דָּבָר זֶה תָּלוּי בִּי! מִיָּד עָמַד וְנִתְחַזֵּק בִּתְפִלָּה, וּבִקֵּשׁ רַחֲמִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates upon additional aspects of the sin of the Golden Calf. It is stated: **“And the Lord said to Moses: Go and descend,** for your people whom you have lifted out of the land of Egypt have been corrupted” (Exodus 32:7). **What is** the meaning of **“go and descend”? Rabbi Elazar said: The Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Moses: Moses, descend from your greatness. Isn’t it only for the sake of Israel,** so that you may serve as an emissary, **that I granted you prominence; and now that Israel has sinned, why do I** need **you?** There is no need for an emissary. **Immediately, Moses’ strength waned and he was powerless to speak** in defense of Israel. **And once** God **said** to Moses: **“Leave Me be, that I may destroy them”** (Deuteronomy 9:14), **Moses said** to himself: If God is telling me to let Him be, it must be because **this matter is dependent upon me. Immediately Moses stood and was strengthened in prayer, and asked** that God have **mercy** on the nation of Israel and forgive them for their transgression.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָשָׁל לְמֶלֶךְ שֶׁכָּעַס עַל בְּנוֹ, וְהָיָה מַכֵּהוּ מַכָּה גְּדוֹלָה. וְהָיָה אוֹהֲבוֹ יוֹשֵׁב לְפָנָיו, וּמִתְיָרֵא לוֹמַר לוֹ דָּבָר. אָמַר הַמֶּלֶךְ: אִלְמָלֵא אוֹהֲבִי זֶה שֶׁיּוֹשֵׁב לְפָנַי — הֲרַגְתִּיךָ. אָמַר: דָּבָר זֶה תָּלוּי בִּי. מִיָּד עָמַד וְהִצִּילוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara says: This is **comparable to a king who became angry at his son** who had sinned against him, **and beat him,** administering **a severe beating.** At that moment, **a well-wisher** of the king **was sitting before him** and witnessed the entire event, **and was afraid to say anything to** the king about the excessive beating. Meanwhile, **the king said** to his son: **Were it not for this well-wisher of mine who is sitting before me, I would have killed you.** Upon hearing this, the king’s friend **said** to himself: This is clearly a sign that **this matter,** rescuing the son from the hands of his father, **is dependent upon me. Immediately he stood and rescued him** from the king.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְעַתָּה הַנִּיחָה לִּי וְיִחַר אַפִּי בָהֶם וַאֲכַלֵּם וְאֶעֱשֶׂה אוֹתְךָ לְגוֹי גָּדוֹל וְגוֹ׳״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֲבָהוּ: אִלְמָלֵא מִקְרָא כָּתוּב, אִי אֶפְשָׁר לְאוֹמְרוֹ. מְלַמֵּד שֶׁתְּפָסוֹ מֹשֶׁה לְהַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, כְּאָדָם שֶׁהוּא תּוֹפֵס אֶת חֲבֵירוֹ בְּבִגְדוֹ, וְאָמַר לְפָנָיו: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, אֵין אֲנִי מַנִּיחֲךָ עַד שֶׁתִּמְחוֹל וְתִסְלַח לָהֶם.
> **Translation:** In an additional aspect of the sin of the Golden Calf, God told Moses: **“Now leave Me be, that My wrath will be enraged against them and I will consume them; and I will make of you a great nation”** (Exodus 32:10). Explaining this verse, **Rabbi Abbahu said: Were the verse not written** in this manner, **it would be impossible to utter** it, in deference to God. The phrase: Leave Me be, **teaches that Moses grabbed the Holy One, Blessed be He, as a person who grabs his friend by his garment** would, **and he said before Him: Master of the Universe, I will not leave You be until You forgive and pardon them.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְאֶעֱשֶׂה אוֹתְךָ לְגוֹי גָדוֹל וְגוֹ׳״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, וּמָה כִּסֵּא שֶׁל שָׁלֹשׁ רַגְלַיִם אֵינוֹ יָכוֹל לַעֲמוֹד לְפָנֶיךָ בִּשְׁעַת כַּעְסֶךְ. כִּסֵּא שֶׁל רֶגֶל אֶחָד — עַל אַחַת כַּמָּה וְכַמָּה!
> **Translation:** In the same verse, God promised Moses: **“And I will make of you a great nation.”** What was Moses’ response? **Rabbi Elazar said: Moses said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, if a chair with three legs,** the collective merit of the three forefathers, **is unable to stand before You in Your moment of wrath, all the more so** that **a chair with one leg,** my merit alone, will be unable to withstand your wrath.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֹא עוֹד אֶלָּא שֶׁיֵּשׁ בִּי בּוֹשֶׁת פָּנִים מֵאֲבוֹתַי, עַכְשָׁיו יֹאמְרוּ: רְאוּ פַּרְנָס שֶׁהֶעֱמִיד עֲלֵיהֶם. בִּקֵּשׁ גְּדוּלָּה לְעַצְמוֹ, וְלֹא בִּקֵּשׁ עֲלֵיהֶם רַחֲמִים.
> **Translation:** **Moreover, but I have** a sense of **shame before my forefathers. Now** they will **say: See** this **leader that** God **placed over** Israel. **He requested greatness for himself but did not pray for** God to have **mercy upon them** in their troubled time.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיְחַל מֹשֶׁה אֶת פְּנֵי ה׳״, אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁעָמַד מֹשֶׁה בִּתְפִלָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, עַד שֶׁהֶחֱלָהוּ. וְרָבָא אָמַר: עַד שֶׁהֵפֵר לוֹ נִדְרוֹ. כְּתִיב הָכָא: ״וַיְחַל״, וּכְתִיב הָתָם: ״לֹא יַחֵל דְּבָרוֹ״, וְאָמַר מָר: הוּא אֵינוֹ מֵיחֵל, אֲבָל אֲחֵרִים מְחִלִּין לוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Torah continues: **“And Moses beseeched [*vayḥal*] before the Lord”** (Exodus 32:11). Many interpretations were given for this uncommon term, *vayḥal*: **Rabbi Elazar said:** It **teaches that Moses stood in prayer before the Holy One, Blessed be He, until it made him ill [***heḥelahu***]** from overexertion. **And Rava said:** Moses stood in prayer **until he nullified His vow,** as the term *vayḥal* alludes to nullification of an oath. **Here it is written *vayḥal*, and there** referring to vows, **it is written: “He shall not nullify [*lo yaḥel*] his word”** (Numbers 30:3). **And** with regard to vows, **the Master said: He** who vowed **cannot nullify** his vow, **but others,** the court, **can nullify** his vow **for him.** Here, it is as if Moses nullified the Lord’s vow to destroy Israel.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁמָּסַר עַצְמוֹ לְמִיתָה עֲלֵיהֶם. שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְאִם אַיִן מְחֵנִי נָא מִסִּפְרְךָ״.
> **Translation:** **And Shmuel said:** The term *vayḥal* **teaches that** Moses **gave his life,** from the term *ḥalal*, a dead person, **for** Israel, **as it is stated: “And if not, erase me, please, from Your book”** (Exodus 32:32).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא אָמַר רַב יִצְחָק: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁהֻחֲלָה עֲלֵיהֶם מִדַּת רַחֲמִים.
> **Translation:** **Rava,** also interpreting this verse, **said** that **Rav Yitzḥak said:** The term *vayḥal* **teaches that he caused the Divine Attribute of Mercy to take effect [*heḥela*] upon them.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן אָמְרִי: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם! חוּלִּין הוּא לְּךָ מֵעֲשׂוֹת כַּדָּבָר הַזֶּה.
> **Translation:** **And the Rabbis say** that this term constitutes the essence of Moses’ claim: **It teaches that Moses said before the Holy One Blessed be He: Master of the Universe! It is a sacrilege [*ḥullin*] for You to do something like this.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיְחַל מֹשֶׁה אֶת פְּנֵי ה׳״, תַּנְיָא, רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר הַגָּדוֹל אוֹמֵר: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁעָמַד מֹשֶׁה בִּתְפִלָּה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא עַד שֶׁאֲחָזַתּוּ אֲחִילוּ. מַאי אֲחִילוּ? אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אֵשׁ שֶׁל עֲצָמוֹת. מַאי אֵשׁ שֶׁל עֲצָמוֹת? אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: אִשָּׁתָא דְגַרְמֵי.
> **Translation:** And another interpretation of the verse, **“And Moses beseeched [*vayḥal*] before the Lord.” It was taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi Eliezer the Great says: This** term **teaches that Moses stood in prayer before the Holy One, Blessed be He, until he was overcome by *aḥilu*.** Even the Sages were unfamiliar with this term. Therefore, the Gemara asks: **What is** the meaning of ***aḥilu*? Rabbi Elazar,** an *amora* of Eretz Yisrael, **said** that *aḥilu* is **fire in the bones.** However, this expression was familiar in Eretz Yisrael but not in Babylonia. They asked in Babylonia: **What is** the disease that they called **fire of the bones? Abaye said** that is a disease known in Babylonia as ***eshta degarmei*,** which in Aramaic means **fire of the bones;** in other words, a fever.

The *gemara* cites the practice of Rabbi Akiva: *keshehaya mispalel im hatzibur, haya mekatzeir veoleh mipnei torech tzibur* — when he prayed with the congregation, he would shorten his prayer out of consideration for the burden it places on the *tzibur* to wait. But *keshehaya mispalel beino levein atzmo*, when he prayed in private, one might leave him in one corner of the room and return to find him in the other corner — *mipnei kri'os vehishtachavayos*, because of his extensive bowing and swaying, his physical movement in prayer carrying him bodily across the room. Some scholars have suggested that Rabbi Akiva's mystical and kabbalistic orientation may have contributed to a near-rapturous state during private prayer, beyond simple *shokeling*. Whether or not that is the case, the contrast is unmistakable.

Before moving on, the *gemara* offers an image that powerfully underscores what is at stake in the act of prayer itself. When God tells Moshe *haniicha li* — leave Me alone — *Rabbi Avahu* observes that *almalei mikra katuv i efshar l'omro* — were it not written explicitly in a verse, one could not say such a thing. For the *gemara* reads in this phrase a remarkable implication: the very fact that God says "leave Me" presupposes that Moshe is holding on. *Melamed she'tafas Moshe l'HaKadosh Baruch Hu k'adam she'tofes et chaveiro bivigdo* — it teaches that Moshe seized the Holy One, blessed be He, as a person grabs a companion by the cloak, and declared before Him that he would not let go until God granted forgiveness. It is a striking, almost startling image — Moshe grabbing God by the collar. And Rabbi Elazar adds a further dimension: when God offers to make Moshe into a great nation, Moshe responds that if even a *kisei shel shalosh raglayim* — a three-legged chair, the *zechut avot* of Avraham, Yitzchak, and Yaakov — cannot sustain Israel before God in a moment of divine anger, then a *kisei shel regel achat*, a chair of one leg, resting on Moshe's merit alone, would fare even worse. The point of both images, taken together, is the same: prayer is not passive petition. It is engagement, even contestation — an act of holding on. That Moshe's prayer ultimately saves *klal Yisrael* only deepens the force of the *gemara*'s point, and it sets in sharp relief everything that the contrast between Rabbi Akiva's communal and private prayer is reaching toward.

### Communal vs. Private

The contrast between Rabbi Akiva's communal and private prayer raises a fundamental question. One explanation is straightforward: there is a *bein adam lachaveiro* dimension to communal prayer — one cannot indulge one's own *kavanah* if doing so burdens the community that is waiting. But the *gemara* may be pointing at something deeper: communal prayer and private prayer exist in different registers. In communal prayer, one participates in a collective act, analogous in its way to the *korban tzibur*, and the communal experience takes precedence over the individual's subjective intensity. In private prayer, by contrast, the full weight of personal *kavanah* can be given free expression.

This surfaces a question that is genuinely unresolved in halachic literature: should one prefer praying with a *minyan* at the cost of some *kavanah*, or praying in private with fuller attention? The *poskim* generally favor communal prayer, emphasizing the external structure of *tefillah betzibur* — in part because *halacha* is better equipped to address and evaluate external forms than internal states, which cannot be quantified or measured. Yet the *gemara*'s presentation of Rabbi Akiva suggests that the private, interior dimension of *tefillah* is equally legitimate and in some respects more demanding. One might also add that the prohibition against audibly vocalizing one's prayer in a way that could distract others in the congregation points to the same tension: when in the *tzibur*, one reins in the more expressive dimensions of prayer for the sake of the communal experience. When praying *beyechidus*, no such restraint is needed.

## Tanakh Models



### Windows in Prayer

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״זְכֹר לְאַבְרָהָם לְיִצְחָק וּלְיִשְׂרָאֵל עֲבָדֶיךָ אֲשֶׁר נִשְׁבַּעְתָּ לָהֶם בָּךְ״. מַאי ״בָּךְ״? אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, אִלְמָלֵא נִשְׁבַּעְתָּ לָהֶם בַּשָּׁמַיִם וּבָאָרֶץ, הָיִיתִי אוֹמֵר: כְּשֵׁם שֶׁשָּׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ בְּטֵלִים — כָּךְ שְׁבוּעָתְךָ בְּטֵלָה. וְעַכְשָׁו שֶׁנִּשְׁבַּעְתָּ לָהֶם בְּשִׁמְךָ הַגָּדוֹל, מָה שִׁמְךָ הַגָּדוֹל חַי וְקַיָּים לְעוֹלָם וּלְעוֹלְמֵי עוֹלָמִים — כָּךְ שְׁבוּעָתְךָ קַיֶּימֶת לְעוֹלָם וּלְעוֹלְמֵי עוֹלָמִים.
> **Translation:** As Moses continues his prayer, he says: **“Remember Abraham, Isaac and Israel Your servants, to whom You swore in Your name”** (Exodus 32:13). **What is** the meaning of **in Your name? Rabbi Elazar said: Moses said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, had You sworn to them by the heavens and the earth, I would say: Just as the heavens and the earth will** ultimately **be no more, so too Your oath will be null and void. Now that You swore to them by Your great name, just as Your name lives and stands for all eternity, so too does Your oath live and stand for all eternity.**

Rabbi Abba teaches: *le'olam yitpallel adam bevayit sheyesh bo chalonot* — a person should always pray in a house that has windows. The proof is from Daniel, who prayed opposite open windows. There is something to be said for this experientially: the sense of connectedness to the natural world — to sky, light, and the broader creation — can open the person who prays to a sense of divine presence that can be harder to access in an entirely enclosed, artificial setting. The *gemara*'s instruction to pray opposite a window points to this dimension of *kavanah* through the orientation of the body and the eyes toward an opening to the world.

The passage concerning Daniel also introduces a striking midrashic elaboration on Moses's intercession after the sin of the golden calf. When Moses invokes the oath God swore to the patriarchs — *zechor le'Avraham le'Yitzchak ule'Yisrael avadecha* — he notes that God swore *bach*, by Himself. Rabbi Elazar explains the significance: had God sworn by heaven and earth, one could have argued that just as heaven and earth are not eternal and will eventually be destroyed, so too the oath might be nullified. But God swore by His own name, *bi nishbati ne'um Hashem*, and just as His name is *chai vekayam*, enduring forever, so too the oath holds forever and cannot be set aside. This is Moses's argument before God: You have bound Yourself irrevocably. The rhetorical force is remarkable — Moses is essentially telling God that He has eliminated His own escape route. This gives the invocation of the merit of the patriarchs in prayer its deeper structure: it is not merely an appeal to God's affection for the ancestors, but an appeal to the unconditional and indestructible character of a divine commitment sworn in God's own name.

### Three Times Daily

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַתְּדַבֵּר אֲלֵיהֶם אַרְבֶּה אֶת זַרְעֲכֶם כְּכוֹכְבֵי הַשָּׁמָיִם וְכׇל הָאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת אֲשֶׁר אָמַרְתִּי״, הַאי ״אֲשֶׁר אָמַרְתִּי״, ״אֲשֶׁר אָמַרְתָּ״ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ!
> **Translation:** In this verse, Moses continues: **“And You said to them: I will make your offspring as numerous as the stars of heaven, and all this land of which I have spoken** I will give to your offspring that they shall inherit it forever.” The Gemara clarifies a puzzling phrase in this verse. **That** phrase **of which I have spoken, it should** have said: **Of which You have spoken,** as Moses is referring to God’s promise to the forefathers.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: עַד כָּאן דִּבְרֵי תַלְמִיד, מִכָּאן וְאֵילָךְ — דִּבְרֵי הָרַב. וְרַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר נַחְמָנִי אָמַר: אֵלּוּ וָאֵלּוּ דִּבְרֵי תַלְמִיד, אֶלָּא כָּךְ אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, דְּבָרִים שֶׁאָמַרְתָּ לִי ״לֵךְ אֱמוֹר לָהֶם לְיִשְׂרָאֵל״ בִּשְׁמִי, הָלַכְתִּי וְאָמַרְתִּי לָהֶם בִּשְׁמֶךָ. עַכְשָׁו מָה אֲנִי אוֹמֵר לָהֶם?
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Elazar said: To this** point, the verse cites **the words of the student,** Moses; **from this point,** and all this land of which I have spoken, the verse cites **the words of the Master,** God. **And Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani said: These and those are the words of the student;** Moses spoke the entire verse. **Rather, Moses said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, those matters which You told me to go and say to Israel in My name, I went and told** it **to them in Your name.** I have already told Israel of God’s promise to the forefathers. **Now what do I say to them?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״מִבִּלְתִּי יְכֹלֶת ה׳״: יָכוֹל ה׳ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara moves to a discussion of additional prayers offered by Moses. Moses said that if God fails to bring the Jewish people into Eretz Yisrael, the nations of the world will say: **“The Lord did not have the ability [*yekholet*]** to bring this people into the land which He swore to them, and He killed them in the desert” (Numbers 14:16). The Gemara examines this verse closely: The verse should not have utilized the term *yekholet*, an abstract feminine noun, but rather, **it should have said: “The Lord** was not **able [*yakhol*],”** a masculine verb.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אָמַר מֹשֶׁה לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, עַכְשָׁיו יֹאמְרוּ אוּמּוֹת הָעוֹלָם תָּשַׁשׁ כֹּחוֹ כִּנְקֵבָה, וְאֵינוֹ יָכוֹל לְהַצִּיל. אָמַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְמֹשֶׁה: וַהֲלֹא כְּבָר רָאוּ נִסִּים וּגְבוּרוֹת שֶׁעָשִׂיתִי לָהֶם עַל הַיָּם. אָמַר לְפָנָיו: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, עֲדַיִין יֵשׁ לָהֶם לוֹמַר: לְמֶלֶךְ אֶחָד יָכוֹל לַעֲמוֹד, לִשְׁלשִׁים וְאֶחָד מְלָכִים אֵינוֹ יָכוֹל לַעֲמוֹד.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Elazar said: Moses** phrased it that way because he **said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, now the nations of the world will say that His strength weakened like a female and He is unable to rescue** the nation of Israel. **The Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Moses: And did** the nations of the world **not already see the miracles and the mighty** acts **that I performed on behalf of** Israel **at the** Red **Sea?** Moses **said before Him: Master of the Universe, they can still say:** The Lord **can stand** up **to a single king** like Pharaoh and defeat him, but **He is unable to stand** up **to the thirty-one kings** in the land of Canaan.

Perhaps one should pray throughout the entire day? The *gemara* answers: it is already made explicit by Daniel — *vezimnin telata*, three times daily. Notably, the proof is drawn from Daniel and not from the sacrificial calendar. The entire framework here is of prayer as a human act of turning toward God, not as a stand-in for *korbanot*. Three fixed times suffice; they divide and mark the day as oriented toward God.

The *sugya* then turns to the content of Moshe's prayer in the passage invoked as a model. The verse contains a grammatical anomaly: Moshe speaks to God in the second person — You spoke to them, You promised — but then shifts to the first person, *asher amarti*, "that I said." *Amar Rabbi Elazar* — Rabbi Elazar taught — *ad kan divrei talmid mikan ve'eilach divrei harav*: until this point the words are those of the student, and from here onward they are the words of the Master. That is, Moshe quotes God's own speech back to Him — "all this land that I have said I will give" — so the *I* is God speaking within Moshe's quotation, not Moshe himself. *Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani amar, elu ve'elu divrei talmid* — Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani disagrees and says both parts are Moshe's own words. On this reading, the shift to *amarti* is not a quotation of God but rather Moshe's way of placing himself in the middle of the promise: You told me to go tell Israel in Your name that they would receive the land, *halachti ve'amarti lahem bishmcha*, I went and told them in Your name — so in that sense, *I* said it to them. *Achshav mah ani omer lahem* — and now what am I to tell them? The rhetorical force is that Moshe has staked his own credibility on God's word, and he presses that vulnerability as grounds for God to honor the promise. This is itself a model of bold, relational prayer — the kind the *sugya* has been building toward throughout.

### Toward Jerusalem

Perhaps one may pray in any direction? The *gemara* answers: one prays *neged Yerushalayim*, toward Jerusalem. Whether this is because Jerusalem is the site of the *Shekhinah* or because it connects to the *korbanot* of the *Beit Hamikdash* is left open. But the thrust of the entire *sugya* — which has consistently cited human models of prayer (Daniel, Chana, David) without invoking sacrificial language — suggests that the orientation toward Jerusalem is about the *Shekhinah* and the locus of divine presence rather than about the sacrificial service.

### Silent Prayer

Perhaps one's voice should be audible during prayer? The *gemara* answers from Chana: *vekola lo yishama* — her voice was not heard. The model of silent, inward prayer is thus established from Chana, who will emerge throughout these *sugyot* as the paradigmatic figure for the laws of *tefillah*.

### Personal Requests

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁחָזַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא וְהוֹדָה לוֹ לְמֹשֶׁה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיֹּאמֶר ה׳ סָלַחְתִּי כִּדְבָרֶיךָ״. תָּנֵי דְּבֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל: כִּדְבָרֶיךָ, עֲתִידִים אוּמּוֹת הָעוֹלָם לוֹמַר כֵּן.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan said: From where** is it derived that **the Holy One, Blessed be He, ultimately conceded to Moses? As it is said: “And the Lord said: I have forgiven according to your word”** (Numbers 14:20). The Sages **of the school of Rabbi Yishmael taught: According to your word,** it will be, as indeed **in the future the nations of the world will say this.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַשְׁרֵי תַלְמִיד שֶׁרַבּוֹ מוֹדֶה לוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara concludes: **Happy is the student whose teacher concedes to him** as the Lord conceded to Moses.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְאוּלָם חַי אָנִי״, אָמַר רָבָא אָמַר רַב יִצְחָק: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאָמַר לוֹ הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְמֹשֶׁה: מֹשֶׁה, הֶחֱיִיתַנִי בִּדְבָרֶיךָ.
> **Translation:** Explaining the next verse, **“Nevertheless, as I live,** and the glory of the Lord fills the entire world” (Numbers 14:21), **Rava said** that **Rav Yitzḥak said:** This **teaches** that **the Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Moses: Moses, you have given Me life with your words.** I am happy that on account of your arguments, I will forgive Israel.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דָּרַשׁ רַבִּי שִׂמְלַאי: לְעוֹלָם יְסַדֵּר אָדָם שִׁבְחוֹ שֶׁל הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, וְאַחַר כָּךְ יִתְפַּלֵּל. מְנָלַן? — מִמֹּשֶׁה, דִּכְתִיב: ״וָאֶתְחַנַּן אֶל ה׳ בָּעֵת הַהִיא״. וּכְתִיב: ״ה׳ אֱלֹהִים אַתָּה הַחִלֹּתָ לְהַרְאוֹת אֶת עַבְדְּךָ אֶת גׇּדְלְךָ וְאֶת יָדְךָ הַחֲזָקָה אֲשֶׁר מִי אֵל בַּשָּׁמַיִם וּבָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר יַעֲשֶׂה כְמַעֲשֶׂיךָ וְכִגְבוּרוֹתֶיךָ״. וּכְתִיב בָּתְרֵיהּ ״אֶעְבְּרָה נָּא וְאֶרְאֶה אֶת הָאָרֶץ הַטּוֹבָה וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** Based on Moses’ prayers, **Rabbi Simlai taught: One should always set forth praise of the Holy One, Blessed be He, and then pray** for his own needs. **From where do we** derive that one should conduct himself in this manner? **From Moses, as it is written** in his prayer: **“And I beseeched the Lord at that time”** (Deuteronomy 3:23). **And** immediately afterward in his prayer, **it is written: “Lord, God, You have begun to show Your servant Your greatness and Your strong hand, for what God is there in the heavens or on earth who can perform deeds such as Yours and Your might”** (Deuteronomy 3:24)? Here, Moses began with praise of God, **and it is** only **thereafter** that **it is written: “Please, let me pass over and see the good land** that is beyond the Jordan, that good hill country and the Lebanon” (Deuteronomy 3:25). Only after his praise did Moses make his personal request.

The *gemara* asks one final question: perhaps personal petitions should come *before* prayer? The answer comes from Shlomo: the verse places *rinah* — understood as *tefillah* in the sense of *shevach*, praise — before *tefillah* in the sense of *bakashah*, petition. The praise comes first, the petition follows. Fascinatingly, the word *tefillah* in the verse is mapped onto *bakashah*, while *rinah* is mapped onto the *shevach* dimension — an implicit acknowledgment that *tefillah* in common usage means petition, even as the structural priority of praise is maintained.

The *gemara* concludes with a practical clarification: one does not interrupt between *emet veyatziv* and *shmoneh esrei* — *geulah* must flow into *tefillah*. But after *shmoneh esrei*, one may extend as long as one wishes, even as long as the *vidui* of *Yom Kippur*. And Rav Yitzchak bar Ashi adds in the name of Rav: *af yishal adam tzerakhav beshome'a tefillah* — one may also insert personal requests within *shmoneh esrei* itself, in the blessing of *shome'a tefillah*. Thus the balance between fixed structure and personal expression is preserved at multiple levels: brief insertions within the body of *shmoneh esrei* in *shome'a tefillah*, and unlimited personal prayer after the conclusion of *shmoneh esrei* before *oker raglav*. The *gemara* continues with the model of Chana, whose prayers will provide the ground for many further laws of *tefillah*.
