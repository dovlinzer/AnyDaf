## Blood Invalidation



### Three Opinions

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר, אָמַר לוֹ רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: לֹא, אִם אָמַרְתָּ. לֵימָא לֵיהּ רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר: אָשָׁם נָמֵי דָּמוֹ לְמַעְלָה!
> **Translation:** § **The Master says: Rabbi Yehoshua said to him:** Your comparison is **not** valid. **If you say** this with regard to a sin offering, whose blood is sprinkled above the red line, should you necessarily say the same with regard to a guilt offering, whose blood is sprinkled below that line? The Gemara challenges: **Let Rabbi Eliezer say to him: The blood of a guilt offering is also** sprinkled **above** the red line, since Rabbi Eliezer holds that the *halakhot* of a guilt offering and a sin offering are parallel.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: אָשָׁם דָּמוֹ לְמַעְלָה לָא מָצֵית אָמְרַתְּ, קַל וְחוֹמֶר מֵעוֹלָה; וּמָה עוֹלָה שֶׁהִיא כָּלִיל – דָּמָהּ לְמַטָּה, אָשָׁם שֶׁאֵינוֹ כָּלִיל – לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן?!
> **Translation:** **Abaye said: You cannot say** that **the blood of a guilt offering is** presented **above** the red line, due to **an *a fortiori*** inference **from** the case of **a burnt offering: Just as** in the case of **a burnt offering, which is** treated stringently in that it is totally **consumed** on the altar, **its blood** is presented **below** the red line, **all the more so** is it **not** clear that in the case of **a guilt offering, which is not** totally **consumed** on the altar, its blood is presented below the red line?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה לְעוֹלָה, שֶׁכֵּן אֵינָהּ מְכַפֶּרֶת!
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects the inference: **What** is notable **about a burnt offering?** It is notable **in that it does not atone** for a sin, unlike a guilt offering. Perhaps this is why its blood is presented below the red line.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חַטַּאת הָעוֹף תּוֹכִיחַ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **A bird sin offering can prove** the point, as it atones for a sin, and still its blood is presented below the red line.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה לְחַטַּאת הָעוֹף, שֶׁכֵּן אֵינָהּ מִין זֶבַח!
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this as well: **What** is notable **about a bird sin offering?** It is notable **in that it is not a type of offering** that is **slaughtered;** it is killed by pinching the nape of its neck. Perhaps this is why its blood is presented below the red line.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עוֹלָה תּוֹכִיחַ. לֹא רְאִי זֶה כִּרְאִי זֶה, וְלֹא רְאִי זֶה כִּרְאִי זֶה. הַצַּד הַשָּׁוֶה שֶׁבָּהֶן – שֶׁהֵן קׇדְשֵׁי קָדָשִׁים, וְדָמָן לְמַטָּה; אַף אֲנִי אָבִיא אָשָׁם – שֶׁהוּא קוֹדֶשׁ קֳדָשִׁים, וְדָמוֹ לְמַטָּה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: If so, **a burnt offering can prove** the point, since it is slaughtered, and its blood is still presented below the red line. Therefore, the *halakha* is derived from a combination of the *halakhot* of a burnt offering and a bird sin offering: **The aspect of this** case **is not like the aspect of that** case **and the aspect of that** case **is not like the aspect of this** case. **Their common element is that they are offerings of the most sacred order, and their blood is** presented **below** the red line. **So too, I shall include a guilt offering** in this *halakha*, **as it is an offering of the most sacred order, and** therefore **its blood is** presented **below** the red line.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רָבָא מִפַּרְזִקְיָא לְרַב אָשֵׁי: וְלִפְרוֹךְ, מָה לְהַצַּד הַשָּׁוֶה שֶׁבָּהֶן – שֶׁכֵּן אֵין לָהֶן קִצְבָה; תֹּאמַר בְּאָשָׁם – שֶׁיֵּשׁ לוֹ קִצְבָה?!
> **Translation:** **Rava of Parzakya said to Rav Ashi: Let** Rabbi Eliezer **refute** this inference: **What** is notable **about the common element of** the two cases? It is notable **in that neither** a burnt offering nor a bird sin offering **has a fixed** value. Need **you say** that the same *halakhot* apply **to a guilt offering, which has a fixed** value of two silver shekels (see Leviticus 5:15)?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא הַיְינוּ טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״הַכֹּהֵן הַמְחַטֵּא אוֹתָהּ״ – אוֹתָהּ דָּמָהּ לְמַעְלָה, וְאֵין דָּמוֹ שֶׁל אַחֵר לְמַעְלָה.
> **Translation:** **Rather, this is Rabbi Eliezer’s reason** for not claiming that the blood of a guilt offering is presented below the red line: **As the verse states** with regard to the presenting of the blood of a sin offering: **“The priest who offers it”** (Leviticus 6:19). The word “it” indicates that **its blood** is presented **above** the red line, **but the blood of another** slaughtered offering is **not** presented **above** the red line.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, חַטָּאת נָמֵי נֵימָא: אוֹתָהּ – לִשְׁמָהּ כְּשֵׁרָה, שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמָהּ פְּסוּלָה; הָא שְׁאָר זְבָחִים – בֵּין לִשְׁמָן בֵּין שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמָן כְּשֵׁרִין!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If so,** if Rabbi Eliezer accepts that the word “it” indicates exclusivity, **let him also say** that the verse: “And slaughter **it** for a sin offering” (Leviticus 4:33), teaches that only **a sin offering is fit** if slaughtered **for its sake,** and **unfit** if slaughtered **not for its sake. Consequently, other slaughtered offerings, whether** slaughtered **for their sake or not for their sake, are fit.** Why then does Rabbi Eliezer deem a guilt offering unfit if slaughtered not for its sake?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָהוּא ״אוֹתָהּ״ לָאו דַּוְקָא, דְּהָא שַׁיַּיר פֶּסַח.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **That** instance of the word **“it”** does **not** indicate that the *halakha* applies **specifically** to a sin offering and to no other offerings, **as** the Torah **excludes** the **Paschal offering** from such a generalization. A Paschal offering is certainly disqualified if sacrificed not for its sake.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכָא נָמֵי לָאו דַּוְקָא, דְּהָא שַׁיַּיר עוֹלַת הָעוֹף!
> **Translation:** The Gemara counters: If so, **here too,** with regard to the presenting of the blood above the red line, one must say the word “it” does **not** indicate that this *halakha* applies **specifically** to a sin offering and no other offerings, **as** the Torah **excludes a bird burnt offering** from such a generalization. The blood of a bird burnt offering is presented above the red line.

The daf opens by citing a *mishnah* from elsewhere in *Masechet Zevachim* — specifically from 91b — which records a three-way dispute concerning bloods that entered the *heichal*, the inner sanctuary. Rabbi Akiva holds that *kol damim shenichnasu laheichal l'chaper psulim* — any blood whatsoever that entered the sanctuary to effect atonement renders the offering *pasul*. The Rabbis restrict this rule to the *chatat* alone. And Rabbi Eliezer includes both the *chatat* and the *asham*, deriving this from the *hekesh* between them expressed in the verse *kachatat k'asham* (Vayikra 7:7).

### Rabbi Akiva's Logic

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּזְבִיחָה מִיהָא לָא שַׁיַּיר מִידֵּי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **With regard to** offerings that require **slaughter, in any event,** the Torah **excluded nothing** from the generalization. Bird offerings are irrelevant, as they are killed by the pinching of the nape.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי בָּעֵית אֵימָא: הָא מַנִּי – רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר בְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן הִיא, דְּאָמַר: הַאי מָקוֹם לְחוֹד, וְהַאי מָקוֹם לְחוֹד.
> **Translation:** **And if you wish, say** instead that even the blood of a bird burnt offering is presented in a manner different from that of a sin offering. In accordance with **whose** opinion **is this** suggestion? **It is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Elazar, son of Rabbi Shimon, who says: This place** where the blood of a sin offering is presented is **discrete, and that place** where the blood of a bird burnt offering is presented is **discrete.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא: דָּמִים הַתַּחְתּוֹנִים נִיתָּנִין מִחוּט הַסִּיקְרָא וּלְמַטָּה, וְהָעֶלְיוֹנִים מִחוּט הַסִּיקְרָא וּלְמַעְלָה. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר בְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן: בַּמֶּה דְּבָרִים אֲמוּרִין – בְּעוֹלַת הָעוֹף; אֲבָל בְּחַטַּאת בְּהֵמָה – הִיא עַצְמָהּ אֵין נַעֲשֶׂה אֶלָּא עַל גּוּפָהּ שֶׁל קֶרֶן.
> **Translation:** **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: **The blood** that must be placed on **the lower** half of the altar **is placed** anywhere **from the red [*hasikra*] line and below, and** the blood that must be placed on **the upper** half is placed anywhere **from the red line and above. Rabbi Elazar, son of Rabbi Shimon, said: In what** case **is this statement said?** It is said **with regard to a bird burnt offering. But** the placement of the blood of **an animal sin offering is itself performed only on the actual corner** of the altar.

Rabbi Akiva draws his expansive position from a verse in *Parshat Tzav* (Vayikra): *v'chol chatat asher yuvah midamah el ohel moed lo teachal ba-esh tisaref* — every *chatat* whose blood is brought into the Tent of Meeting shall not be eaten but burned in fire. The double formulation — *v'chol chatat*, "and every sin offering" — reads to Rabbi Akiva as a kind of double plural, a linguistic amplification that extends the disqualification beyond the *chatat* itself to all bloods that enter the sanctuary.

### Rabbis vs. R. Eliezer

The Rabbis confine the rule to the *chatat* alone, while Rabbi Eliezer extends it to include the *asham* on the basis of the *hekesh* linking *chatat* and *asham*. The tension between these positions — when the *chatat* and *asham* are treated as equivalent and when they are not — runs as a central thread through much of the discussion that follows. As will become clear, the question of which direction legal inferences may travel between these offerings is not settled so simply.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּנַן הָתָם, שֶׁהָיָה רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא אוֹמֵר: כׇּל דָּמִים שֶׁנִּכְנְסוּ לַהֵיכָל לְכַפֵּר – פְּסוּלִים. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: חַטָּאת בִּלְבַד. רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר אוֹמֵר: אַף הָאָשָׁם, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״כַּחַטָּאת כָּאָשָׁם״.
> **Translation:** § **We learned** in a mishna **elsewhere** (81b): **As Rabbi Akiva would say: Any blood that** should have been sprinkled on the external altar but instead **entered to atone in the Sanctuary** is **unfit. And the Rabbis say:** That is the *halakha* **only** with regard to the blood of **a sin offering. Rabbi Eliezer says:** The halakhic status of **a guilt offering** is **also** like that of a sin offering, **as it is stated: “As is the sin offering, so is the guilt offering”** (Leviticus 7:7).

## Rava's Reasoning



### Rava's Answer


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר – כִּדְקָאָמַר טַעְמָא, אֶלָּא רַבָּנַן מַאי טַעְמָא?
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Granted,** the opinion of **Rabbi Eliezer** is understandable, **as he states the reason** for his opinion. **But what is the reason** for the opinion of **the Rabbis?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: אָשָׁם נִכְנַס דָּמוֹ לִפְנִים פָּסוּל – לָא מָצֵית אָמְרַתְּ, קַל וָחוֹמֶר מֵעוֹלָה; מָה
> **Translation:** **Rava said: You cannot say** that **the blood of a guilt offering that entered into** the Sanctuary is **disqualified,** due to **an *a fortiori*** inference **from** the case of **a burnt offering: Just as**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עוֹלָה שֶׁהִיא כָּלִיל, נִכְנַס דָּמָהּ לִפְנִים כְּשֵׁרָה; אָשָׁם שֶׁאֵינוֹ כָּלִיל, לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן?!
> **Translation:** with regard to **a burnt offering, which is** totally **consumed** by the fire on the altar, nevertheless, even if **its blood enters into** the Sanctuary it is **fit,** with regard to **a guilt offering, which is not** entirely **consumed** on the altar, **all the more so** is it **not** clear that even if its blood enters the Sanctuary it remains fit?

The *Gemara* asks: *bishlama* Rabbi Eliezer, his reason is stated — but what is the Rabbis' reason for excluding the *asham*? Rava explains: we cannot derive from a *kal vachomer* from the *olah* that the *asham*'s blood entering the sanctuary renders it *pasul*.

### Failed Kal va-Chomer


The attempted *kal vachomer* runs as follows: if the *olah*, which is completely burned — *kalil* — and yet its blood entering the sanctuary does not disqualify it, could we not argue *kal vachomer* that the *asham*, which is not burned completely, would similarly not be disqualified when its blood enters? The argument collapses immediately on inspection, however: this proves the *asham* is *kasher* when its blood enters, not *pasul* — the exact opposite of what we were trying to establish.

### Refuting Olah Logic

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה לְעוֹלָה, שֶׁכֵּן אֵינָהּ מְכַפֶּרֶת!
> **Translation:** The Gemara refutes this inference: **What** is notable **about a burnt offering?** It is notable **in that it does not atone** for a sin. Perhaps this is why its blood is not disqualified if it enters the sanctuary.

A possible objection to the *olah* as the basis for this reasoning is then raised: *mah l'olah sheken einah mechaperet* — what is special about the *olah* is that it does not atone for a specific transgression. Since the *olah* is not an atonement offering in the way the *chatat* is, the comparison breaks down. The *olah* belongs to a fundamentally different category, and legal inferences across such a divide are suspect.

### Meal Offering Attempt

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִנְחַת חוֹטֵא תּוֹכִיחַ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **The meal offering of a sinner can prove** the point, as it is not disqualified by entering the Sanctuary, even though it atones for a sin.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֵימָא: חַטַּאת הָעוֹף תּוֹכִיחַ! חַטַּאת הָעוֹף – בַּעְיָא דְּרַבִּי אָבִין הִיא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara interjects: **But let us say** that **a bird sin offering can prove** the point, as it too atones for a sin but is not disqualified by entering the Sanctuary. This case is more similar to that of a guilt offering, as a bird has blood, unlike a meal offering. The Gemara answers: It is not certain that **a bird sin offering** is not disqualified by entering the Sanctuary; this **is a dilemma** raised **by Rabbi Avin.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִפְרוֹךְ: מָה לְמִנְחַת חוֹטֵא, שֶׁכֵּן אֵינָהּ מִין זֶבַח!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But let us refute** the above response as follows: **What** is notable **about a sinner’s meal offering?** It is notable **in that it is not a type of slaughtered offering.**

The *Gemara* therefore attempts to construct the argument from the *minchat choteh*, the meal offering of a sinner — an offering that, like the *chatat* and *asham*, does serve an atonement function. But this too is rejected: *mah leminchat choteh sheken einah min hazevach* — the *minchah* is simply not in the category of animal sacrifice (*zevach*), and the analogy therefore fails for a different reason. The *Gemara* briefly considers whether the *chatat ha'of*, the bird sin offering, might serve instead, but that too is rejected — the *chatat ha'of* follows the ruling of Rabbi Avin and involves a different process; the blood is collected differently, with a finger rather than through standard *shechitah* and *kabbalah*.

### Kal va-Chomer Fails

Having exhausted these alternatives, the *Gemara* reverts to the *olah*. Now both possible analogies have failed on their own terms. As the formulation goes: *lo rei zeh k'rei zeh v'lo rei zeh k'rei zeh* — this case is not fully like that one, and that one is not fully like this one. The *kal vachomer* cannot be constructed from either direction.

## Kodshei Kodashim



### Holiness Level

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עוֹלָה תּוֹכִיחַ. וְחָזַר הַדִּין; לֹא רְאִי זֶה כִרְאִי זֶה, וְלֹא רְאִי זֶה כִּרְאִי זֶה; הַצַּד הַשָּׁוֶה שֶׁבָּהֶן: שֶׁהֵן קׇדְשֵׁי קָדָשִׁים, וְנִכְנַס (דָּמָן) לִפְנִים – כְּשֵׁרִין; אַף אֲנִי אָבִיא אָשָׁם – שֶׁהוּא קוֹדֶשׁ קֳדָשִׁים, וְנִכְנַס דָּמוֹ לִפְנִים – כָּשֵׁר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: If so, **a burnt offering can prove** the point, since it is a slaughtered offering, and still if its blood enters the Sanctuary, it is not disqualified. **And the inference has reverted** to its starting point. The *halakha* is derived from the *halakhot* of a burnt offering and a sinner’s meal offering: **The aspect of this** case **is not like the aspect of that** case **and the aspect of that** case **is not like the aspect of this** case. **Their common element is that they are offerings of the most sacred order, and** if they **enter into** the Sanctuary **they are** still **fit. So too, I shall include a guilt offering** and infer that **since it is** also **an offering of the most sacred order,** if **its blood enters into** the Sanctuary it is still **fit.**

Since no single offering provides an adequate basis for a *kal vachomer*, the *Gemara* seeks instead the *tzad hashaveh* — the common feature shared by the *olah* and the *chatat* that might allow an inference to the *asham*. The answer: both the *olah* and the *chatat* are *kodshei kodashim*, offerings of the highest degree of sanctity. From this shared characteristic one might seek to derive: *af ani avi asham shehu kodesh kodashim* — I will similarly bring the *asham*, which is also *kodshei kodashim*, into the same legal category.

### Asham Classification

The *asham* does indeed qualify as *kodshei kodashim*. The attempt to group it with the *olah* and *chatat* on this basis initially seems promising. However, a distinction is then introduced that cuts against the inference.

### Asham's Minimum

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רָבָא מִבַּרְנִישׁ לְרַב אָשֵׁי: וְלִפְרוֹךְ, מָה לְהַצַּד הַשָּׁוֶה שֶׁבָּהֶן – שֶׁכֵּן אֵין לָהֶן קִצְבָה; תֹּאמַר בְּאָשָׁם – שֶׁיֵּשׁ לוֹ קִצְבָה?!
> **Translation:** **Rava of Barnish said to Rav Ashi: But let us refute** this inference: **What** is notable **about their common element?** It is notable **in that neither** a burnt offering nor a sinner’s meal offering **has a fixed** value. **Should you say** the same *halakhot* apply **to a guilt offering, which has a fixed** value?

Rava of Barnish points out to Rav Ashi: *mah l'tzad hashaveh shebahen sheken ein lahen kitzvah* — what is distinctive about the common feature of the *olah* and *chatat* is that neither has a prescribed minimum monetary value. *Tomar b'asham sheyesh lo kitzvah* — but you cannot make the same inference regarding the *asham*, which does have a minimum value (*kitzvah*). The Torah was more stringent with the *asham* in requiring a minimum, and this distinguishing feature prevents the inference from succeeding. This, the *Gemara* concludes, is the fundamental reason (*ta'amei d'Rabbanan*) underlying the Rabbis' position.

## Verse Interpretation



### Damah Pronoun

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא הַיְינוּ טַעְמַיְיהוּ דְּרַבָּנַן, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״דָּמָהּ״ – דָּמָהּ שֶׁל זוֹ, וְלָא דָּמָהּ שֶׁל אַחֵר.
> **Translation:** **Rather, this is the reason** for the opinion **of the Rabbis: As the verse states:** “And any sin offering, any of **whose blood** is brought into the Tent of Meeting to atone in the Sanctuary, shall not be eaten” (Leviticus 6:23). The term “whose blood” teaches that **the blood of this** offering, i.e., a sin offering, **and not the blood of another** offering, is disqualified in this manner.

The *Gemara* now turns to the precise derivation from the verse itself. The relevant verse (Vayikra) reads: *v'chol chatat asher yuvah midamah el ohel moed* — every *chatat* from whose blood is brought into the Tent of Meeting. The word *damah* — "its blood" — is now subjected to close analysis. The *Gemara* first reads it as *damah shel zu v'lo damah shel acher*: the verse speaks of the blood of this specific offering and not the blood of a different one. That is, it is only the *chatat*'s own blood entering the sanctuary that disqualifies it; blood from another offering entering does not disqualify the *chatat*.

### R. Eliezer's Read

Rabbi Eliezer takes the word *damah* differently. For him, the reference to blood — *damah v'lo besarah* — teaches that it is the blood of the offering that triggers disqualification, not its flesh. He is not concerned with whether it is the blood of this offering or another's; his reading addresses a different question entirely.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִידַּךְ – ״דָּמָהּ״ וְלֹא בְּשָׂרָהּ.
> **Translation:** **And** how does **the other** *tanna*, Rabbi Eliezer, interpret this term? He derives from it that only **its blood** is disqualified by entering the Sanctuary, **but not its flesh.**

### Rabbis' Limit


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִידָּךְ – ״דָּם״–״דָּמָהּ״.
> **Translation:** **And** from where do **the other** Rabbis derive that the flesh of a sin offering is not disqualified by entering the Sanctuary? They maintain that both *halakhot* are derived from this term, as the verse could have simply stated: **Blood,** but stated: **“Whose blood.”** The redundancy excludes not only the flesh, but also other offerings.

The Rabbis, by contrast, learn two separate lessons from *dam damah*. First, as Rabbi Eliezer does, that the verse speaks of blood and not flesh. Second — and this was already noted at the bottom of 10b — that the rule applies only to the *chatat* and does not extend to other offerings. The double formulation (*dam... damah*) yields both restrictions simultaneously for the Rabbis.

### R. Eliezer Differs

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִידַּךְ – ״דָּם״–״דָּמָהּ״ לָא דָּרֵישׁ.
> **Translation:** **And the other** *tanna*, Rabbi Eliezer, **does not interpret** the redundancy indicated by the change from: **Blood,** to: **“Whose blood,”** to be significant. Once the word blood is mentioned, it is grammatically necessary for the verse to state “whose blood.”

Rabbi Eliezer does not read the verse in this doubled fashion. Since the word *damah* is the grammatically correct form for "its blood," he treats it as a single, necessary term — not an amplification from which additional rulings may be derived — and uses it solely to teach that blood, not flesh, is the operative element. He therefore cannot draw from the same verse the Rabbis' limiting conclusion.

## Mincha Connection



### Asham Intent

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא לְרַבָּנַן, דְּאָמְרִי: אָשָׁם שֶׁשְּׁחָטוֹ שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמוֹ – כָּשֵׁר; הַיְינוּ דְּאִיתַּקַּשׁ מִנְחָה לְחַטָּאת, מִנְחָה לְאָשָׁם.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara resumes its discussion of the dispute in the mishna between Rabbi Eliezer and the Rabbis. **Granted, according to the Rabbis, who say** that **a guilt offering that one slaughtered not for its sake** is **fit, that is why a meal offering is juxtaposed with a sin offering,** and **a meal offering** is also juxtaposed **with a guilt offering.**

Returning to the broader question, the *Gemara* notes: *bishlama l'Rabbanan d'amri asham sheshachato shelo lishmo kasher* — according to the Rabbis, an *asham* that was slaughtered *shelo lishmo*, not for its designated purpose, remains *kasher*. It does not fulfill the owner's obligation, but the offering itself is valid. This sets up the question of how the *minchah* is linked, by *hekesh*, to both the *chatat* and the *asham*.

### Mincha Dual Nature


The *Gemara* observes that the *minchat choteh* — the meal offering brought by a sinner — can in some respects resemble a *chatat* and in other respects resemble an *asham*, depending on the context. This dual character follows from a specific verse.

### Rabbi Shimon Quote

Rabbi Shimon cites the verse from *Parshat Tzav* (Vayikra 6:10): *kodesh kodashim hi kachatat v'ka'asham* — it is most holy, like the *chatat* and like the *asham*. This verse, he explains, is the source for the *minchat choteh* being treated as analogous to both.

### Two Mincha Types

The practical consequence runs as follows: *minchat choteh harei hi kachatat* — the *minchat choteh* is like the *chatat* — therefore, if the *kemitzah*, the removal of the handful, was performed *shelo lishmah*, not for its intended purpose, it is *psulah*, invalid. By contrast, *minchat nedavah harei hi ka'asham* — the voluntary *minchah* is like the *asham* — therefore, if its *kemitzah* was performed *shelo lishmah*, it remains *kasher*. The *asham* slaughtered *shelo lishmo* is still valid; so too the voluntary *minchah* whose handful was removed without the requisite intent.

## Four Blood Works



### Blood Services

The *Gemara* now identifies the four *avot ha-dam*, the four primary blood services performed in connection with animal sacrifices: *shechitah* (slaughter), *kabbalah* (collection of the blood), *holachah* (conveying the blood to the altar), and *zrikah* (sprinkling the blood).

### Mincha Parallels

Each of these four services has a parallel in the *minchah*. In place of *shechitah* stands the *kemitzah* — the removal of the handful. In place of *kabbalah* stands the placing of the *minchah* into a *kli sharet*, a sacred service vessel. *Holachah* corresponds directly to *holachah* — carrying the handful to the altar. And in place of *zrikah* stands the placing of the *kometz* upon the fire.

### Kemitza Role

The analogy between *kemitzah* and *shechitah* is worth pausing over. Just as *shechitah* is the initial act of preparation that enables all subsequent blood service, the *kemitzah* is the first act that sets the *minchah* service in motion. And just as any valid *shochet* may perform *shechitah* in the context of sacrifices that require a *kohen* for the blood service, the *kemitzah* must be performed specifically by a *kohen*. This shared requirement of priestly performance reinforces the parallel.

### Kli Sharet Need

The requirement that the *minchah* be placed into a *kli sharet* parallels the *kabbalah* of the blood into a vessel. There is a deeper principle at work here as well. Animal sacrifices possess an intrinsic *kedushat haguf* — a sanctity of the body — that attaches to the animal from the moment of designation. The *minchah*, however, acquires this sanctity of the body only through the act of placing it into the *kli sharet*. Without the *kli sharet*, the *minchah* has not yet received the full measure of *kedushah* that enables the subsequent service to be valid. The teaching recorded in the *mishnah* — *shelo bichli sharet psulah* — that a *minchah* processed without a sacred vessel is *psulah* — reflects this principle, though Rabbi Shimon disagrees and validates it.

## Rav Yehuda Explains



### Kodesh Kodashim

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: ״קֹדֶשׁ קָדָשִׁים הִוא כַּחַטָּאת וְכָאָשָׁם״ – מִנְחַת חוֹטֵא הֲרֵי הִיא כְּחַטָּאת, לְפִיכָךְ קְמָצָהּ שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמָהּ, פְּסוּלָה; מִנְחַת נְדָבָה הֲרֵי הִיא כְּאָשָׁם, לְפִיכָךְ קְמָצָהּ שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמָהּ, כְּשֵׁרָה.
> **Translation:** This is **as it is taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi Shimon says:** The verse states with regard to a meal offering: **“It is most sacred, as the sin offering, and as the guilt offering”** (Leviticus 6:10), teaching that **a meal offering of a sinner is like a sin offering. Therefore,** if one **removed its handful not for its sake,** it is **disqualified.** And the verse also teaches that **a voluntary meal offering is like a guilt offering. Therefore,** if one **removed its handful not for its sake,** it is still **fit.** This accords with the opinion of the Rabbis, that a guilt offering slaughtered not for its sake is fit.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא לְרַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר – לְמַאי הִלְכְתָא אִיתַּקַּשׁ מִנְחָה לְחַטָּאת וּמִנְחָה לְאָשָׁם?
> **Translation:** **But according to Rabbi Eliezer, for what *halakha* is a meal offering juxtaposed with a sin offering, and a meal offering** juxtaposed **with a guilt offering?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְכִי אִידַּךְ דְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן, דִּתְנַן: שֶׁלֹּא בִּכְלֵי שָׁרֵת – פְּסוּלָה. וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן מַכְשִׁיר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: They are juxtaposed **to** teach **the other** *halakha* derived **by Rabbi Shimon** from this verse; **as we learned** in a mishna (*Menaḥot* 26a): If the handful of a meal offering is conveyed to the altar **not in a service vessel,** the offering is **unfit. And Rabbi Shimon deems** it **fit.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי חִיָּיא: מַאי טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן? אָמַר קְרָא: ״קֹדֶשׁ קֳדָשִׁים הִוא כַּחַטָּאת וּכְאָשָׁם״ – בָּא לְעוֹבְדָהּ בַּיָּד, עוֹבְדָהּ בַּיָּמִין כַּחַטָּאת; בִּכְלִי, עוֹבְדָהּ בִּשְׂמֹאל כָּאָשָׁם.
> **Translation:** **And Rav Yehuda, son of Rabbi Ḥiyya, says: What is the reasoning of Rabbi Shimon? The verse states** with regard to a meal offering: **“It is most sacred, as the sin offering, and as the guilt offering,”** teaching that if a priest **comes to perform** its sacrificial rites **by hand, he must perform** them **with** his **right** hand, **like** one performs the rites of **a sin offering.** And if he performs them **with a vessel, he may perform** them **with** his **left** hand, **like** one performs the rites of **a guilt offering,** since Rabbi Shimon maintains this is permitted in the case of a guilt offering.

Rav Yehuda the son of Rav Chiyya explains the reason behind Rabbi Shimon's position. The verse states: *kodesh kodashim hi kachatat v'ka'asham* — most holy it is, like the *chatat* and like the *asham*. This single verse, he argues, contains within it two distinct teachings about how the *minchah* resembles each of these offerings in turn.

### Hand Distinction

The parallel to the *chatat* emerges from the right-handed performance of the *minchah*'s service: *ba le'ovda beyad, ovda be-yamin kachatat* — when it comes to performing the service by hand, performing it with the right hand makes it analogous to the *chatat*. The parallel to the *asham* emerges from the left-handed dimension: *ovda be-smol ka'asham* — when performed with the left hand, the service resembles that of the *asham*. This yields a coherent framework: the same offering may behave like a *chatat* in one register and like an *asham* in another, depending on the specific act being analyzed.

### Dual Extraction

Rabbi Shimon extracts both of these lessons from the single verse. The verse *kodesh kodashim hi kachatat v'ka'asham* is not redundant; each element of the comparison — *kachatat*, *ka'asham* — carries its own distinct legal meaning, and Rabbi Shimon deploys both.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן – הַאי קְרָא מַפֵּיק לֵיהּ לְהָכִי וּמַפֵּיק לֵיהּ לְהָכִי?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** as for **Rabbi Shimon** himself, **does he expound this verse for this** purpose, concerning one who if one removes its handful not for its sake, **and expound it for that** purpose, concerning conveying the handful with one’s hand?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עִיקָּר קְרָא – לְכִדְרַב יְהוּדָה בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי חִיָּיא הוּא דַּאֲתָא; וּמִנְחַת חוֹטֵא דִּפְסוּלָה שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמָהּ [– טַעְמָא אַחֲרִינָא הוּא]: חַטָּאת טַעְמָא מַאי – דִּכְתִיב בַּהּ ״הִיא״; מִנְחַת חוֹטֵא נָמֵי כְּתִיב בַּהּ ״הִיא״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **The verse comes mainly to** teach **that which Rav Yehuda, son of Rabbi Ḥiyya,** explained. **And** the *halakha* **that a meal offering of a sinner** whose handful was removed **not for its sake** is **unfit is** due to **another reason: What is the reason a sin offering** sacrificed not for its sake is disqualified? The reason is **that** the word **“it” is written with regard to it** in the verse: “It is a sin offering” (Leviticus 4:24). Since the word **“it” is also written with regard to the meal offering of a sinner,** in the verse: “For it is a sin offering” (Leviticus 5:11), the same applies in that case.

## Kemitza Intent



### Purpose Requirement

The *Gemara* now specifies the conditions under which the *minchat choteh* is rendered invalid on account of improper intent. If the *kemitzah* — the removal of the handful — was performed *shelo lishmah*, not for the purpose of the *minchat choteh*, the offering is *psulah*. The requirement of *lishmah* is not merely aspirational; a deviation in intent at this stage invalidates the service entirely.

### Specificity from 'Hi'


The source for this ruling is the word *hi* — "it is" — appearing in the verse: *kodesh kodashim hi kachatat v'ka'asham*. The word *hi* is not syntactically necessary; the verse could have read *kodesh kodashim kachatat v'ka'asham* without it. Its presence teaches that the offering must be specifically what it is — *davka* this *minchah*, for this purpose. The word *hi* introduces a requirement of particularity: the handful must be removed with the *minchah choteh*'s own identity in mind.

### Chatat Parallel

The same word *hi* appears in the verse governing the *chatat* itself (Vayikra 4:24), and the Rabbis read it there identically: the *chatat* too must be slaughtered specifically for its own purpose. The parallel structure of the two verses — each containing the word *hi* in an apparently superfluous position — allows the *Gemara* to derive the same purpose requirement for both offerings from the same linguistic device.

## Semichah Requirement



### Hekesh Logic

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְרַבָּנַן, לְמַאי הִלְכְתָא אִיתַּקַּשׁ אָשָׁם לְחַטָּאת? לוֹמַר לָךְ: מָה חַטָּאת טְעוּנָה סְמִיכָה, אַף אָשָׁם טָעוּן סְמִיכָה.
> **Translation:** In the mishna, Rabbi Eliezer cites the verse: “As is the sin offering, so is the guilt offering” (Leviticus 7:7), as a source for his opinion. The Gemara asks: **And according to the Rabbis, for what *halakha* is a guilt offering juxtaposed with a sin offering?** The Gemara answers: They are juxtaposed **to tell you** that **just as a sin offering requires placing hands** on the head of the offering, **so too, a guilt offering requires placing hands** on the head of the offering.

The *Gemara* then asks how the Rabbis employ the *hekesh* linking *asham* to *chatat*, given that they reject Rabbi Eliezer's use of it to extend the blood-invalidation rule to the *asham*. The answer: *mah chatat te'unah semikhah af asham ta'un semikhah* — just as the *chatat* requires *semikhah* — the owner's leaning upon the animal's head prior to slaughter — so too the *asham* requires *semikhah*. This, and not the extension of blood-disqualification, is the legal lesson the Rabbis extract from the *hekesh* between the two offerings.

## Wrong Purpose



### Yosef & R. Eliezer

The *Gemara* now returns to the *mishnah* from the beginning of the tractate (2a), where Yosef ben Choni states: *hanishchatim leshem pesach v'leshem chatat psulim* — animals slaughtered with the intent of a *pesach* or a *chatat* are *psulim*. The reasoning is apparent: the *chatat* is an atonement offering tied to a specific sin and a specific individual; intent cannot be freely redirected between atonement offerings. And the *pesach* carries its own cluster of stringencies — a fixed time, a fixed group of designated eaters, a fixed sacrificial identity — that make it uniquely resistant to substitution.

### Yochanan Expansion

Rabbi Yochanan reads Yosef ben Choni's position as equivalent to that of Rabbi Eliezer, and goes further: the agreement between them, he argues, extends beyond *chatat* and *pesach* themselves. Not only are those specific offerings *pasul* when done *shelo lishmam*, but all *korbanot* performed for the sake of a *chatat* or *pesach* are likewise *pasul*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יוֹסֵף בֶּן חוֹנִי אוֹמֵר: הַנִּשְׁחָטִין כּוּ׳. אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: יוֹסֵף בֶּן חוֹנִי וְרַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר, אָמְרוּ דָּבָר אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **Yosef ben Ḥoni says:** Other offerings **that are slaughtered** for the sake of a Paschal offering or a sin offering are unfit. **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: Yosef ben Ḥoni and Rabbi Eliezer said the same thing,** i.e., Rabbi Eliezer agrees with Yosef ben Ḥoni.

### Raba's Limit


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבָּה אָמַר: בַּאֲחֵרִים לְשֵׁם חַטָּאת פְּלִיגִי.
> **Translation:** **Rabba says:** Although they agree that other offerings slaughtered for the sake of a Paschal offering are unfit, **they disagree with regard to other** offerings slaughtered **for the sake of a sin offering.** Whereas Yosef ben Ḥoni holds that they are unfit, Rabbi Eliezer maintains that they are fit.

Raba offers a more restrained reading. He holds that Yosef ben Choni and Rabbi Eliezer agree regarding the *pesach* — an *olah* slaughtered for the sake of the *pesach* is *pasul* — but they diverge when it comes to other offerings slaughtered for the sake of a *chatat*. For Yosef ben Choni, such an offering is *pasul*; for Rabbi Eliezer, it is *kasher*, though it does not fulfill the owner's *chiyuv*. The sacrifice itself remains valid; only its capacity to discharge the obligation is lacking. This disagreement between Raba and Rabbi Yochanan — whether they are truly in agreement or differ on the question of *bacharim leshem chatat* — is noted as a matter of dispute, with the *Tosafot* on 11b arguing that Raba is not in fact disputing Rabbi Yochanan but elaborating upon him.

## Pesach Dispute



### Baraita Setup

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא: הַפֶּסַח שֶׁעִבְּרָה שְׁנָתוֹ, וּשְׁחָטוֹ בִּזְמַנּוֹ לִשְׁמוֹ; וְכֵן הַשּׁוֹחֵט אֲחֵרִים לְשֵׁם פֶּסַח בִּזְמַנּוֹ – רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר פּוֹסֵל, וְרַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ מַכְשִׁיר.
> **Translation:** This is **as it is taught** in a *baraita*: In the case of **a Paschal offering whose** first **year has passed,** and it must therefore be sacrificed as a peace offering, **and one slaughtered it at its** designated **time** on Passover eve **for its own sake** as a Paschal offering, **and likewise,** if **one slaughtered other** offerings **for the sake of a Paschal offering at its** designated **time,** the *tanna’im* disagree as to the status of the offering. **Rabbi Eliezer deems** it **unfit, and Rabbi Yehoshua deems** it **fit.**

A *baraita* is now introduced that sets out the positions of Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua more fully. Two cases are considered together: (1) a *pesach* that passed its year — the animal designated for *pesach* grew older than one year before being brought, and is now slaughtered at the time of *pesach* with *pesach* intent; and (2) an animal that was not designated as *pesach* but is slaughtered at the time of *pesach* for the sake of *pesach*. In both cases, Rabbi Eliezer declares the offering *pasul* and Rabbi Yehoshua declares it *kasher*.

### Three Categories

Before entering the debate, it is important to note that the *Gemara* works with three distinct legal statuses, not two. An offering can be *kasher* — valid and accepted. It can be *pasul* — invalid. Or it can occupy an intermediate position: *kasher* but *lo yotzei hachiyuv* — valid as a sacrifice in that it may be eaten, but not fulfilling the owner's religious obligation. This third category is crucial to understanding the dispute. A *pesach* offering done with imperfect intent does not necessarily become forbidden; the question is whether it achieves anything beyond producing a meal.

### Yehoshua's Kal

Rabbi Yehoshua advances a *kal vachomer*. During the rest of the year, the *pesach* offering is not valid for its own sake — it cannot be a *pesach* when offered outside of its time — yet other animals offered for its sake are *kasher* (they become *shelamim*). How much more so, then, at the time of *pesach* itself — when the *pesach* is valid for its own sake — should other animals slaughtered for its sake also be *kasher*?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: אִם בִּשְׁאָר יְמוֹת הַשָּׁנָה, שֶׁאֵינוֹ כָּשֵׁר לִשְׁמוֹ – אֲחֵרִים כְּשֵׁרִים לִשְׁמוֹ; בִּזְמַנּוֹ שֶׁהוּא כָּשֵׁר לִשְׁמוֹ – אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁיִּכְשְׁרוּ אֲחֵרִים לִשְׁמוֹ?!
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yehoshua said: If, during the rest of the days of the year, when** a Paschal offering **is not fit** if it is slaughtered **for its own sake,** nevertheless, **other** offerings that are slaughtered **for its sake are fit,** then **at its** designated **time, when** a Paschal offering **is fit** if it is slaughtered **for its own sake, isn’t it logical that other** offerings slaughtered **for its sake should be fit?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לוֹ רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר, אוֹ חִילּוּף הַדְּבָרִים: וּמָה אִם בִּשְׁאָר יְמוֹת הַשָּׁנָה, שֶׁאֵינוֹ כָּשֵׁר לִשְׁמוֹ – כָּשֵׁר הוּא לְשֵׁם אֲחֵרִים; בִּזְמַנּוֹ, שֶׁהוּא כָּשֵׁר לִשְׁמוֹ – אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁיּוּכְשַׁר לְשֵׁם אֲחֵרִים?! וְיוּכְשַׁר פֶּסַח בְּאַרְבָּעָה עָשָׂר שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמוֹ!
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Eliezer said to him: Or conversely, just as during the rest of the days of the year, when** a Paschal offering **is not fit** if it is slaughtered **for its own sake, it is fit** if slaughtered **for the sake of other** offerings, so too, **at its** designated **time, when it is fit** if slaughtered **for its own sake, isn’t it logical that it should be fit** if slaughtered **for the sake of other** offerings? **And** accordingly, **a Paschal offering** slaughtered **not for its own sake on** the **fourteenth** of Nisan **should be deemed fit,** which contradicts the *halakha*. Clearly, this line of reasoning leads to incorrect conclusions.

### Eliezer's Counter



Rabbi Eliezer refuses this *kal vachomer* and offers the reverse. During the rest of the year, the *pesach* offering is not *kasher* for its own sake, yet it is *kasher* for the sake of other offerings — meaning a *pesach*-designated animal offered outside its time becomes *shelamim* and is valid. If so, at the time of *pesach* itself — when it is *kasher* for its own sake — *kal vachomer* it should be *kasher* for the sake of other offerings as well. But this conclusion — that a *pesach* slaughtered on the fourteenth of Nisan *shelo lishmo* is *kasher* — directly contradicts established *halacha*. Since the reverse *kal vachomer* produces an absurd result, Rabbi Eliezer uses it to refute Rabbi Yehoshua's original reasoning.

### Yehoshua Responds

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכָךְ אַתָּה אוֹמֵר?! מָה לִי הוּכְשְׁרוּ אֲחֵרִים בִּשְׁאָר יְמוֹת הַשָּׁנָה לִשְׁמוֹ – שֶׁכֵּן הוּא כָּשֵׁר לְשֵׁם אֲחֵרִים; וְיוּכְשְׁרוּ אֲחֵרִים בִּזְמַנּוֹ לִשְׁמוֹ – שֶׁכֵּן הוּא פָּסוּל לְשֵׁם אֲחֵרִים?!
> **Translation:** Furthermore, Rabbi Eliezer refuted Rabbi Yehoshua’s logic as follows: **And do you say this** derivation? **What** does it matter **to me** that **other** offerings slaughtered **during the rest of the days of the year for the sake of** a Paschal offering **are deemed fit? After all,** a Paschal offering slaughtered **for the sake of other** offerings **is** also **fit. But** must you say that **other** offerings slaughtered **at its** designated **time for its sake should be deemed fit? After all,** at its designated time, a Paschal offering slaughtered **for the sake of other** offerings **is unfit.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לוֹ רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: אִם כֵּן, הוֹרַעְתָּה כֹּחַ פֶּסַח, וְנָתַתָּ כֹּחַ בִּשְׁלָמִים.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yehoshua said to him: If,** as you maintain, other offerings slaughtered on Passover eve for the sake of a Paschal offering are disqualified, **you have diminished the force** of the requirement that **a Paschal offering** be sacrificed for its own sake, **and you have given** more **force to** the requirement that **a peace offering** be sacrificed for its own sake. A Paschal offering sacrificed at the proper time, i.e., during the rest of the year, for the sake of a peace offering, is fit, whereas a peace offering sacrificed on Passover eve for the sake of a Paschal offering is unfit.

Rabbi Yehoshua pushes back sharply: if Rabbi Eliezer insists on this reading, then he has diminished the power of the *pesach* and artificially elevated the power of the *shelamim*. The argument Rabbi Eliezer is making would make the *pesach* — in some respects — weaker than the *shelamim*, which is a counterintuitive and legally uncomfortable result.

### One-Way Logic

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חָזַר רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר וְדָנוֹ דִּין אַחֵר: מָצִינוּ מוֹתַר פֶּסַח בָּא שְׁלָמִים, וְאֵין מוֹתַר שְׁלָמִים בָּא פֶּסַח.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: **Rabbi Eliezer then presented a different logical inference: We find** that **a leftover Paschal offering,** an animal consecrated as a Paschal offering but ultimately not sacrificed on Passover eve, is **brought** as **a peace offering; but a leftover peace offering,** one bought with money left over from funds dedicated toward the acquisition of a peace offering, is **not brought** as **a Paschal offering.** A Paschal offering is a potential peace offering, but a peace offering is not a potential Paschal offering.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמָה פֶּסַח, שֶׁמּוֹתָרוֹ בָּא שְׁלָמִים – שְׁחָטוֹ בִּזְמַנּוֹ לְשׁוּם שְׁלָמִים, פָּסוּל; שְׁלָמִים, שֶׁאֵין מוֹתָרָן בָּא פֶּסַח – אִם שְׁחָטָם לְשֵׁם פֶּסַח בִּזְמַנּוֹ, אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁיְּהוּ פְּסוּלִין?!
> **Translation:** **And** if so, one may infer *a fortiori*: **Just as** with regard to **a Paschal offering, whose leftover** offering **is brought** as **a peace offering,** and nevertheless, if one **slaughtered it at its** designated **time for the sake of a peace offering** it is **unfit,** so too, with regard to **a peace offering, whose leftover** offering **is not brought** as **a Paschal offering, if** one **slaughtered** it **for the sake of a Paschal offering at its** designated **time, isn’t it logical that** it **should be unfit?**


Rabbi Eliezer then presents a different principle. *Matzinu motar pesach ba-shelamim v'ein motar shelamim ba-pesach* — we find that the surplus designated for a *pesach* offering may become a *shelamim*, but the surplus designated for a *shelamim* may never become a *pesach*. The *pesach* is a subcategory within the broader family of *kodashim kalim* — and like any subcategory, it can be absorbed upward into the general category (*shelamim*), but the general category cannot be pressed downward into it. If a person set aside funds for a *pesach* and had money left over, those surplus funds may be directed toward a *shelamim*; but funds designated for a *shelamim* cannot be redirected to acquire a *pesach*. From this asymmetry Rabbi Eliezer argues: *u'mah pesach shemotaro ba-shelamim shechato bizmano leshum shelamim pasul* — even the *pesach* whose surplus becomes *shelamim*, if it is slaughtered at its proper time with the intent of *shelamim*, is *pasul*. How much more so would *shelamim*, whose surplus can never become *pesach*, be *pasul* if slaughtered at the time of *pesach* for the sake of *pesach*.

### Chatat-Olah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לוֹ רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: מָצִינוּ מוֹתַר חַטָּאת בָּא עוֹלָה, וְאֵין מוֹתַר עוֹלָה בָּא חַטָּאת;
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yehoshua said to him:** This inference is not valid, as **we find** that **a leftover sin offering,** e.g., one bought with money left over from funds dedicated to the acquisition of a sin offering, **is brought** as **a burnt offering,** whereas **a leftover burnt offering is not brought** as **a sin offering.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמָה חַטָּאת, שֶׁמּוֹתָרָהּ בָּא עוֹלָה – שְׁחָטָהּ לְשֵׁם עוֹלָה, פְּסוּלָה; עוֹלָה, שֶׁאֵין מוֹתָרָהּ בָּא חַטָּאת – שְׁחָטָהּ לְשֵׁם חַטָּאת, אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁתְּהֵא פְּסוּלָה?!
> **Translation:** **And** according to Rabbi Eliezer’s logic, one should derive *a fortiori*: **Just as** with regard to **a sin offering, whose leftover is brought** as **a burnt offering,** nevertheless, if one **slaughtered it for the sake of a burnt offering** it is **unfit,** so too, with regard to **a burnt offering, whose leftover is not brought** as **a sin offering,** if one **slaughtered it for the sake of a sin offering, isn’t it logical that it should be unfit?** Yet, the *halakha* is that a burnt offering slaughtered for the sake of a sin offering is fit.

Rabbi Yehoshua counters with a parallel case. *Matzinu motar chatat ba-olah v'ein motar olah ba-chatat* — we find that the surplus designated for a *chatat* may become an *olah*, but the surplus designated for an *olah* can never become a *chatat*. (The *olah* is completely burned; there is no surplus left to redirect, and furthermore the *chatat* is female while the *olah* is typically male — they cannot even be the same animal.) If the *chatat*, whose surplus goes to an *olah*, is nonetheless *psulah* when slaughtered for the sake of an *olah*, then by the same logic, the *olah*, whose surplus cannot come to a *chatat*, would certainly be *psulah* if slaughtered for the sake of a *chatat*. Rabbi Yehoshua is testing whether Rabbi Eliezer's framework yields consistent and defensible results when applied to other pairs of offerings.

### Time-Specific Rule

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לוֹ רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר: לֹא; אִם אָמַרְתָּ בְּחַטָּאת – שֶׁכֵּן כְּשֵׁרָה לִשְׁמָהּ כׇּל הַשָּׁנָה כּוּלָּהּ, תֹּאמַר בַּפֶּסַח – שֶׁאֵינוֹ כָּשֵׁר לִשְׁמוֹ אֶלָּא בִּזְמַנּוֹ?! הוֹאִיל וְהוּא פָּסוּל לִשְׁמוֹ, דִּין הוּא שֶׁיִּפָּסְלוּ אֲחֵרִים לִשְׁמוֹ.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Eliezer said to him: No, if you said** that this is the *halakha* **with regard to a sin offering,** that another offering sacrificed for its sake is fit, this is only **because** a sin offering **is fit** when sacrificed **for its sake all year long. Shall you** also **say** that this is the *halakha* **with regard to a Paschal offering, which is fit** when sacrificed **for its sake only at its** designated **time? Since** a Paschal offering **is unfit** when sacrificed **for its sake** during the rest of the year, **it is logical that other** offerings **should be disqualified** when sacrificed **for its sake** at its designated time.

Rabbi Eliezer offers his final and decisive principle. *Lo, im amarta b'chatat sheken k'sheirah lishmah kol hashanah kulah, tomar b'pesach she'eino kasher lishmah ela bizmano* — you cannot reason from the *chatat* to the *pesach*, because the *chatat* is valid for its own sake throughout the entire year. The *pesach*, by contrast, is valid for its own sake only at its designated time. Since the *pesach* offering is *pasul* for its own sake during the rest of the year, it follows logically that other offerings done for its sake at its designated time are equally *pasul*. The *pesach*'s identity is inseparable from its time; only what belongs to that time may participate in it.

## Offering Hierarchy



### Hierarchy Rule

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שִׁמְעוֹן אֲחִי עֲזַרְיָה אוֹמֵר כּוּ׳.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **Shimon, brother of Azarya, says** that this is the distinction: With regard to all offerings, if one slaughtered them for the sake of an offering whose sanctity is greater than theirs, they are fit; if one slaughtered them for the sake of an offering whose sanctity is less than theirs, they are unfit.

The *Gemara* now returns to the *mishnah* and the position of Shimon *achi* Azariah, which was cited at the opening of the tractate (2a). His rule: *shechatan l'shem gavoha meihen k'sheirim, l'shem namuch meihen psulim* — if lower-sanctity offerings are slaughtered for the sake of higher-sanctity offerings, they are *kasher*; if higher-sanctity offerings are slaughtered for the sake of lower-sanctity offerings, they are *pasul*. The hierarchy of sanctity operates as a one-way street: ascending is permitted, descending is not.

### Desecration Verse

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אָשֵׁי מַתְנֵי מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן, וְרַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרָבָא מַתְנֵי מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי יַנַּאי: מַאי טַעְמָא דְּשִׁמְעוֹן אֲחִי עֲזַרְיָה? דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״וְלֹא יְחַלְּלוּ אֶת קׇדְשֵׁי בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל אֵת אֲשֶׁר יָרִימוּ לַה׳״ – בְּמוּרָם מֵהֶן אֵין מִתְחַלְּלִין, בְּנָמוּךְ מֵהֶם מִתְחַלְּלִין.
> **Translation:** **Rav Ashi teaches in the name of Rabbi Yoḥanan, and Rav Aḥa, son of Rava, teaches** the same statement **in the name of Rabbi Yannai: What is the reason** for the opinion **of Shimon, brother of Azarya?** It is derived from that **which the verse states: “And they shall not profane the holy things of the children of Israel, which they will set apart [*yarimu*] unto the Lord”** (Leviticus 22:15). The verse is interpreted not as a prohibition but as a halakhic axiom, teaching that offerings **are not profaned,** i.e., disqualified, **by** sacrifice for the sake of offerings whose sanctity is **more elevated [*muram*] than theirs; they are profaned** only **by** sacrifice for the sake of offerings whose sanctity is **less than theirs.**

The source for this rule is a verse in Vayikra 22:15: *v'lo yechalelu et kodshei bnei Yisrael et asher yarimu l'Hashem* — and they shall not desecrate the holy things of the children of Israel that they shall lift up (*yarimu*) for *Hashem*. The word *yarimu*, from the root of *terumah* — to lift up — is read to teach: what is desecrated (*mechullal*) is that which is brought downward (*namuch*); what is lifted upward (*gavoha*) is not subject to desecration. The verse is transmitted in two versions: Rav Ashi teaches it in the name of Rabbi Yochanan, and Rav Acha son of Rava teaches it in the name of Rabbi Yannai.

### Shmuel's Use


The same verse, however, is also the basis for a teaching of Shmuel in an entirely different context. Shmuel derives from it that one who eats *tevel* — produce from which *terumot* and *ma'asrot* have not yet been separated — is liable to the death penalty. The verse reads: *v'lo yechalelu et kodshei bnei Yisrael asher yarimu l'Hashem* — the holy things of Israel which they *will* separate for *Hashem*. Shmuel reads *yarimu* as a future tense referring to what is *destined* to be set apart; the *tevel* is already designated in principle for *terumah*, and one who eats it before that separation has been made has desecrated something consecrated. The *Gemara* notes that the verse is needed for Shmuel's derivation and asks how it can simultaneously be available for Shimon *achi* Azariah's hierarchy principle.

### Tense Lesson

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַאי לְהָכִי הוּא דַּאֲתָא?! וְהָא מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ לִכְדִשְׁמוּאֵל – דְּאָמַר שְׁמוּאֵל: מִנַּיִן לָאוֹכֵל אֶת הַטֶּבֶל שֶׁהוּא בְּמִיתָה? שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְלֹא יְחַלְּלוּ אֶת קׇדְשֵׁי בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל אֵת אֲשֶׁר יָרִימוּ לַה׳״ – בַּעֲתִידִים לִתְרוֹם הַכָּתוּב מְדַבֵּר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And does that** verse **come to** teach **this** *halakha*? **But isn’t it necessary for Shmuel’s** derivation? **As Shmuel says: From where** is it derived **that one who eats untithed produce is** liable **to** be punished with **death** at the hand of Heaven? **As it is stated: “And they shall not profane the holy things of the children of Israel, which they will set apart unto the Lord.”** The term: “Will set apart,” indicates that **the verse is speaking of** holy items **that are to be set apart in the future,** i.e., untithed produce, which includes the portion designated for the priest [*teruma*].

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם כֵּן, לִכְתּוֹב קְרָא ״אֲשֶׁר הוּרְמוּ״; מַאי ״אֲשֶׁר יָרִימוּ״? שְׁמַע מִינַּיהּ תַּרְתֵּי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **If so,** if **the verse** taught only one *halakha*, the *halakha* of Shimon, brother of Azarya, it **should have written: Which they have set apart. What** is the reason the verse states: **“Which they will set apart,”** in the future tense? **Conclude from it** that the verse teaches **two** *halakhot*: An offering sacrificed for the sake of an offering of greater sanctity is fit, and consumption of untithed produce is punishable by death at the hand of Heaven.

The resolution lies in the verb tense. The verse uses the future form *yarimu* — "they will lift up" — when, if it were speaking only of a past act, it should have said *hurmu*, "that which has been lifted." The use of the future tense is therefore intentional and carries extra meaning. *Shema mina tartei* — we learn two things from it: one governing past consecrated items (Shimon *achi* Azariah's hierarchy rule applied to offerings already sanctified) and one governing future ones (Shmuel's ruling about *tevel*, which is destined for sanctification but not yet separated). A single verb in a single tense is thus pressed into service for two distinct *halachic* domains.

## Valid Not Accepted



### Zeira's Query

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בָּעֵי רַבִּי זֵירָא: כְּשֵׁרִין וְאֵין מְרַצִּין – וְכִי פְּלִיג בַּחֲדָא פְּלִיג, אוֹ דִלְמָא כְּשֵׁרִין וּמְרַצִּין – וּבְתַרְתֵּי פְּלִיגִי?
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira raises a dilemma:** According to Shimon, brother of Azaria, who says that less sacred offerings sacrificed for the sake of more sacred offerings are fit, perhaps they **are fit but do not propitiate** God, i.e., they do not fulfill the owner’s obligation, as the Rabbis hold. If so, **when** Shimon, brother of Azaria, **disagrees** with the first *tanna* in the mishna, **he disagrees** only **with regard to one** matter, i.e., that more sacred offerings sacrificed for the sake of less sacred ones are disqualified altogether. **Alternatively, perhaps** Shimon, brother of Azaria, holds that if less sacred offerings are sacrificed for the sake of more sacred offerings they are both **fit and propitiate** God, **and they disagree** with the Rabbis **about two** matters.


Rabbi Zeira raises a pointed question regarding Shimon *achi* Azariah's position. When Shimon holds that lower-sanctity offerings slaughtered for the sake of higher-sanctity ones are *kasher*, does he mean they are *kasher v'ein mirtzin* — valid but not accepted, meaning the owner has not fulfilled his obligation — and that Shimon is disagreeing with the Rabbis on only one matter (the *kasher* status of such an offering)? Or does Shimon hold that such offerings are both *kasher u'mirtzin* — valid and accepted — meaning they fully discharge the owner's obligation, and Shimon therefore disagrees with the Rabbis on two matters?

### Two Positions

The question opens up a three-part logical space. An offering can be *kasher* and fulfill the obligation (*kasher u'mirtzin*); it can be *kasher* but not fulfill the obligation (*kasher v'ein mirtzin*); or it can be *pasul* entirely. Whether Shimon's leniency extends to acceptance and obligation-fulfillment, or only to the technical validity of the sacrifice, determines the full scope of his disagreement with the Rabbis.

### Bechor Example


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי, וְאִיתֵּימָא רַבִּי זְרִיקָא, תָּא שְׁמַע: הַבְּכוֹר וְהַמַּעֲשֵׂר שֶׁשְּׁחָטָן לְשֵׁם שְׁלָמִים – כְּשֵׁרִים. שְׁלָמִים שֶׁשְּׁחָטָן לְשֵׁם בְּכוֹר אוֹ לְשֵׁם מַעֲשֵׂר – פְּסוּלִין.
> **Translation:** **Abaye, and some say Rabbi Zerika, said: Come** and **hear** proof that they do not satisfy the owners’ obligation: Shimon, brother of Azarya, elaborated on his opinion in the mishna: **A firstborn** animal **and the** animal **tithe that one slaughtered for the sake of peace offerings are fit,** as the sanctity of peace offerings is greater. **Peace offerings that one slaughtered for the sake of a firstborn** animal **or for the sake of** the animal **tithe are unfit.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי סָלְקָא דַעְתָּךְ כְּשֵׁרִין וּמְרַצִּין, בְּכוֹר בַּר רַיצּוֹיֵי הוּא?! אֶלָּא כְּשֵׁרִין וְאֵין מְרַצִּין! וּמִדְּסֵיפָא כְּשֵׁרִין וְאֵין מְרַצִּין, רֵישָׁא נָמֵי כְּשֵׁרִין וְאֵין מְרַצִּין!
> **Translation:** **And if it enters your mind** that offerings sacrificed for the sake of offerings of greater sanctity are both **fit and propitiate** God, **is a firstborn** an offering that **propitiates?** Sacrificing a firstborn does not serve to propitiate God; **rather,** conclude that such offerings **are fit but do not propitiate. And** one can infer that **since** in **the latter clause they are fit but do not propitiate,** in **the former clause as well,** concerning all offerings of lesser sanctity slaughtered for the sake of offerings of the most sacred order, **they are fit but do not propitiate.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִידֵּי אִירְיָא?! הָא כִּדְאִיתַהּ, וְהָא כִּדְאִיתַהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **Are the cases comparable? This** case is **as it is, and that** case is **as it is.** Perhaps Shimon, brother of Azarya, holds that offerings of lesser sanctity slaughtered for the sake of offerings of the most sacred order propitiate God, but a firstborn animal and animal tithe slaughtered for the sake of peace offerings do not propitiate God.

Abaye — or, as some attribute it, Rabbi Zrika — brings a *ta shema* to probe the question. The *mishnah* teaches: a *bechor*, firstborn animal, or *ma'aser beheimah*, tithe animal, that was slaughtered with the intent of *shelamim*, is *kasher*. These are lower in the holiness hierarchy than *shelamim* proper, and slaughtering them for the sake of the higher offering is permitted.

### Reverse Invalid


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מַאי קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן? גָּבוֹהַּ וְנָמוּךְ קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן?! תְּנֵינָא: כֵּיצַד? קׇדְשֵׁי קָדָשִׁים שֶׁשְּׁחָטָן לְשֵׁם קֳדָשִׁים קַלִּים כּוּ׳!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Rather, what is** the latter clause concerning a firstborn and animal tithe **teaching us,** if not that no offerings of lesser sanctity propitiate God when sacrificed for the sake of offerings of greater sanctity? How is the clause not redundant? **Does it teach us** an example of offerings of **higher** sanctity **and** offerings of **lower** sanctity? **We** already **learn** such an example in the former clause, which teaches: **How so? Offerings of the most sacred order that one slaughtered for the sake of offerings of lesser sanctity** are unfit; offerings of lesser sanctity slaughtered for the sake of offerings of the most sacred order are fit.

The same *mishnah* continues: *shelamim* slaughtered for the sake of a *bechor* or *ma'aser* are *pasul* — the higher for the lower is disqualified, consistent with Shimon's rule. The *Gemara* then asks: if we were to entertain the possibility that the lower-for-higher case yields both *kasher* and *meratzeh* — fully acceptable — then is the *bechor* the kind of offering that can achieve *ritzuy*, acceptance? The *bechor* is not an offering chosen by the owner for the purpose of pleasing *Hashem* in the sense of fulfilling an elected obligation; it is an offering mandated by the firstborn status of the animal. It therefore does not function as a vehicle of *ritzuy* in the relevant sense. The conclusion follows: the *bechor* slaughtered for the sake of *shelamim* is *kasher v'ein mirtzin* — valid but not accepted.

### Hierarchy Only

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַהוּ דְּתֵימָא: קׇדְשֵׁי קָדָשִׁים וְקָדָשִׁים קַלִּים הוּא דְּאִיכָּא גָּבוֹהַּ וְנָמוּךְ, אֲבָל קַלִּים וְקַלִּים – לָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: It is stated **lest you say that** the *halakha* of offerings of **higher** sanctity **and** offerings of **lower** sanctity **applies only** when the difference is between **offerings of the most sacred order and offerings of lesser sanctity, but** with regard to offerings of **lesser** sanctity **and** other offerings of **lesser** sanctity this *halakha* does **not** apply, as they are all considered to possess the same level of sanctity. Therefore the mishna adds the example of a firstborn animal and animal tithe slaughtered for the sake of peace offerings, to clarify that one can distinguish between different offerings of lesser sanctity as well.

From the concluding clause, the *Gemara* infers that the opening clause must be read consistently: the *bechor* slaughtered for *shelamim* is also *kasher v'ein mirtzin*. The principle that emerges is that the hierarchy rule governs validity (*kashrus*) but does not automatically generate full acceptance (*ritzuy*). Shimon *achi* Azariah's leniency, on this reading, concerns only whether the sacrifice is *pasul* — not whether it discharges the owner's obligation.

## Order Precedence



### Shelamim Priority

The *Gemara* then raises another *mishnah*, which teaches that *shelamim* take precedence over *bechor* and *ma'aser* when a *kohen* must decide which offering to perform first. The reason: *shelamim* require additional services that *bechor* and *ma'aser* do not.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא נָמֵי תְּנֵינָא: שְׁלָמִים קוֹדְמִים אֶת הַבְּכוֹר, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהֵן טְעוּנִין מַתַּן אַרְבַּע, וּסְמִיכָה, וּנְסָכִים, וּתְנוּפַת חָזֶה וָשׁוֹק!
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises an objection to this answer: **We** already **learn this as well,** in another mishna (89a): The **peace offering precedes** sacrifice of **the firstborn because** the peace offering **requires placement** of its blood twice so as to be visible on **four** sides of the altar, **placing hands** on the head of the offering, **libations, and waving of** the **breast and thigh** by the priest and the owner, none of which is required for the firstborn.

### Shelamim Services


The services unique to *shelamim* are enumerated: *matan arba'* — the four applications of blood to the corners of the altar; *semikhah* — the leaning of hands upon the offering; *nesachim* — the wine libations; and *tenufat chazeh v'shok* — the waving of the chest and thigh. Because *shelamim* require this more elaborate service, they are given priority so that the *kohen* may complete their more demanding *avodah* while fully attentive.

### Hierarchy Primary

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא עִיקָּר, הָהִיא אַגְּרָרָא נַסְבַהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: The **primary** mention of the distinction is in **this** mishna; **that** mishna **cites it incidentally,** in discussing the order of precedence of offerings.

The *Gemara* notes that the primary and authoritative statement of the hierarchy principle between higher and lower offerings appears in the earlier *mishnah* addressed in the main discussion, not in this later one. The second *mishnah*, which addresses the order of precedence among offerings of comparable status, invokes the hierarchy principle only incidentally — in the course of explaining why *shelamim* precede *bechor* in the queue. The foundational rule, however, is the one derived from Shimon *achi* Azariah and grounded in the verse of *yarimu*, and it is to that earlier *mishnah* that the primary statement properly belongs.
