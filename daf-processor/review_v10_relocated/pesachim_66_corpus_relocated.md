## Pesach on Shabbat



### Intro & Debate

Daf 66 opens immediately after the *mishnah*, which had catalogued what aspects of *shabbat* may be overridden when the fourteenth of Nisan falls on *shabbat*. The *mishnah* had framed this primarily as a dispute between Rabbi Eliezer on one side, who took the radical position that virtually anything necessary to enable the *korban pesach* may be done even if it could have been prepared the day before, and Rabbi Yehoshua together with his student Rabbi Akiva on the other. At the end of the *mishnah*, Rabbi Akiva had cited the word *b'moado* — "in its appointed time" — as the scriptural basis for bringing the *korban pesach* on *shabbat* regardless of when the fourteenth falls.

The *gemara*, however, does not begin by analyzing that dispute. Instead it steps back to a more fundamental question: how do we know in the first place that *pesach* overrides *shabbat* at all? The answer emerges through a remarkable historical narrative.

### B'nei Beteira Forget


*Tanu rabbanan* — our Rabbis taught: *halacha zu nitalma mi-B'nei Beteira* — this *halacha* that *pesach* may be brought on *shabbat* was lost to *B'nei Beteira*. *B'nei Beteira* were the major authorities at the *Beit HaMikdash* at the time of Hillel. Rashi identifies them as *nesi'im*. They appear elsewhere in the tradition as well — most famously in the story involving Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai at Yavneh, where *shabbat* fell on Rosh Hashanah and *B'nei Beteira* argued that the *shofar* could only be blown on *shabbat* in the *Beit HaMikdash*, which no longer existed.

One might wonder how such a basic *halacha* could have been forgotten, given that *pesach* falls on *shabbat* on average once every seven years. It is possible that the leaders of that era found ways to manipulate the calendar to avoid the situation; in any case, the *gemara* states plainly that when the fourteenth fell on *shabbat* that year, they had simply forgotten — *shachachu v'lo yadu im pesach doche et hashabbat im lav* — whether *pesach* overrides *shabbat* or not.

They asked around: does anyone know whether *pesach* overrides *shabbat*? They were told: there is a certain man who has recently come up from Bavel — *Hillel HaBavli* — who spent time attending upon the two great leaders of the generation, Shemaya and Avtalyon, and he would know. The designation *Hillel HaBavli* is striking, since we do not normally know him by that name; Hillel's family was connected to the *nesi'im* and the *Reish Galuta*, yet the tradition here identifies him as coming from Bavel. They sent for him and summoned him.

They asked: do you know whether *pesach* overrides *shabbat*? His opening response is unforgettable: *v'chi pesach echad yesh lanu bashanah shedoche et hashabbat* — what, do we have only one *korban pesach* in a year that overrides *shabbat*? We have more than 200 sacrifices each year that override *shabbat*! The arithmetic is simple: two *tamid* offerings every day times roughly fifty *shabbatot* is one hundred, plus the *musaf* of *shabbat* — two additional animals — is another hundred. Add the *musaf* offerings of *yamim tovim* that fall on *shabbat* and you already exceed 200. The point is that the overriding of *shabbat* by fixed-time obligations is a thoroughly established principle in *avodah*.

### Hillel's Proof

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרוּ לוֹ: מִנַּיִן לְךָ? אָמַר לָהֶם: נֶאֱמַר ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ בַּפֶּסַח, וְנֶאֱמַר ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ בַּתָּמִיד: מָה ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ הָאָמוּר בַּתָּמִיד דּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת, אַף ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ הָאָמוּר בַּפֶּסַח דּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** **They said to him: From where do you** know this? **He said to them: “Its appointed time” is stated with regard to the Paschal lamb and “its appointed time” is** also **stated with regard to the daily offering,** for the verse says: “Command the children of Israel and say to them, My offering, the provision of My sacrifice made with fire, for a sweet savor to Me, shall you observe to offer Me at its appointed time” (Numbers 28:2). From here we learn that the daily offering is brought even on Shabbat. Thus, the daily morning and afternoon offerings are brought on more than fifty *Shabbatot* over the course of the year, and two sheep are offered every Shabbat as additional offerings, for a total of more than two hundred sacrifices a year that override Shabbat. **Just as** the expression **“its appointed time,” which is stated with regard to the daily offering,** indicates that it **overrides Shabbat, so too “its appointed time,” which is stated with regard to the Paschal lamb,** indicates that it **overrides Shabbat.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעוֹד, קַל וָחוֹמֶר הוּא: וּמָה תָּמִיד שֶׁאֵין עָנוּשׁ כָּרֵת דּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת, פֶּסַח שֶׁעָנוּשׁ כָּרֵת — אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁדּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** **And furthermore, it is an *a fortiori*** inference: **If the daily offering,** the neglect of which **is not punishable by *karet*, overrides Shabbat, is it not right that the Paschal lamb,** the neglect of which **is punishable by *karet*, should override Shabbat?**

Hillel then proceeded to lay out his formal proofs. He said: *ne'emar moado b'Pesach v'ne'emar moado b'tamid* — the word *mo'ado*, "in its appointed time," appears both by the *korban pesach* and by the *tamid* daily offering. Just as *mo'ado* by the *tamid* indicates that it overrides *shabbat*, so too *mo'ado* by the *pesach* indicates the same.

But there is an immediate difficulty: where do we know that *tamid* itself overrides *shabbat*? The word *mo'ado* appears there too, so if *mo'ado* alone established the override, we would not need to derive *pesach* from *tamid* — *pesach* has its own *mo'ado*. The *gemara* will address this in detail later, but for now, Hillel is presenting both a *gezerah shavah* — a verbal analogy linking the shared word — and, additionally, a *kal vachomer*: if the *tamid*, for which there is no punishment of *karet* if one fails to bring it, overrides *shabbat*, then certainly the *pesach*, for which neglect carries the penalty of *karet*, should override *shabbat*. This *kal vachomer* added independent logical weight beyond the verbal link.

### Hillel Becomes Nasi

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִיָּד הוֹשִׁיבוּהוּ בָּרֹאשׁ, וּמִינּוּהוּ נָשִׂיא עֲלֵיהֶם, וְהָיָה דּוֹרֵשׁ כׇּל הַיּוֹם כּוּלּוֹ בְּהִלְכוֹת הַפֶּסַח. הִתְחִיל מְקַנְטְרָן בִּדְבָרִים. אָמַר לָהֶן: מִי גָּרַם לָכֶם שֶׁאֶעְלֶה מִבָּבֶל וְאֶהְיֶה נָשִׂיא עֲלֵיכֶם — עַצְלוּת שֶׁהָיְתָה בָּכֶם, שֶׁלֹּא שִׁמַּשְׁתֶּם שְׁנֵי גְּדוֹלֵי הַדּוֹר, שְׁמַעְיָה וְאַבְטַלְיוֹן.
> **Translation:** After Hillel brought these proofs, **they immediately seated him at the head and appointed him *Nasi* over them, and he expounded the laws of Passover that entire day.** In the course of his teaching, **he began rebuking them [*mekanteran*] them with words. He said to them: What caused** this to happen **to you, that I should come up from Babylonia and become *Nasi* over you?** It was **the laziness in you** that **you did not serve the two** most **eminent** scholars **of the generation** living in Eretz Yisrael, **Shemaya and Avtalyon.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרוּ לוֹ: רַבִּי, שָׁכַח וְלֹא הֵבִיא סַכִּין מֵעֶרֶב שַׁבָּת, מַהוּ? אָמַר לָהֶן: הֲלָכָה זוֹ שָׁמַעְתִּי וְשָׁכַחְתִּי, אֶלָּא הַנַּח לָהֶן לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, אִם אֵין נְבִיאִים הֵן — בְּנֵי נְבִיאִים הֵן.
> **Translation:** **They said to** Hillel: **Our teacher,** if one **forgot and did not bring a knife on the eve of Shabbat** and cannot slaughter his Paschal lamb, **what is** the law? Since he could have brought the knife before Shabbat, he cannot bring it on Shabbat; but what should he do in this situation? **He said to them: I** once **heard this *halakha*** from my teachers **but I have forgotten** it. **But leave** it **to the Jewish people; if they are not prophets** to whom God has revealed His secrets, **they are the sons of prophets,** and will certainly do the right thing on their own.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְמָחָר, מִי שֶׁפִּסְחוֹ טָלֶה — תּוֹחֲבוֹ בְּצַמְרוֹ, מִי שֶׁפִּסְחוֹ גְּדִי — תּוֹחֲבוֹ בֵּין קַרְנָיו. רָאָה מַעֲשֶׂה וְנִזְכַּר הֲלָכָה, וְאָמַר: כָּךְ מְקוּבְּלַנִי מִפִּי שְׁמַעְיָה וְאַבְטַלְיוֹן.
> **Translation:** **The next day,** on Shabbat that was the eve of Passover, **one whose Paschal offering was a lamb** took the knife and **stuck it in its wool;** and **one whose Paschal offering was a goat,** which does not have wool, **stuck it between its horns.** Hillel **saw the incident and remembered the *halakha*** that he had once learned **and said: This** is the tradition **I received from the mouths of Shemaya and Avtalyon,** meaning that this is in fact the proper course of action. This concludes the text of the *baraita* and the Gemara will begin to elucidate it.

The reaction was immediate: *miyad hoshivuhu b'rosh u'minuhu nasi aleihem* — they placed him at their head and appointed him *nasi* over them. He then spent the entire day expounding the laws of *pesach*, both to educate the people and to demonstrate his newly established authority.

Then, pointedly, he began to taunt them: *mi garam lachem she'e'eleh miBavel v'ehyeh nasi aleihem* — what caused this, that I, an outsider, had to come from Bavel to be your leader? *Atzlut shebachem* — your laziness. You did not do as I did; you did not devote yourself to attending upon and truly learning from the great masters of the generation, Shemaya and Avtalyon.

At that moment — and as the narrative makes clear, this is where the element of *comeuppance* enters — they turned to him with a practical question. *Rabbi*, they now called him, deferentially: what is the law if someone forgot to bring his slaughtering knife to the *Beit HaMikdash* before *shabbat*? Could he bring it on *shabbat* itself? This was, as the *gemara* will later connect, precisely the question debated by Rabbi Eliezer — whether preparatory acts for the *korban pesach* override *shabbat*.

Hillel's answer is arresting: *halacha zo shamati v'shachachti* — I heard this *halacha*, and I have forgotten it. There it is — the man who had just mocked the leaders for their failure to learn properly from Shemaya and Avtalyon now cannot remember what he himself heard from Shemaya and Avtalyon.

But Hillel did not leave them without guidance. *Hanach lahem l'Yisrael*, he said — leave it to the Jewish people. *Im einam nevi'im hem b'nei nevi'im hem* — if they are not themselves prophets, they are at least the descendants of prophets. They will have the religious intuition to work it out correctly.

The following day — which was the fourteenth itself, a *shabbat* — this is precisely what the people did. Those whose *pesach* was a sheep embedded the knife in the animal's wool; those whose *pesach* was a goat stuck it between the animal's horns. The animal would then walk itself to the *Beit HaMikdash* carrying the knife, without the person having performed the act of carrying. When Hillel saw what they were doing, he remembered the *halacha*: *kach mekublani mipi Shemaya v'Avtalyon* — this is exactly the tradition I received from Shemaya and Avtalyon.

There is something striking and beautiful about this episode. The *gemara* elsewhere employs the principle of *puk chazei mai ama davar* — if you do not know the *halacha*, go and see what the people are doing, and take your cue from established popular practice. But that principle relies on an existing practice already being observed. Here no one knew what to do; there was no established practice. Hillel was trusting not existing practice but the people's forward-looking religious intuition — *im einam nevi'im hem b'nei nevi'im hem*. That is a considerably stronger statement. There are *teshuvot* that invoke this phrase, though typically in the sense of "since they have been doing it, we may assume they are right" — a kind of retroactive validation of existing practice. The use of the phrase here, to mean "let us see what they will do and trust they will arrive at the truth," is rarer and more arresting.

One might also note how *B'nei Beteira* address Hillel here: *rebbi*. We do not normally encounter the title "Rabbi Hillel." The *zugot*, the pairs of leaders, were not called *rabbis* in the technical sense; the title had not yet come into that usage. When *B'nei Beteira* say *rebbi*, they mean it as a deferential honorific — "my master" — not as a formal designation.

## Knife on Shabbat



### Animal Carries Knife

The *gemara* now turns to a closely related issue that the narrative raises. Having described how the animal could carry the knife — embedded in the wool of the sheep or tied between the horns of the goat — the *gemara* notes that this itself presents a problem: one is not permitted to have an animal perform *melacha* and carry for you on *shabbat* either. This is something the *gemara* will address in its fuller treatment ahead.

There is also a notable textual detail worth pausing on before moving forward. The *gemara* mentions in passing that a certain *posek* ends a *teshuva* with the phrase *ella hanach lahem l'Yisrael v'chulu* — and a debate arose over what he meant by that *v'chulu*, that "and so on." Did he intend *hanach lahem l'Yisrael im einam nevi'im hem b'nei nevi'im hem* — leave it to Israel, for if they are not prophets they are descendants of prophets, and we may therefore assume they are doing the right thing? Or did he mean *hanach lahem l'Yisrael mutav sheyiheyu shogegin v'al yehu mezidin* — leave it to Israel, for it is better they sin unwittingly than deliberately — which is an entirely different *hanach lahem l'Yisrael*, one that carries no validation of the practice at all? The ambiguity is telling: the two phrases begin identically but arrive at opposite conclusions about the people's reliability.

## Gemara Analysis



### Gezerah Shavah Analysis

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר: נֶאֱמַר ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ בַּפֶּסַח, וְנֶאֱמַר ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ בְּתָמִיד, מָה ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ הָאָמוּר בַּתָּמִיד דּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת, אַף ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ הָאָמוּר בַּפֶּסַח דּוֹחֶה שַׁבָּת. וְתָמִיד גּוּפֵיהּ מְנָלַן דְּדָחֵי שַׁבָּת? אִילֵּימָא מִשּׁוּם דִּכְתִיב בֵּיהּ ״בְּמוֹעֲדוֹ״ — פֶּסַח נָמֵי הָא כְּתִיב בֵּיהּ ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״!
> **Translation:** **The Master said** above: **“Its appointed time” is stated with regard to the Paschal lamb and “its appointed time” is stated with regard to the daily offering. Just as “its appointed time,” which is stated with regard to the daily offering,** indicates that it **overrides Shabbat, so too “its appointed time,” which is stated with regard to the Paschal lamb,** indicates that it **overrides Shabbat. And from where do we** derive **that the daily offering itself overrides Shabbat? If we say because “in its appointed time” is written in its regard, “in its appointed time” is also written with regard to the Paschal lamb.** Were it possible to derive from this expression that the sacrifice is offered even on Shabbat, it would not be necessary to derive the law governing the Paschal lamb from a verbal analogy between the daily offering and the Paschal lamb.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ לָא מַשְׁמַע לֵיהּ. הָכָא נָמֵי ״מוֹעֲדוֹ״ לָא מַשְׁמַע לֵיהּ. אֶלָּא אָמַר קְרָא: ״עוֹלַת שַׁבָּת בְּשַׁבַּתּוֹ עַל עוֹלַת הַתָּמִיד״ — מִכְּלָל [עוֹלָה] דְּתָמִיד קְרֵבָה בְּשַׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** you must conclude that the expression **“its appointed time,”** which is stated with regard to the Paschal lamb, **does not indicate to** Hillel that the Torah was so particular about the timing of the Paschal lamb that its slaughter overrides Shabbat. **Here too,** with regard to the daily offering, you must say that **“its appointed time” does not indicate to him** that it is brought on Shabbat, and so this expression is not the source of this law. **Rather,** the law is derived from **the verse** that **states: “The burnt-offering of Shabbat on its Shabbat, beside the continual burnt-offering** and its libation” (Numbers 28:10), from which **it may be inferred that the daily burnt-offering is brought** even **on Shabbat.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר: וְעוֹד קַל וָחוֹמֶר, וּמָה תָּמִיד שֶׁאֵין עָנוּשׁ כָּרֵת דּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת, פֶּסַח שֶׁעָנוּשׁ כָּרֵת — אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁדּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת. אִיכָּא לְמִיפְרַךְ: מָה לְתָמִיד שֶׁכֵּן תָּדִיר וְכָלִיל. קַל וָחוֹמֶר אֲמַר לְהוּ בְּרֵישָׁא וּפַרְכוּהּ, וַהֲדַר אֲמַר לְהוּ גְּזֵירָה שָׁוָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises another question: **The Master said** in that same *baraita*: **And furthermore, it is an *a fortiori* inference: If the daily offering,** the neglect of which **is not punishable by *karet*, overrides Shabbat, is it not right that the Paschal lamb,** the neglect of which **is punishable by *karet*, should override Shabbat?** The Gemara points out that **there is room to refute** the logic of this argument: **What** is unique about **the daily offering** that enables it to override Shabbat? **That it is frequent,** and something that is frequent always takes precedence; **and** also that it is totally **consumed** on the altar, unlike the Paschal lamb, most of which is eaten by human beings. The Gemara explains that this is what happened: Hillel **first told them the *a fortiori* inference, but they refuted it** and proved that it was not reliable, as explained above; **and then he told them the verbal analogy,** and a verbal analogy is based on an oral tradition originating from Moses at Sinai and must be accepted.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי מֵאַחַר דְּגָמַר גְּזֵירָה שָׁוָה, קַל וָחוֹמֶר לְמָה לִי? אֶלָּא לְדִידְהוּ קָאָמַר לְהוּ: בִּשְׁלָמָא גְּזֵירָה שָׁוָה לָא גָּמְרִיתוּ, דְּאֵין אָדָם דָּן גְּזֵירָה שָׁוָה מֵעַצְמוֹ. אֶלָּא קַל וָחוֹמֶר, דְּאָדָם דָּן מֵעַצְמוֹ, אִיבְּעִי לְכוּ לְמֵידָן! אֲמַרוּ לֵיהּ: קַל וָחוֹמֶר פְּרִיכָא הוּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But since** Hillel **learned** this **verbal analogy** from his teachers, **why do I** need **an *a fortiori*** inference? Why did he add a logical argument of his own if he had an explicit verbal tradition that this was the *halakha*? The Gemara answers: **Rather, he said it for them,** to show that they had not sufficiently exerted themselves in clarifying this *halakha*: **Granted, you did not learn the verbal analogy** on your own, because you acted according to the principle that **one may not expound a verbal analogy on one’s own.** Since there is no limit to the laws that one can extract using this method of derivation, such a derivation is only legitimate if it has been transmitted as part of the oral tradition, and apparently they did not learn this verbal analogy from their teachers. **But an *a fortiori*** inference, **which one can derive on one’s own, you should have derived** and you would then have known how to resolve this question. **They said to him: It is a faulty *a fortiori*** inference, as we have shown that it can be easily refuted.

The *gemara* now steps back to analyze the *baraita*. The *gezerah shavah* linking *pesach* and *tamid* through the shared word *mo'ado* raises an immediate question: where do we know that the *tamid* itself overrides *shabbat*? You cannot say the answer is simply the word *mo'ado* written by *tamid*, because *mo'ado* appears by *pesach* as well — in that case there would have been no need to derive *pesach* from *tamid* at all. The *gemara* concludes: *mo'ado lo mashma lei* — to Hillel, the word *mo'ado* by itself does not imply overriding *shabbat*. The basis for *tamid* overriding *shabbat* is rather the verse *olat Shabbat b'shabato al olat hatamid* — the *shabbat* offering is brought alongside the *tamid*, which means the *tamid* is itself offered on *shabbat*. From there, the *gezerah shavah* on *mo'ado* carries the rule over to *pesach*.

The *gemara* then turns to the *kal vachomer*: if the *tamid*, which carries no *karet*, overrides *shabbat*, certainly *pesach*, which carries *karet*, should. But this *kal vachomer* is immediately challenged: *mah l'tamid sheken tadir v'chalil* — the *tamid* is offered every single day and is entirely burnt, giving it a special character that may be what allows it to override *shabbat*. The *pesach*, brought only once a year with its meat eaten by people, may not be comparable. The *kal vachomer* is therefore *paricha* — refuted.

The *gemara* asks: if Hillel possessed a valid *gezerah shavah*, why did he open with a *kal vachomer* that turned out to be contradicted? The answer is *ella l'didehu ka'amar lehu* — he was arguing for their sake. A *gezerah shavah* is only effective for one who has received it as a tradition; *she'ein adam dan gezerah shavah me'atzmo* — you cannot construct one independently. Clearly *B'nei Beteira* lacked this tradition. A *kal vachomer*, by contrast, is something *adam dan me'atzmo* — one may derive it through one's own reasoning. Hillel was offering them a proof they could have arrived at themselves. When they successfully rebutted it, he presented the *gezerah shavah* as his own received tradition.

The result is striking: Hillel did not prove the *halacha* to *B'nei Beteira* by logical necessity. The *kal vachomer* failed; the *gezerah shavah* rests on a tradition they did not share. In the end they were simply accepting his word. The *Ramban* pushes this further, arguing that a *gezerah shavah* was never a tradition about which specific words could be linked — the tradition was the *halacha* itself, and the *gezerah shavah* served as a textual peg for what was already known. This touches on a broader question about the nature of *Torah she-be'al peh* that will be taken up more fully below.

## Knife on Shabbat (II)



### Melacha Ba-Kodashim

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר: לְמָחָר מִי שֶׁפִּסְחוֹ טָלֶה — תּוֹחֵב לוֹ בְּצַמְרוֹ, גְּדִי — תּוֹחֵב לוֹ בֵּין קַרְנָיו.
> **Translation:** **The Master said** further in the *baraita*: **The next day, one whose Paschal offering was a lamb stuck** the knife **in its wool,** and one whose Paschal offering was a **goat stuck it between its horns** so as to avoid carrying the knife on Shabbat.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא קָא עָבֵיד עֲבוֹדָה בְּקָדָשִׁים! כְּהִלֵּל. דְּתַנְיָא, אָמְרוּ עָלָיו עַל הִלֵּל: מִיָּמָיו לֹא מָעַל אָדָם בְּעוֹלָתוֹ. אֶלָּא מְבִיאָהּ חוּלִּין לָעֲזָרָה וּמַקְדִּישָׁהּ, וְסוֹמֵךְ יָדוֹ עָלֶיהָ וְשׁוֹחֲטָהּ.
> **Translation:** **But** surely **he did work with consecrated** animals, using the lambs and goats that had been consecrated as sacrifices to transport the knife, and it is forbidden to make use of consecrated animals. The Gemara answers that the person acted here **in accordance with** the opinion of **Hillel, as it was taught** in a *baraita*: **They said about Hillel** that **no one ever misused his burnt-offering.** How did he ensure this? He was careful not to consecrate the animal in advance, but **rather he would bring it** in an **unconsecrated** state **to the Temple courtyard and** there he would **consecrate it, and** then immediately he would **place his hand on its** head **and slaughter it.** On that day, those who used their Paschal lambs and goats to transport knives consecrated their animals only after they arrived in the Temple courtyard.


Having resolved the question of how one sanctifies the *pesach* on *shabbat* itself, the *gemara* returns to the remaining *shabbat* problem with the knife. If the animal was unsanctified when it carried the knife, there is no violation of *melacha b'kodashim* — using a consecrated animal for labor. But there is still the prohibition of *mechamer* — using an animal to carry loads, as one would use a donkey (*chamor*). Having an animal carry something for you on *shabbat* is a form of prohibited *melacha*, even when the animal itself is not consecrated.

Yet the *gemara* notes that what is being raised here is not only an *issur melacha b'shabbat* — a prohibition against *melacha* on *shabbat* — but a distinct and additional problem: *issur melacha b'kodashim*, using a sanctified animal for labor, which is its own prohibition rooted in the verse *lo ta'avod b'bechor shorecha*. On top of that, there is the concern of *me'ilah* — the prohibition against deriving benefit from consecrated property, which applies independently of *shabbat*. The *gemara* here draws on a *baraita* that says of Hillel: *miyamav lo ma'al adam b'olato* — in all his days, no person ever came to commit *me'ilah* through his *olah*. How so? Because Hillel's practice was to bring his animal into the *Beit Hamikdash* as ordinary, unsanctified property — *mevi chullin l'azarah* — and only at the last possible moment would he sanctify it, perform *smicha*, and slaughter it. By compressing the window between sanctification and slaughter to virtually nothing, there was simply no opportunity for *me'ilah* to occur. This same practice explains how the people could bring their knives on the animal's wool or between its horns without transgressing: the animal had not yet been sanctified at that point, so neither *melacha b'kodashim* nor *me'ilah* was implicated.

This in turn raises the question that drives the next stage of the *gemara*'s discussion: if Hillel's solution depended on waiting until the last moment to sanctify the animal, how could anyone sanctify the *pesach* on *shabbat* at all? The *mishna* teaches *ein makdishin v'ein ma'arichin v'ein machrimin* — one may not consecrate, assign valuation, or place a *cherem* ban, nor may one separate *trumah* or *ma'asrot*, and all of this applies on *yom tov*; certainly, then, by a *kal vachomer*, it applies on *shabbat*. The practical resolution to this problem — that a *chovah she'kavua lahem zman*, an obligation with a fixed time, may be sanctified on *shabbat* — belongs to the next stage of the discussion.

### Sanctification on Shabbat

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פֶּסַח בְּשַׁבָּת הֵיכִי מָצֵי מַקְדֵּישׁ לֵיהּ, וְהָתְנַן: אֵין מַקְדִּישִׁין וְאֵין מַעֲרִיכִין וְאֵין מַחְרִימִין וְאֵין מַגְבִּיהִין תְּרוּמָה וּמַעַשְׂרוֹת. כׇּל אֵלּוּ בְּיוֹם טוֹב אָמְרוּ, קַל וָחוֹמֶר בַּשַּׁבָּת!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: If so, **how could they consecrate the Paschal offerings** that year when Passover eve occurred **on Shabbat? Didn’t we learn** in a mishna: **One may not consecrate** animals, **take a valuation vow, consecrate** objects for use by the priests or the Temple, **or separate *terumot* and tithes. They stated all of these** prohibitions with regard to **a Festival,** and it is **an *a fortiori* inference** that these activities are prohibited **on Shabbat** as well, for the Sages decreed that one should not engage in these activities because they are similar to business transactions and weekday activities.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנֵי מִילֵּי בְּחוֹבוֹת שֶׁאֵין קָבוּעַ לָהֶן זְמַן. אֲבָל בְּחוֹבוֹת שֶׁקָּבוּעַ לָהֶן זְמַן — מַקְדִּישִׁין. דְּאָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מַקְדִּישׁ אָדָם אֶת פִּסְחוֹ בְּשַׁבָּת וַחֲגִיגָתוֹ בְּיוֹם טוֹב.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **This** prohibition of consecrating an animal as a sacrifice on Shabbat or a Festival **applies only to obligatory** sacrifices **that do not have a set time** to be brought. **But obligatory** sacrifices **that have a set time,** such as the Paschal lamb, **one may consecrate** even on Shabbat. **For Rabbi Yoḥanan said: A person may consecrate his Paschal lamb on Shabbat and his Festival peace-offering on the Festival.** Since these sacrifices must be brought on a specific day, they may be consecrated on that day even when it is Shabbat or a Festival, as the Sages did not uphold their decree in this circumstance.

As discussed, the resolution to the *hekdesh* problem — the prohibition against consecrating on *shabbat* — is that an obligation with a fixed time (*chovah she'kavua lahem zman*) such as the *korban pesach* may be sanctified on *shabbat* itself. Rabbi Yochanan states explicitly: *makdish adam et pischo b'shabbat* — a person may sanctify his *pesach* on *shabbat*, and similarly may sanctify his *chagigah* on *yom tov*.

### K'Le'Achar Yad

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַהֲלֹא מְחַמֵּר! מְחַמֵּר כִּלְאַחַר יָד: מְחַמֵּר כִּלְאַחַר יָד נָמֵי נְהִי דְּאִיסּוּרָא דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא לֵיכָּא אִיסּוּרָא מִדְּרַבָּנַן מִיהָא אִיכָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But is he not driving a laden animal?** One who leads a lamb that is carrying a knife is considered as one who is driving a laden animal, which is prohibited on Shabbat. The Gemara answers: It is **driving a laden animal in an unusual manner,** as a lamb is not typically used to carry loads. The Gemara asks: **Even driving a laden animal in an unusual manner** is problematic; **granted that there is no prohibition by Torah law,** but **there is at least a rabbinic prohibition.** When one performs a prohibited act on Shabbat in an unusual manner, he does not transgress a Torah prohibition, but nonetheless, he violates a rabbinic prohibition.

The remaining problem is *mechamer*. Here the *gemara* offers an important resolution: *mechamer k'le'achar yad* — the animal is carrying in an unusual manner. If one truly intended to use a sheep as a beast of burden, one would not embed the knife in its wool. If one wanted a goat to carry something, one would not attach it between its horns. The animal is performing the function of carrying, but doing so via a *shinuy* — a significant deviation from normal procedure. This reduces the act from a biblical prohibition to a rabbinic one.

But the *gemara* acknowledges that even a rabbinic prohibition remains: *issur d'Rabbanan miha ika*. And as our *mishnah* established — against the position of Rabbi Eliezer — one may not violate a rabbinic prohibition that is merely *hechsher* for the *korban pesach*, i.e., preparatory rather than the act of sacrifice itself. So how was this permitted?

Tosafot explains that two mitigating factors together made it permissible to override this *d'rabanan*. First, the act was done *k'le'achar yad* — in an unusual manner — which signals clearly that this is not normal procedure and renders the violation less severe. Second, it was not the person performing the carrying but the animal. Even though using an animal inappropriately is itself prohibited, the prohibition is less direct when the person is not personally the one performing the *melacha*. A *d'rabanan* that is indirect and performed in an unusual manner is more susceptible to being overridden for the sake of the *korban pesach* than a straightforward *d'rabanan* of the kind that our *mishnah* does not permit.

### People's Intuition

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַיְינוּ דְּקָא בָּעֵי מִינֵּיהּ: דָּבָר שֶׁיֵּשׁ לוֹ הֶיתֵּר מִן הַתּוֹרָה, וּדְבַר שְׁבוּת עוֹמֵד לְפָנָיו, לְעׇקְרוֹ כִּלְאַחַר יָד בִּמְקוֹם מִצְוָה מַאי? אָמַר לָהֶן: הֲלָכָה זוֹ שָׁמַעְתִּי וְשָׁכַחְתִּי, אֶלָּא הַנִּיחוּ לָהֶן לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, אִם אֵין נְבִיאִים הֵן — בְּנֵי נְבִיאִים הֵן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **This is** precisely **what** the sons of Beteira **asked** Hillel: If there is **an act that is permitted by Torah law, and a rabbinic decree stands before it** and disallows it, **what** is the law with regard to the permissibility of **uprooting** the rabbinic decree **in an unusual manner, in a situation** in which one does so in order to fulfill **a mitzva?** Bringing the sacrifice is a mitzva, whereas leading the animal while it carries a knife is an unusual way of violating a rabbinic prohibition. Is this permitted? Hillel **said to them: I** once **heard this *halakha* but I have forgotten** it. **But leave** it **to the Jewish people** and rely on them to come up with a solution on their own, for **if they are not prophets, they are the sons of prophets.**

The *gemara* now ties this back to the narrative: *hainu d'ka ba'a minei* — this is precisely the question they were asking Hillel when they asked about the knife. The question was whether something that has a biblical *heter* — i.e., could in principle be done in a permitted way — but is rabbinically problematic in the manner being contemplated (*davar shevut omed l'fanav*) may be overridden in a *k'le'achar yad* fashion for the sake of the *mitzvah*. And Hillel's answer, as we saw: *halacha zo shamati v'shachachti* — I heard this and I have forgotten it. *Ela hanichu lahem l'Yisrael im einam nevi'im hem b'nei nevi'im hem* — leave it to Israel; if they are not prophets, they are descendants of prophets, and they will arrive at the truth through their own religious intuition.

What is striking here is that Hillel, who spent the entire preceding episode demonstrating the power of logical derivation — *kal vachomer*, *gezerah shavah*, hermeneutic principle after hermeneutic principle — now falls back on something that is none of those things. He does not have a tradition, and at this moment he cannot produce a derivation. Yet he is not paralyzed. He points instead to the collective religious instinct of *Klal Yisrael* as itself a reliable guide to *halacha*. The people will carry their knives in their wool or between their horns, and from that behavior the *halacha* will become clear. This is a third epistemological mode — neither *mesorah* nor derivation, but something closer to sanctified intuition — and Hillel endorses it unreservedly. The Tosafot's explanation of why this particular *d'rabanan* could be overridden, as discussed in the previous section, addresses the theoretical justification; but it is worth noting that the justification only becomes visible after the fact, precisely because Hillel himself did not have it to hand. The people arrived at the answer first, and the analysis followed.

## Gemara Analysis (II)



### Bavli vs. Yerushalmi

The *Yerushalmi* contains a parallel version of this entire story, but with significant differences that illuminate a much deeper dispute about the nature of *Torah she-be'al peh* itself. It is worth comparing the two carefully.

The *Yerushalmi* opens similarly: this *halacha* was lost to the elders of *Beteira* (*ziknei Beteira*, not *B'nei Beteira*). They are told there is a certain Babylonian — simply *Bavli echad*, not even named at first — who studied under Shemaya and Avtalyon, and perhaps there is hope to be had from him. They summon him, he gives the same dramatic opening about there being more than one *pesach* that overrides *shabbat*, and the elders respond approvingly: yes, indeed we said there might be hope from you.

But what follows is entirely different. The *Yerushalmi* explicitly states that Hillel then proceeded to prove his case *mehekesh umikal vachomer umigzeirah shavah* — from an analogy, a *kal vachomer*, and a *gezerah shavah*. And critically, the *Yerushalmi* identifies all three *before* presenting the content, deliberately foregrounding the hermeneutic tools being deployed. The *hekesh* — a conceptual analogy rather than a verbal link — runs as follows: just as the *tamid* is a *korban tzibur*, a communal offering, and the *pesach* is likewise a *korban tzibur*, they are analogous and the *tamid*'s override of *shabbat* applies equally to the *pesach*. Then comes the *kal vachomer* as in our *gemara*, and finally the *gezerah shavah* of *mo'ado mo'ado*.

Now notice the *Yerushalmi*'s response to all three arguments: *yesh lo teshuvah* — each one can be answered. The *hekesh* has a rebuttal; the *kal vachomer* has a rebuttal; and the *gezerah shavah* — *she'ein adam dan gezerah shavah me'atzmo* — you cannot construct that on your own. Every argument he offered, they dismissed.

In the *Yerushalmi*'s account, Hillel then spent the entire day — *kol hayom kulo* — not teaching *halachot* and holding court as the new *nasi*, but trying to convince them. *Lo kiblu mimenu* — they refused to accept anything he said. Finally, at the end of the day, he said: *yavo alay kach shamati miShemaya v'Avtalyon* — let it come upon me, this is what I received as a tradition from Shemaya and Avtalyon. The moment they heard this — *keivan shesham'u mimenu ken amdu u'minuhu nasi aleihem* — they immediately stood and appointed him *nasi*.

This is a completely different story. In the *Yerushalmi*, all the logical derivations, all the hermeneutics, meant nothing to them. What mattered was the *mesorah* — do you have this as a received tradition from an authoritative teacher? The moment he invoked the direct chain of transmission from Shemaya and Avtalyon, they accepted him entirely. The story ends the same way — with him mocking them and then forgetting the *halacha* about the knife, and being forced to say *kach shamati v'shachachti*. Even there, the resolution comes through the invocation of tradition.

This contrast between the *Bavli* and the *Yerushalmi* versions is not merely a narrative variation. It encodes a fundamental dispute about what *Torah she-be'al peh* is. In the *Yerushalmi*'s telling, *Torah she-be'al peh* is primarily about *mesorah* — the transmission of a received tradition from teacher to student, generation to generation. Logical proofs and hermeneutic derivations, however sophisticated, are not authoritative in themselves. What is authoritative is: who taught you, and can you trace your teaching back to an unbroken chain of transmission?

In the *Bavli*'s telling, by contrast, what impressed *B'nei Beteira* was Hillel's ability to derive the *halacha* through logic and hermeneutic principles. The *kal vachomer* alone ultimately failed, but the *gezerah shavah* was accepted, and the whole framework of logical derivation was treated as genuinely probative.

Hillel comes from Bavel. In Bavel, the tradition had always leaned more toward the creative, intellectually generative model of *Torah she-be'al peh* — the *yud-gimmel middot*, the hermeneutic principles, the derivation of *halacha* from first principles. The chain of direct transmission from teachers in *Eretz Yisrael* was more attenuated in Bavel; necessity itself drove a more derivation-based approach. *B'nei Beteira* represent the *Mikdash*-centered, tradition-bound model — this is how we have always done it, and we need to know that it is what was always done.

What Hillel brought to *Eretz Yisrael* was precisely the Babylonian *Torah she-be'al peh* — and this was the founding moment of the rabbinic leadership that would define the tradition going forward, from Hillel's own dynasty through Rabbi Akiva's school and ultimately to Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and the redaction of the *mishnah*. The whole subsequent rabbinic project — a *Torah she-be'al peh* grounded in derivation, interpretation, and the creative application of hermeneutic principles — has its symbolic origin in this encounter.

There is also a thread worth noting within the story itself that complicates a pure *mesorah*-only view: the story begins precisely because something was *forgotten*. The whole problem is that even received traditions can be lost. And in the resolution, Hillel says: trust the people's intuition. *Im einam nevi'im hem b'nei nevi'im hem*. They are not using the *yud-gimmel middot*; they are not applying *gezerah shavah*; and they do not have a tradition. Yet they arrive at the correct answer through religious intuition. This too is a form of arriving at truth that is neither pure derivation nor pure *mesorah* — and Hillel, the arch-proponent of the derivation model, endorses it.

There is an important *tosefta* in Sanhedrin that illuminates this further. It describes seven things Hillel expounded before *B'nei Beteira* — and the seven things are precisely his seven hermeneutic principles: *kal vachomer*, *gezerah shavah*, *binyan av mikatuv echad*, *binyan av mishnei ketuvim*, *kelal u'frat*, *prat u'chelal*, and *davar halamed me'inyano*. What he was expounding was not merely the *halacha* about *pesach* on *shabbat* — he was teaching them the very methodology by which *halacha* is derived. He was, in effect, presenting a manifesto for a new kind of *Torah she-be'al peh*.

### Seven Principles

The *tosefta* in Sanhedrin states: *shiv'ah devarim darash Hillel haZaken lifnei B'nei Beteira* — seven things Hillel the Elder expounded before *B'nei Beteira*. The seven are enumerated as: *kal vachomer*, *gezerah shavah*, *binyan av mikatuv echad*, *binyan av mishnei ketuvim*, *kelal u'frat*, *prat u'chelal*, *davar halamed me'inyano* — and also *kayyotzei bo mimakom acher*, a case similar to another case from elsewhere. That accounts for all seven.

The significance of the *tosefta*'s phrasing is the word *darash* — he was expounding, teaching them actively. What was he teaching them? Not just the specific *halacha* about *pesach* and *shabbat*, but the framework: here are the authorized principles of derivation by which *halacha* is legitimately determined. This is the deeper revolution that Hillel represented. *B'nei Beteira* operated in a world where authority meant transmission; Hillel was introducing — or formalizing and championing — a world in which authority could also mean disciplined, principled derivation.

The profound question this raises — whether *Torah she-be'al peh* is primarily a repository of transmitted laws or primarily an authorized interpretive process given to human beings — is one that runs throughout the entire *talmud*. The *Rambam* in his *Perush HaMishnayot* addresses this directly in response to the Karaite challenge: if everything was transmitted, how can there be debates on every page of the *talmud*? His answer is that what was transmitted directly — what we call *halacha l'Moshe m'Sinai* — is a tiny fraction of the tradition. The overwhelming majority was given to human beings to derive through the application of authorized principles; and because derivation by human beings is inherently not predetermined, different scholars can and do arrive at different conclusions. The debates are not a sign of confusion but of the system working as intended. The *Ramban*'s view on *gezerah shavah* — that it was never a tradition of which words to link, but rather a tradition of the *halacha* for which one then found textual support — represents a mediating position between pure derivation and pure *mesorah*.

Both of these positions are embedded in this story. The *Bavli*'s Hillel champions derivation; the *Yerushalmi*'s *B'nei Beteira* insist on *mesorah*. And the resolution — in both versions — ultimately involves both: Hillel had a tradition, which he was able to express and support through hermeneutic reasoning, and the people's intuition arrived at the same place. The episode becomes a founding narrative not just for a specific *halacha*, but for the nature of *Torah she-be'al peh* itself.

## Aggadic Stories



### Hillel's Pride Falls

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה אָמַר רַב: כׇּל הַמִּתְיַהֵר, אִם חָכָם הוּא — חׇכְמָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ, אִם נָבִיא הוּא — נְבוּאָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ. אִם חָכָם הוּא חׇכְמָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ — מֵהִלֵּל, דְּאָמַר מָר הִתְחִיל מְקַנְטְרָן בִּדְבָרִים, וְקָאָמַר לְהוּ: הֲלָכָה זוֹ שָׁמַעְתִּי וְשָׁכַחְתִּי. אִם נָבִיא הוּא נְבוּאָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ — מִדְּבוֹרָה, דִּכְתִיב: ״חָדְלוּ פְרָזוֹן בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל חָדֵלּוּ עַד שַׁקַּמְתִּי דְּבוֹרָה שַׁקַּמְתִּי אֵם בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל וְגוֹ׳״, וּכְתִיב: ״עוּרִי עוּרִי דְּבוֹרָה עוּרִי עוּרִי דַּבְּרִי שִׁיר וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** With regard to the incident with Hillel, **Rav Yehuda said that Rav said: Anyone who acts haughtily, if he is a** Torah **scholar, his wisdom departs from him;** and **if he is a prophet, his prophecy departs from him.** The Gemara explains: That **if he is a** Torah **scholar, his wisdom departs from him** is learned **from Hillel, for the Master said** in this *baraita*: Hillel **began to rebuke them with words.** Because he acted haughtily, he ended up **saying to them: I** once **heard this *halakha*, but I have forgotten** it, as he was punished for his haughtiness by forgetting the law. That **if he is a prophet his prophecy departs from him** is learned **from Deborah, as it is written: “The villagers ceased, they ceased in Israel, until I, Deborah, arose, I arose a mother in Israel”** (Judges 5:7). For these words of self-glorification, Deborah was punished with a loss of her prophetic spirit, **as it is written** later that it was necessary to say to her: **“Awake, awake, Deborah; awake, awake, utter a song”** (Judges 5:12), because her prophecy had left her.

After these passages, the *gemara* shifts into an *aggadic* register with a series of morally instructive narratives connected to the themes of pride and anger. *Amar Rav Yehuda amar Rav*: anyone who conducts himself with haughtiness — if he is a wise person, his wisdom departs from him; if he is a prophet, his prophecy departs from him.

The proof that a wise man's wisdom departs from him on account of haughtiness is drawn directly from Hillel himself. We just saw: he mocked *B'nei Beteira* for their laziness, exalted himself for having attended upon Shemaya and Avtalyon, and was immediately made to say *halacha zo shamati v'shachachti* — I heard this and I forgot it. The very thing he boasted about — his superior learning from the great masters — was the thing he was then shown to have forgotten. The narrative works as a precise and pointed comeuppance.

### Moshe's Anger

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ אָמַר: כׇּל אָדָם שֶׁכּוֹעֵס, אִם חָכָם הוּא — חׇכְמָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ, אִם נָבִיא הוּא — נְבוּאָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ. אִם חָכָם הוּא חׇכְמָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ — מִמֹּשֶׁה, דִּכְתִיב: ״וַיִּקְצוֹף מֹשֶׁה עַל פְּקוּדֵי הֶחָיִל וְגוֹ׳״, וּכְתִיב: ״וַיֹּאמֶר אֶלְעָזָר הַכֹּהֵן אֶל אַנְשֵׁי הַצָּבָא הַבָּאִים לַמִּלְחָמָה זֹאת חֻקַּת הַתּוֹרָה אֲשֶׁר צִוָּה ה׳ אֶת מֹשֶׁה וְגוֹ׳״, מִכְּלָל דְּמֹשֶׁה אִיעֲלַם מִינֵּיהּ.
> **Translation:** Similarly, **Reish Lakish said: Any person who becomes angry, if he is a** Torah **scholar, his wisdom departs from him,** and **if he is a prophet, his prophecy departs from him.** The Gemara explains: That **if he is a** Torah **scholar his wisdom departs from him** is learned **from Moses, as it is written: “And Moses became angry with the officers of the host,** the captains over thousands and the captains over hundreds, who came from the battle” (Numbers 31:14). **And** what was his punishment? As **it is written** afterward: **“And Elazar the priest said to the men of war who went to the battle: This is the statute of the law, which the Lord commanded Moses”** (Numbers 31:21), which proves **by inference that** this law **had become hidden from Moses due to** his anger.

*Reish Lakish amar*: anyone who gets angry — if he is wise, his wisdom departs from him; if he is a prophet, his prophecy departs from him. The proof that anger causes wisdom to depart comes from Moshe. The verse says *vayiktzof Moshe al pekudei hachayil* — Moshe became angry at the officers of the army when they returned from the war against Midian. After that anger, when there was a *halacha* to be transmitted — regarding the purification of vessels captured in war — it was Elazar the *kohen* who transmitted it, not Moshe: *vayomer Elazar hakohen el anshei hatzava... zot chukat hatorah asher tziva Hashem et Moshe* — this is the statute of the Torah that God commanded Moshe. The verse specifies it was commanded to Moshe, yet it was Elazar who transmitted it. The conclusion is that Moshe had forgotten it on account of his anger.

### Prophecy & Anger

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם נָבִיא הוּא נְבוּאָתוֹ מִסְתַּלֶּקֶת מִמֶּנּוּ — מֵאֱלִישָׁע, דִּכְתִיב: ״לוּלֵי פְּנֵי יְהוֹשָׁפָט מֶלֶךְ יְהוּדָה אֲנִי נוֹשֵׂא אִם אַבִּיט אֵלֶיךָ וְאִם אֶרְאֶךָּ וְגוֹ׳״, וּכְתִיב: ״וְעַתָּה קְחוּ לִי מְנַגֵּן וְהָיָה כְּנַגֵּן הַמְנַגֵּן וַתְּהִי עָלָיו יַד ה׳ וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** And that **if he is a prophet, his prophecy departs from him,** we learn **from Elisha, as it is written** that he became angry with the king of Israel and said to him: **“Were it not that I have regard for the presence of Jehoshaphat the king of Judea, I would not look toward you, nor see you”** (II Kings 3:14), **and it is** afterward **written: “But now bring me a minstrel; and it came to pass when the minstrel played that the hand of the Lord came upon him”** (II Kings 3:15). Because Elisha became angry with the king of Israel, his prophetic spirit departed from him and a minstrel was needed to rouse it anew.

The proof that anger causes prophecy to depart comes from Elisha. After expressing anger at Yehoram, Elisha said: *lulei p'nei Yehoshafat melech Yehudah ani nosei im abit elecha v'im ereka* — were it not for my regard for Yehoshafat king of Judah, I would not even look at you. He was venting his anger openly. Then immediately the verse says: *ve'ata k'chu li menagen* — and now bring me a musician — *vehaya kenagen hamenagen vatehi alav yad Hashem* — and it was only when the musician played that the spirit of God rested upon him again.

The contrast between these two examples is worth noting. The case of Elisha and music has a naturalistically comprehensible quality: extreme anger disturbs the inner equilibrium that prophecy requires; calming music restores it. This reads as something close to cause and effect in a psychological sense. The case of Hillel's haughtiness reads differently — more as divine *comeuppance*, a measure-for-measure correction.

The proof that anger caused prophecy to depart comes also from Devorah, who in her song said: *ad shekamti Devorah ad shekamti em b'Yisrael* — until I arose, Devorah, until I arose as a mother in Israel. This self-aggrandizement on her part led to her then having to push herself to prophecy: *uri uri Devorah uri uri dabri shir* — rouse yourself, Devorah, rouse yourself, speak your song — implying that prophetic inspiration did not come easily to her and she had to prod herself to attain it.

### Eliab's Story

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי מָנִי בַּר פַּטִּישׁ: כׇּל שֶׁכּוֹעֵס, אֲפִילּוּ פּוֹסְקִין עָלָיו גְּדוּלָּה מִן הַשָּׁמַיִם — מוֹרִידִין אוֹתוֹ. מְנָלַן? מֵאֱלִיאָב, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּחַר אַף אֱלִיאָב בְּדָוִד וַיֹּאמֶר לָמָּה [זֶּה] יָרַדְתָּ וְעַל מִי נָטַשְׁתָּ מְעַט הַצֹּאן הָהֵנָּה בַּמִּדְבָּר אֲנִי יָדַעְתִּי אֶת זְדֹנְךָ וְאֵת רוֹעַ לְבָבֶךָ כִּי לְמַעַן רְאוֹת הַמִּלְחָמָה יָרָדְתָּ״. וְכִי אֲזַל שְׁמוּאֵל לְמִמְשְׁחִינְהוּ, בְּכֻלְּהוּ כְּתִיב: ״לֹא בָּזֶה בָחַר ה׳״, וּבֶאֱלִיאָב כְּתִיב: ״וַיֹּאמֶר ה׳ אֶל שְׁמוּאֵל אַל תַּבֵּיט אֶל מַרְאֵהוּ וְאֶל גְּבֹהַּ קוֹמָתוֹ כִּי מְאַסְתִּיהוּ״, מִכְּלָל דַּהֲוָה רָחֵים לֵיהּ עַד הָאִידָּנָא.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Mani bar Patish said: Whoever becomes angry, even if greatness has been apportioned to him from heaven, he is lowered** from his greatness. **From where do we** derive this? **From Eliab,** David’s older brother, **as it is stated: “And Eliab’s anger burned against David and he said: Why did you come down, and with whom have you left those few sheep in the wilderness? I know your insolence and the evil of your heart, for you have come down to see the battle”** (I Samuel 17:28); we see that Eliab became angry. **And when Samuel went to anoint him** after God had told him that one of Yishai’s sons was to be the king, **concerning all of** the other brothers **it is written: “The Lord has not chosen this one”** (I Samuel 16:8), **whereas with regard to Eliab it is written: “And the Lord said to Samuel: Look not at his appearance, nor at the height of his stature, for I have rejected him”** (I Samuel 16:7). This proves **by inference that until now He had loved him,** and it was only at this point that Eliab was rejected. Had it not been for his anger, Eliab would have been fit for greatness; but owing to this shortcoming, God rejected him.

*Amar Rav Mani bar Patish*: anyone who gets angry — even if greatness was decreed for him from heaven, he is brought down from it. This is derived from Eliab, the brother of David. The verse says *vayichar af Eliab beDavid* — Eliab became angry at David when David came to the battle camp before the confrontation with Goliath. Eliab accused David of abandoning his flock and coming out of idle curiosity or impudence: *ani yadati et zedonecha v'et roa levavecha ki lemaan re'ot hamilchama yaradta* — I know your willfulness and the evil of your heart; you came just to see the battle.

When Shmuel came to anoint the new king from among the sons of Yishai, God's response to each son he considered was: *lo beze bachar Hashem* — this is not the one God has chosen. But for Eliab specifically, the verse reads differently: *vayomer Hashem el Shmuel al tabet el marehu v'el geva komato ki me'astihu* — God said, do not look at his appearance or his height, for I have despised him. Not merely "not chosen" — *despised*.

The *gemara* reads this as follows: Eliab had indeed *been* chosen — *michlal dehavah rachim lei* — he had been selected for greatness by heaven, but was subsequently rejected. The anointing episode occurred before the scene of his anger at David, which the *gemara* acknowledges. Rashi explains that Eliab's anger in that scene was simply the latest expression of a deep-seated anger that had been there all along, and that it was this character trait that led to his rejection even prior to the Goliath episode. The language of the verse — *me'astihu*, I have despised him — echoes the language used of Saul's rejection, creating a deliberate parallel between two figures who were chosen and then removed on account of inner flaws.

## Pesach & Tumah



### Pesach Overrides Tumah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַשְׁכְּחַן תָּמִיד וּפֶסַח דְּדָחוּ שַׁבָּת, דְּדָחוּ טוּמְאָה מְנָא לַן? אָמְרִי: כִּי הֵיכִי דְּיָלֵיף פֶּסַח מִתָּמִיד לְעִנְיַן שַׁבָּת, הָכִי נָמֵי יָלֵיף תָּמִיד מִפֶּסַח לְעִנְיַן טוּמְאָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises an additional question incidental to the previous discussion proving that the Paschal lamb overrides Shabbat: **We have found** proofs that **the daily offering and the Paschal lamb override Shabbat. From where do we** derive **that they** also **override ritual impurity?** For we have a tradition that if the entire community is ritually impure, they nonetheless offer the communal sacrifices and the Paschal lamb. **They say: Just as** the law governing the **Paschal lamb is derived from** the law governing **the daily offering in regard to** the overriding of **Shabbat, so too the** law concerning the **daily offering is derived from** the law concerning **the Paschal lamb in regard to ritual impurity;** just as the Paschal lamb overrides communal impurity, so does the daily offering.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּפֶסַח גּוּפֵיהּ מְנָא לַן? אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: דְּאָמַר קְרָא ״אִישׁ אִישׁ כִּי יִהְיֶה טָמֵא לָנֶפֶשׁ״. אִישׁ נִדְחֶה לְפֶסַח שֵׁנִי, וְאֵין צִיבּוּר נִידְחִין לְפֶסַח שֵׁנִי, אֶלָּא עָבְדִי בְּטוּמְאָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** with regard to **the Paschal lamb itself, from where do we** derive that if most of the nation is ritually impure, the sacrifice is offered anyway? **Rabbi Yoḥanan said: For the verse states:** “Speak to the children of Israel, saying: **Any man** of you or your generations **who shall be impure by reason of a corpse,** or on a distant journey, he shall keep the Passover to the Lord. On the fourteenth day of the second month at evening they shall keep it, and eat it with *matzot* and bitter herbs” (Numbers 9:10–11). We can infer from here that **a** single **individual** or a group of individuals are **deferred to the second *Pesaḥ*** if they are ritually impure, **but the** entire **community** or the majority thereof **is not deferred to the second *Pesaḥ*; rather, they observe** the first *Pesaḥ* **in** a state of **ritual impurity.**

The *gemara* now pivots back to *halachic* analysis. We have established that *tamid* and *pesach* override *shabbat*. The next question is: how do we know they override *tumah*? The *gemara* reasons as follows: the same way we derived the override of *shabbat* for *pesach* from *tamid* — knowing that *tamid* overrides *shabbat* and applying that to *pesach* — we can reverse the reasoning for *tumah*: knowing that *pesach* overrides *tumah*, we can derive the same for *tamid*.

But this immediately invites the question: *pesach gufeih minlan* — where do we know that *pesach* itself overrides *tumah*? *Amar Rabbi Yochanan*: the verse states *ish ish ki yiheyeh tameh la-nefesh* — if any individual is *tamei* due to contact with the dead, he is pushed off to *Pesach Sheni*. The *drasha* is: an individual is pushed off to *Pesach Sheni*, but the entire congregation is not pushed off to *Pesach Sheni*. If the majority of the congregation is *tamei*, they do not wait — *ella avdi be-tumah* — they perform the *korban pesach* while *tamei*. This is derived from the fact that the Torah provides an alternative (*Pesach Sheni*) only for an individual, signaling that no such alternative operates when it is the congregation as a whole.

### Reish Lakish's View

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן לָקִישׁ לְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: אֵימָא, אִישׁ נִדְחֶה לְפֶסַח שֵׁנִי, צִיבּוּר לֵית לְהוּ תַּקַּנְתָּא לָא בְּפֶסַח רִאשׁוֹן וְלָא בְּפֶסַח שֵׁנִי!
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish said to Rabbi Yoḥanan:** This verse cannot serve as proof, for you can **say** that it is to be understood as follows: A single **individual** or a group of individuals is **deferred to the second *Pesaḥ*,** but **the community has no remedy, neither on the first *Pesaḥ* nor on the second *Pesaḥ*.**

Reish Lakish challenges Rabbi Yochanan's derivation. Why read this *drasha* as a leniency for the congregation? One could equally read it as a stringency: an individual is pushed off to *Pesach Sheni*, but if the entire congregation is *tamei*, they have no recourse at all — neither *Pesach Rishon* nor *Pesach Sheni*. The derivation cuts in the opposite direction: the Torah's provision of an alternative is granted only to individuals, not to congregations — meaning the congregation is actually worse off, not better. Reish Lakish rejects the leniency reading as insufficiently grounded.

### Three Categories

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן לָקִישׁ, מֵהָכָא: ״וִישַׁלְּחוּ מִן הַמַּחֲנֶה כׇּל צָרוּעַ וְכׇל זָב וְכֹל טָמֵא לָנָפֶשׁ״, יֵאָמֵר טְמֵאֵי מֵתִים וְאַל יֵאָמֵר זָבִין וּמְצוֹרָעִין, וַאֲנִי אוֹמֵר: אִם טְמֵאֵי מֵתִים, מִשְׁתַּלְחִין, זָבִין וּמְצוֹרָעִין — לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן?
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish** rejected this proof and **said** that a different proof may be brought **from here:** “Command the children of Israel **that they send out from the camp any leper and any *zav* and anyone impure by reason of a corpse”** (Numbers 5:2). **Let** the verse **say** only that they are to send out **those who are ritually impure due to a corpse, and not say** anything about ***zavin* and lepers, and I would say** this law on my own through an *a fortiori* inference: **If those ritually impure due to a corpse,** whose impurity is not so severe as it is contracted from an external source, **are sent out** from the camp, with regard to ***zavin* and lepers** who are the source of their own impurity, **all the more so** is it **not** clear that they should be sent out? Thus, the verse contains unnecessary information.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא יֵשׁ לְךָ שָׁעָה שֶׁזָּבִין וּמְצוֹרָעִין מִשְׁתַּלְּחִין וְאֵין טְמֵאֵי מֵתִים מִשְׁתַּלְּחִין, וְאֵיזֶה זֶה — פֶּסַח הַבָּא בְּטוּמְאָה.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** it teaches you that **you have a time when *zavin* and lepers are sent out** from the camp, **but those who are ritually impure due to** contact with **a corpse are not sent out. And what is this** time? When **a Paschal lamb is brought in impurity,** when those impure due to contact with a corpse are permitted to participate, but a *zav* and a leper may not. From here we learn that when most of the nation is ritually impure due to contact with a corpse, the Paschal lamb is brought anyway in a state of ritual impurity.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: אִי הָכִי, לֵימָא נָמֵי: יֵאָמֵר זָב וּטְמֵאֵי מֵתִים, וְאַל יֵאָמֵר מְצוֹרָע, וַאֲנִי אוֹמֵר: זָב מִשְׁתַּלֵּחַ, מְצוֹרָע — לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן. אֶלָּא: יֵשׁ לְךָ שָׁעָה שֶׁמְּצוֹרָעִין מִשְׁתַּלְּחִין, וְאֵין זָבִין וּטְמֵאֵי מֵתִים מִשְׁתַּלְּחִין, וְאֵיזֶה זֶה — פֶּסַח הַבָּא בְּטוּמְאָה.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said: If so,** that this is how the verse is to be interpreted, **let us also say** that the verse **should** only **say *zav* and those ritually impure due to a corpse and not say leper, and I** would **say** this law on my own through an *a fortiori* inference: If a ***zav* is sent out,** then with regard to **a leper,** whose ritual impurity is more severe than that of a *zav*, **all the more so** is it **not** clear that he should be sent out? **Rather,** the seemingly unnecessary mention of a leper teaches that **you have a time when** only **lepers are sent out, but *zavin* and those ritually impure due to** contact with **a corpse are not sent out. And what is this** time? When **a Paschal offering is brought in impurity,** when *zavin* and those impure due to contact with a corpse are permitted to participate, but a leper may not.

Reish Lakish therefore offers an alternative source for the principle that *pesach* overrides *tumah*, and specifically derives from it an important limitation. The verse in Bemidbar states: *vishalchu min hamachaneh kol tzarua v'chol zav v'chol tamei la-nefesh* — send out of the camp all who are *tzarua*, all who are *zav*, and all who are *tamei* through contact with the dead. Why enumerate all three categories? The most severe are the *tzarua* and the *zav*, whose *tumah* arises intrinsically from their own bodies. The least severe is the *tamei meit*, whose *tumah* comes from an external source. Logic alone would have told us: if even the *tamei meit* — the least severe — must be sent away, certainly the *zav* and *metzora* must be as well. The explicit enumeration of all three must therefore be teaching something that could not have been derived by logic alone.

Reish Lakish's answer: the verse is telling us that there is a time of year when only the *zav* and *metzora* are sent out, but *tamei meitim* are not. That time is *pesach* celebrated in a state of *tumah* — *zeh pesach haba b'tumah*. The *halacha* that *pesach* overrides *tumah* applies only to *tamei meit* — the person rendered impure through contact with a corpse. It does not apply to those whose *tumah* is intrinsic, arising from their own bodies, such as the *zav* and the *metzora*. For the congregation's *tumah* to be overridden and the *korban pesach* brought, it is necessary that the *tumah* be *tumat meit* — externally contracted impurity — not the more severe, intrinsic impurities of *zivah* or *tzara'at*. For those, even a congregation must wait until they are able to bring the *korban* in purity.

The *gemara* will continue this analysis on the following daf.
