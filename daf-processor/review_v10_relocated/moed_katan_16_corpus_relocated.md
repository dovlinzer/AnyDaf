## Practices Comparison



### Intro: Practices List

We pick up in the middle of 15b, continuing through a list of practices that apply to an *avel* and then examining whether those same practices apply to a *metzora* and a *menudeh* — mostly yes, but with notable exceptions. The *gemara* proceeds category by category, asking in each case whether the rule that governs the *avel* extends to these other two figures.

### Ne'ilat Hasandal

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: אֵין נִידּוּי פָּחוֹת מִשְּׁלֹשִׁים יוֹם, וְאֵין נְזִיפָה פָּחוֹת מִשִּׁבְעָה יָמִים. וְאַף עַל פִּי שֶׁאֵין רְאָיָה לַדָּבָר, זֵכֶר לַדָּבָר, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְאָבִיהָ יָרֹק יָרַק בְּפָנֶיהָ הֲלֹא תִכָּלֵם שִׁבְעַת יָמִים״.
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught** the following *baraita*: **Ostracism does not** apply **for less than** a period of **thirty days, and admonition,** which is less severe than ostracism, **does not** apply **for less than** a period of **seven days. And although there is no proof with regard to the matter,** i.e., the standard duration of admonition, there is **an allusion to the matter, as it is stated: “If her father had but spit in her face, should she not be ashamed for seven days?”** (Numbers 12:14). This implies that admonition lasts for seven days.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: נִידּוּי שֶׁלָּנוּ כִּנְזִיפָה שֶׁלָּהֶן, וּנְזִיפָה דִּידְהוּ שִׁבְעָה וְתוּ לָא. וְהָא רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בַּר רַבִּי וּבַר קַפָּרָא הֲווֹ יָתְבִי וְקָא גָרְסִי, קַשְׁיָא לְהוּ שְׁמַעְתָּא, אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן לְבַר קַפָּרָא: דָּבָר זֶה צָרִיךְ רַבִּי. אֲמַר לֵיהּ בַּר קַפָּרָא לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן: וּמָה רַבִּי אוֹמֵר בְּדָבָר זֶה?
> **Translation:** **Rav Ḥisda said: Our** decree of **ostracism** in Babylonia has the power **of their admonition** in Eretz Yisrael. Since the authorities in Eretz Yisrael are ordained with the title Rabbi, their admonition carries more weight than a decree of ostracism issued in Babylonia. The Gemara asks: Is **their admonition** in Eretz Yisrael only **seven** days **and no more? But isn’t** it related that **Rabbi Shimon, son of Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **and bar Kappara were sitting and studying,** and they posed **a difficulty with** a certain ***halakha*. Rabbi Shimon said to bar Kappara: This issue requires** my father, **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, to explain it. **Bar Kappara said to Rabbi Shimon,** somewhat mockingly: **And what can** your father, **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **say about this** issue? What can he add and teach us about it?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲזַל אֲמַר לֵיהּ לַאֲבוּהּ, אִיקְּפַד. אֲתָא בַּר קַפָּרָא לְאִיתְחֲזוֹיֵי לֵיהּ, אֲמַר לֵיהּ: בַּר קַפָּרָא, אֵינִי מַכִּירְךָ מֵעוֹלָם! יְדַע דִּנְקַט מִילְּתָא בְּדַעְתֵּיהּ, נְהַג נְזִיפוּתָא בְּנַפְשֵׁיהּ תְּלָתִין יוֹמִין.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Shimon **went and told his father,** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, what bar Kappara had said, and Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **became angry** with him. When **bar Kappara came** at some later point **to visit,** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **said to him: Bar Kappara, I do not know you at all.** Bar Kappara **understood that** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **had taken** his **statement to heart,** i.e., was insulted. He subsequently **behaved** as if he had been **admonished,** as a self-imposed punishment, **for thirty days.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שׁוּב פַּעַם אֶחָד גָּזַר רַבִּי שֶׁלֹּא יִשְׁנוּ לַתַּלְמִידִים בַּשּׁוּק, מַאי דְּרַשׁ — ״חַמּוּקֵי יְרֵכַיִךְ כְּמוֹ חֲלָאִים״. מָה יָרֵךְ בַּסֵּתֶר,
> **Translation:** **Once again, on another occasion, Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **decreed that students not be taught in the marketplace** but only in a study hall. **What** verse did he **expound** to serve as the basis for this decree? The verse states: **“Your rounded thighs are like jewels,** the work of the hands of an artist” (Song of Songs 7:2). **Just** as **a thigh is** ordinarily **hidden** and kept covered with clothes,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַף דִּבְרֵי תוֹרָה בַּסֵּתֶר.
> **Translation:** **so too, the words of Torah,** which are “the work of the hands of an artist,” i.e., God, must remain **hidden** in the study hall.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יָצָא רַבִּי חִיָּיא וְשָׁנָה לִשְׁנֵי בְּנֵי אֶחָיו בַּשּׁוּק, לְרַב וּלְרַבָּה בַּר (בַּר) חָנָה. שְׁמַע רַבִּי, אִיקְּפַד. אֲתָא רַבִּי חִיָּיא לְאִיתְחֲזוֹיֵי לֵיהּ, אֲמַר לֵיהּ: עִיָּיא, מִי קוֹרֵא לְךָ בַּחוּץ! יְדַע דִּנְקַט מִילְּתָא בְּדַעְתֵּיהּ, נְהַג נְזִיפוּתָא בְּנַפְשֵׁיהּ תְּלָתִין יוֹמִין.
> **Translation:** Despite Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi’s decree, **Rabbi Ḥiyya went out and taught his two nephews, Rav and Rabba bar bar Ḥana, in the marketplace. Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **heard** what he had done and **became angry** with him. When **Rabbi Ḥiyya came** at some later date **to visit him,** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi mockingly **said to him: Iyya, who is calling you outside?** By asking this question Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi was intimating that Rabbi Ḥiyya should leave his house. Rabbi Ḥiyya **understood that** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **had taken the matter to heart** and was insulted, and so **he conducted** himself as if he had been **admonished,** as a self-imposed punishment, **for thirty days.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּיוֹם תְּלָתִין שְׁלַח לֵיהּ: תָּא. הֲדַר שְׁלַח לֵיהּ דְּלָא לֵיתֵי.
> **Translation:** **On the thirtieth day,** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **sent him** a message, saying: **Come** and visit me. However, **he later** reversed his opinion and **sent him** another message, telling him **not to come.**

The *gemara* establishes that an *avel* is forbidden in *ne'ilat hasandal* — wearing a shoe. The source is the verse in which God instructs Yechezkel: *u'na'alecha tasim beraglecha* — "put your shoes on your feet" (Yechezkel 24:17). The inference, as the *gemara* explains, is *miklal d'chulei alma assur*: from the fact that Yechezkel required a special dispensation to wear shoes, we derive that the ordinary rule is prohibition. The *gemara* here follows its recurring pattern of learning *avel* practices from Yechezkel — God commanding Yechezkel to do the opposite of the usual practice.

The *gemara* then asks: *menudeh mahu bine'ilat hasandal* — what is the rule for a *menudeh* regarding shoes? Rav Yosef cites a *baraita* concerning the practices of a *ta'anit* (public fast day): when they said one may not wear a *sandal*, they said so only *be'ir* — within the city — but on the road one may wear shoes. If one sets out on a journey, he puts his shoe on; when he enters the city, he removes it. The *baraita* then continues: *vechen atta motzeh bemenudeh u've'avel* — and so too you find with a *menudeh* and an *avel*. This seems like fairly strong evidence that the prohibition of *ne'ilat hasandal* applies to a *menudeh* as well.

The *gemara*, however, offers its characteristic deflection: *lo a'shara* — the statement that the same applies to a *menudeh* and an *avel* refers to *other* practices shared between *ta'anit* and *avel*, such as the prohibition against bathing or haircuts, but not necessarily to the specific practice of shoes just mentioned. So although the surface reading strongly suggests that a *menudeh* does not wear shoes, the *gemara* stops short of a definitive ruling.

As for a *metzora*, the *gemara* simply says *teiku* — the question of *ne'ilat hasandal* for a *metzora* remains unanswered. This is noteworthy when read alongside the earlier unresolved question of *rechitzah* (bathing). In both cases the plain sense of the *baraita* — *vechen atta motzeh bemenudeh u've'avel* — suggests that the practice does extend to a *menudeh*, but the *gemara* does not confirm this conclusively.

From a conceptual standpoint, *ne'ilat hasandal* fits comfortably into a category of externally visible practices that mark a person as separated from normal social participation — disheveled clothes, wild hair, shoes removed. These are the outward signs of someone who has been, in some sense, cast outside the ordinary rhythms of communal life. This is distinct from the practices unique to the *avel* that reflect the inner state of mourning — *tefillin*, *Torah* study, and *kefiyat hamittah* — which express the loss of joy and the embrace of sadness rather than social separation.

It is worth pausing on what distinguishes an *avel* from a *menudeh* in this regard. The *avel* has not been expelled from society; the community comes to him in the form of *nichum avelim*. Yet he, too, withdraws from the normal rhythms of life — he does not leave the house during *shiva*, he does not engage in ordinary interaction — precisely to make space for the internal work of grief, to fully absorb the reality of what has occurred. The *metzora*, by contrast, is explicitly commanded in the Torah to call out *tamei tamei*, to let his garments hang loose, to dwell alone outside the camp — the marks of one who is genuinely kept apart. And the *menudeh* is indeed a case of societal censure: this person has acted in unacceptable ways, and the *halacha* imposes upon him outwardly antisocial behavior as a form of communal pressure until he conforms. Most of the demands of *niduy* fall on the *menudeh* himself — how he must behave — even as society is simultaneously required to give him the cold shoulder.

There is also the matter of *asiyat melachah*. Recall that the *avel* is forbidden to work, but this prohibition is not primarily about social interaction. Rather, the *gemara* elsewhere identifies it as *mesiach da'at min ha'aveilus* — work distracts from the interior focus that mourning demands. It is thus a prohibition oriented inward. Tellingly, the *menudeh* is not only permitted to do his own work, but may hire laborers and be hired out. The *menudeh* is not fully cut off from society in that economic sense — only from its more intimate registers.

### Tashmish HaMitah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵעִיקָּרָא מַאי סְבַר וּלְבַסּוֹף מַאי סְבַר? מֵעִיקָּרָא סָבַר: מִקְצָת הַיּוֹם כְּכוּלּוֹ. וּלְבַסּוֹף סָבַר: לָא אָמְרִינַן מִקְצָת הַיּוֹם כְּכוּלּוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **At the outset what did he hold, and ultimately what did he hold? Initially,** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **held that** the legal status of **part of the day is like** that **of an entire** day, and since the thirtieth day already begun, Rabbi Ḥiyya’s time of admonition had ended. **But ultimately he held** that with regard to this issue **we do not say** that the legal status of **part of the day is like** that **of an entire** day.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְסוֹף אֲתָא. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אַמַּאי אֲתֵית? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: דְּשָׁלַח לִי מָר דְּלֵיתֵי. וְהָא שְׁלַחִי לָךְ דְּלָא תֵּיתֵי! אֲמַר לֵיהּ: זֶה רָאִיתִי, וְזֶה לֹא רָאִיתִי. קָרֵי עֲלֵיהּ: ״בִּרְצוֹת ה׳ דַּרְכֵי אִישׁ גַּם אוֹיְבָיו יַשְׁלִים אִתּוֹ״.
> **Translation:** **In the end** Rabbi Ḥiyya **came** on that same day. Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **asked him: Why have you come?** Rabbi Ḥiyya **responded: Because** you, **Master, sent** me a message that **I should come.** He said to him: **But I sent** you a second message **that you should not come. He responded: This** messenger that you sent, i.e., the first one, **I saw** him and I did as he said, **but that** messenger, i.e., the second one, **I did not see.** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **read** the verse **about** Rabbi Ḥiyya: **“When a man’s ways please the Lord, He makes even his enemies to be at peace with him”** (Proverbs 16:7), as it was clear to him that Rabbi Ḥiyya had merited divine assistance.

An *avel* is forbidden in *tashmish hamittah* — marital relations. The source is the verse *vayenachem David et Batsheva ishto* (II Shmuel 12:24), which describes David consoling Batsheva and coming unto her. The context makes clear this occurred after the period of *aveilus* for the infant had ended, from which we infer — *miklal d'me'ikara assur* — that during *aveilus* it is forbidden.

The *gemara* asks: *menudeh mahu betashmish hamittah*? Rav Yosef attempts a proof: *kol otan shanim shehayu Yisrael bamidbar menudin hayu* — throughout all the years Yisrael were in the *midbar*, they were in a state of rejection by God, yet they clearly had marital relations, as evidenced by the children born during that period. Abaye immediately challenges this: *d'dilma menudeh lashamayim shani d'kil* — perhaps the case of being rejected by heaven is fundamentally different, and perhaps more lenient. But then, the *gemara* notes, in the previous discussion Abaye had implied the opposite — that *menudeh lashamayim* might be *chamur*, more severe. The *gemara* concludes that *sefuki mesapka lei* — Rav Yosef simply does not know which way the status of *menudeh lashamayim* cuts: *zeil hacha kamidachi lei vezeil hacha kamidachi lei* — whichever direction you try to prove from *menudeh lashamayim*, he will push you away. It is genuinely unclear whether being rejected by heaven is more stringent or more lenient than being rejected by one's community.

This uncertainty has a striking implication: the *gemara* is suggesting that *menudeh labriyot* — being cast out by people — might actually carry harsher practical consequences than *menudeh lashamayim*. This is conceptually fascinating. If the practices of *niduy* are understood as expressions of one's relationship to society, then the fact that one's behavior was found unacceptable *by society* might translate into more severe societal practices than a rejection rooted in one's relationship to God. God's rejection carries its own consequences, but *niduy* as an institution is a human communal mechanism, and perhaps it is most fully activated when the breach is a human communal one.

For a *metzora*, a *baraita* teaches: *veyashav michutz le'ahalo* — he shall sit outside his tent — followed by the ruling that he is *ke'menudeh ve'chaver ve'assur betashmish hamittah*, forbidden in marital relations. The proof that *ahalo* refers to his wife is from the verse *shuvu lachem le'ohaleichem* — go back to your tents, said to Bnei Yisrael after Matan Torah — and the surrounding context of *al tigshu el ishah*, which establishes that "tent" is a euphemism for one's wife. So *shema minah*, the *metzora* is clearly forbidden in *tashmish hamittah*. 

One might then try to derive: *venifshot nami lemenudeh* — learn it out for a *menudeh* as well, since the *baraita* says the *metzora* is *ke'menudeh*. But Rav Huna the son of Rav Pinchas, citing Rav Yosef, resists: the *baraita* does not necessarily say the *metzora* is forbidden in *tashmish hamittah* because he is *like a menudeh*. Rather, he is forbidden in *tashmish hamittah* as a separate ruling, and separately he is compared to a *menudeh* regarding *other* practices — *bemilei achrayita*. So again, the proof is blocked.

What is worth noting here is that *tashmish hamittah* is the most private of all the practices on this list. It has nothing to do with one's public presentation, with how one appears in the marketplace, or with how one interacts with neighbors. It takes place entirely within the intimate domestic space. This complicates any clean framework that would divide *avel* practices into "expressions of mourning" on one hand and "expressions of social separation" on the other. After all, on *Shabbat* and *Yom Tov*, there is the rule of *devarim shebetzina noheig* — that the *avel* still observes the intimate, hidden practices of *aveilus*. *Tashmish hamittah* is precisely such a practice: totally hidden from society, yet still governed by the rules of *aveilus*. This does raise real questions about a purely sociological reading of these practices. There may be an additional dimension — the idea that certain physical pleasures are simply inappropriate for one in a state of grief, or in a state of *niduy*, regardless of whether they are visible to anyone.

### Shiluach Korbanos


An *avel* may not send his *korbanos* to be brought in the *Beis Hamikdash* on his behalf. The source is Rabbi Shimon's teaching that the *shelamim* offering is called *shelamim* because it is brought *bizman shehu shaleim* — when one is whole — and not *bizman shehu onen*, when one is an *onen*. Even extending this to the full period of *aveilus*, the idea fits the category of *tefillin* and *Torah* — affirmative expressions of religious engagement and completeness that are inappropriate for one in a state of acute loss.

For a *menudeh*, Rav Yosef again cites the case of Bnei Yisrael in the *midbar*: they were in a state of divine rejection yet apparently continued to bring *korbanos* throughout. Abaye again deflects: *dilma menudeh lashamayim shani dekil* — perhaps that is more lenient. And again the contradiction arises: the *gemara* previously seemed to suggest the opposite. *Speiki mesapka lei*, Rav Yosef remains uncertain, and whichever side he attempts to argue from, Abaye deflects him in the other direction.

What makes this case particularly striking is that *korbanos* — unlike shoes or marital relations — are obviously about one's relationship to God. One might have thought that *menudeh lashamayim* would be more constrained here than *menudeh labriyot*, since bringing an offering is an act directed toward heaven. Yet the *gemara* appears to entertain the possibility that even in this domain, *menudeh labriyot* is more severe — a truly counterintuitive suggestion. One way to understand this is that *korban* offering in the *Beis Hamikdash* was not merely a private, spiritual act; it was deeply communal and participatory. Being *meshale'ach korbanot* involves sending to a public, national religious space. In that sense, it belongs partly to the category of communal participation, which a *menudeh labriyot* is precisely cut off from.

For a *metzora*, the *gemara* cites a *baraita* interpreting a verse in Yechezkel (chapter 44): *ve-acharei taharaso*, after his purification, implying that before purification he may not bring. Rabbi Shimon reads the verse as establishing that *bizman sheraui levi'ah raui le-hakravah* — when he is permitted to enter the Temple precinct, he may bring; *bizman she-eino raui levi'ah eino raui le-hakravah* — when he cannot enter, he cannot bring. Rabbi Yehuda disputes this reading entirely, maintaining the verse refers only to a *kohen*'s inaugural offering. So this remains a *machloket* between Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon, and the question of *shiluach korbanos* for a *metzora* is not definitively settled.

One might ask why the *gemara* derives *metzora* rules from Yechezkel at all, given the well-known difficulties with using Yechezkel as a halachic source. The *gemara* elsewhere addresses this: before Yechezkel made it explicit, we already had a *masorah* for this practice; Yechezkel's verse serves as confirmation rather than the sole derivation.

### Conceptual Framework

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי טַעְמָא עֲבַד מָר הָכִי? אֲמַר לֵיהּ, דִּכְתִיב: ״חׇכְמוֹת בַּחוּץ תָּרוֹנָּה״. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אִם קָרִיתָ — לֹא שָׁנִיתָ, וְאִם שָׁנִיתָ — לֹא שִׁילַּשְׁתָּ, וְאִם שִׁילַּשְׁתָּ — לֹא פֵּירְשׁוּ לְךָ.
> **Translation:** § Concerning the issue with which the entire incident had begun, Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi asked Rabbi Ḥiyya: **What is the reason that** you, **the Master, acted as** you did, ignoring my instructions not to teach Torah in the marketplace? Rabbi Ḥiyya **said to him: As it is written: “Wisdom cries aloud in the streets”** (Proverbs 1:20), which implies that Torah should be publicized in the streets. Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **said to him: If you read** this verse once, **you** certainly **did not read** it **a second time** in greater depth; **and if you read** it **a second time, you** certainly **did not read** it **a third time;** and **if you read** it **a third time,** then **it was not** adequately **explained to you,** as it is clear that you do not understand it properly.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״חׇכְמוֹת בַּחוּץ תָּרוֹנָּה״, כִּדְרָבָא. דְּאָמַר רָבָא: כׇּל הָעוֹסֵק בַּתּוֹרָה מִבִּפְנִים, תּוֹרָתוֹ מַכְרֶזֶת עָלָיו מִבַּחוּץ.
> **Translation:** The words: **“Wisdom cries aloud in the streets,”** should be understood **in accordance with** the opinion **of Rava. As Rava said:** With regard to **everyone who occupies himself with Torah** study **inside** the privacy of his home, **his Torah** knowledge **will proclaim his** greatness **outside,** as it will be revealed to the masses and they will see his greatness.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא כְּתִיב: ״לֹא מֵרֹאשׁ בַּסֵּתֶר דִּבַּרְתִּי״! הָהוּא בְּיוֹמֵי דְכַלָּה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But isn’t it written: “From the beginning I have not spoken in secret”** (Isaiah 48:16), implying that the Torah should be taught and proclaimed in public? The Gemara answers: **That** verse is referring to **the days of the *kalla*,** the gathering for Torah study held during Elul and Adar, when many people come to listen to Torah discourses. During this time, it is not only permitted but even recommended to teach Torah to the masses. In this way, the verse can be explained in accordance with the opinion of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי חִיָּיא, הַאי ״חַמּוּקֵי יְרֵכַיִךְ״ מַאי עָבֵיד לֵהּ? מוֹקֵי לֵהּ בִּצְדָקָה וּבִגְמִילוּת חֲסָדִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And what did Rabbi Ḥiyya do with this** verse: **“Your rounded thighs are like jewels”?** How did he understand it? This verse implies that the Torah must be kept hidden in the study hall and not publicized in the marketplace. The Gemara explains: **He interprets it** not as a reference to Torah, but as referring **to** acts of **charity and loving-kindness,** which should certainly be performed in private.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַלְמָא נְזִיפָה דִּידְהוּ תְּלָתִין יוֹמִין! נְזִיפַת נָשִׂיא שָׁאנֵי.
> **Translation:** This incident demonstrates **that, apparently, admonition of those** who live in Eretz Yisrael lasts for **thirty days** and not for seven days. The Gemara answers that this is not a conclusive proof, since Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi was the *Nasi*. **The admonition of the *Nasi*** of the Sanhedrin **is different** i.e., more severe, than the admonition of anyone else.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּנְזִיפָה דִּידַן כַּמָּה הָוֵי? חַד יוֹמָא. כִּי הָא דִּשְׁמוּאֵל וּמָר עוּקְבָא, כִּי הֲווֹ יָתְבִי גָּרְסִי שְׁמַעְתָּא, הֲוָה יָתֵיב מָר עוּקְבָא קַמֵּיהּ דִּשְׁמוּאֵל בְּרָחוֹק אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת. וְכִי הֲווֹ יָתְבִי בְּדִינָא, הֲוָה יָתֵיב שְׁמוּאֵל קַמֵּיהּ דְּמָר עוּקְבָא בְּרָחוֹק אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת. וַהֲווֹ חָיְיקִי לֵיהּ דּוּכְתָּא לְמָר עוּקְבָא בְּצִיפְּתָא, וְיָתֵיב עִילָּוֵיהּ כִּי הֵיכִי דְּלִישְׁתַּמְעָן מִילֵּיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And how** long **is our admonition** in Babylonia? The Gemara answers: It is only **one day, as in** the case involving **Shmuel and** the Exilarch **Mar Ukva. When they would sit and study *halakha*, Mar Ukva would sit before Shmuel at a distance of four cubits** as a sign of respect. Mar Ukva would conduct himself as though Shmuel were his teacher because Shmuel was much greater than him in Torah matters. **And when they would sit** together **in judgment, Shmuel would sit before Mar Ukva at a distance of four cubits** because Mar Ukva was the Exilarch and the chief judge. **But they would lower a place for Mar Ukva in the matting** upon which he sat, **and he would sit on it so that he could hear** Shmuel’s **words** of Torah even when they were engaged in judgment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כׇּל יוֹמָא הֲוָה מְלַוִי לֵיהּ מָר עוּקְבָא לִשְׁמוּאֵל עַד אוּשְׁפִּיזֵיהּ. יוֹמָא חַד אִיטְּרִיד בְּדִינֵיהּ הֲוָה אָזֵיל שְׁמוּאֵל בָּתְרֵיהּ. כִּי מְטָא לְבֵיתֵיהּ, אָמַר לֵיהּ: לָא נְגַהּ לָךְ? לִישְׁרֵי לִי מָר בְּתִיגְרֵיהּ! יְדַע דִּנְקַט מִילְּתָא בְּדַעְתֵּיהּ, נְהַג נְזִיפוּתָא בְּנַפְשֵׁיהּ חַד יוֹמָא.
> **Translation:** **Every day, Mar Ukva would accompany Shmuel to his lodgings,** in the manner that a student would show honor toward his teacher. **One day,** Mar Ukva **was** so heavily **preoccupied with a case** that had been brought before him for judgment that he did not realize that **Shmuel was walking behind him** to show him respect due to his position as the Exilarch. **When** Mar Ukva **reached his home,** Shmuel **said to him: Is it not enough for you** that I accompanied you until here? **Release me, Master, from my obligation,** so that I may return home. Mar Ukva **understood that** Shmuel **had taken the matter to heart** and was insulted. Therefore, **he conducted** himself as if he had been **admonished, for one day** as a self-imposed punishment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָהִיא אִיתְּתָא דַּהֲווֹת יָתְבָה בִּשְׁבִילָא, הֲווֹת פָּשְׁטָה כַּרְעַהּ וְקָא מְנִיפָה חוּשְׁלָאֵי, וַהֲוָה חָלֵיף וְאָזֵיל צוּרְבָּא מֵרַבָּנַן וְלָא אִיכַּנְעָה מִקַּמֵּיהּ. אֲמַר: כַּמָּה חַצִּיפָא הָהִיא אִיתְּתָא! אֲתַאי לְקַמֵּיהּ דְּרַב נַחְמָן. אֲמַר לַהּ: מִי שְׁמַעְתְּ שַׁמְתָּא מִפּוּמֵּיהּ? אֲמַרָה לֵיהּ: לָא. אֲמַר לַהּ: זִילִי נְהוּגִי נְזִיפוּתָא חַד יוֹמָא בְּנַפְשִׁיךְ.
> **Translation:** It was related that **a certain woman was sitting alongside a path** with **her leg extended** while **she was sifting barley. A Torah scholar passed** by her on this path, **but she did not yield to him** and move her leg to make room for him. **He said: How rude is that woman!** The woman **came before Rav Naḥman** to ask if this statement should be deemed as excommunication. **He said to her: Did you hear** the word **excommunication** explicitly issue **from his mouth? She said to him: No. He said to her:** If this is the case, then **go and observe an admonition for one day,** as it appears that the Torah scholar sought only to admonish you.

Stepping back across all these practices, one can observe a general pattern, even if it does not hold perfectly. The practices most clearly exclusive to the *avel* — *tefillin*, *Torah* study, *kefiyat hamittah* — concern the internal, affirmative, and joyful dimensions of religious life. The practices shared more broadly — disheveled appearance, shoes, perhaps bathing — concern external markers of separation from normal social existence. But the case of *tashmish hamittah* sits uncomfortably in either column. It is neither a public marker nor an affirmative religious expression; it is the most intimate and private of practices, and yet it appears on the shared side of the ledger.

The uncertainty that permeates the *gemara*'s analysis of *menudeh lashamayim* versus *menudeh labriyot* is itself deeply illuminating. The *gemara* cannot commit to a consistent view of whether rejection by heaven is stricter or more lenient than rejection by people, and it acknowledges this openly by noting that Rav Yosef is pushed away from either direction. The most coherent resolution is simply that the two dimensions — one's status before God and one's status within the community — operate on parallel tracks that do not map cleanly onto one another.

A striking illustration of how these two dimensions of authority — the religious and the communal — could coexist without one simply absorbing the other is found in the relationship between Mar Ukva and Shmuel. Mar Ukva held the greater political authority, functioning as the *reish galusa*, and so when they sat together in *din* he was the senior figure. Yet in matters of Torah, Shmuel was the greater authority, and Mar Ukva consistently deferred to him — accompanying him to his lodging each day as a gesture of respect. One day, preoccupied by the court case before him, Mar Ukva forgot to walk Shmuel home and went directly to his own lodging instead; Shmuel then followed Mar Ukva, and when Mar Ukva finally noticed, he realized Shmuel was displeased. The detail the *gemara* emphasizes is that even within the *din* setting, where Mar Ukva sat in the position of authority, he arranged the seating precisely so that he could hear what Shmuel had to say — because even there he regarded himself as the student. The two kinds of standing, judicial and scholarly, were entirely real and entirely distinct, neither canceling the other out.

## Court Powers Sources



### Summons from Korach

With the list of practices complete, the *gemara* turns to a new topic: how does someone come to be placed in *niduy* in the first place? This requires establishing the procedural and legal foundations of rabbinic court authority — the power to summon, to censure, to excommunicate, and if necessary to compel through more forceful means. Rava opens with a question: *menalan de'meshadrinan shlicha de'vei dina u'mezamnan leih le'dina* — from where do we know that the court sends a messenger to issue a summons requiring a person to appear for judgment?

The source is the verse *vayishlach Moshe likro le'Datan vela'Aviram bnei Eliav* (Bamidbar 16:12): Moshe sent and called to Datan and Aviram. This is the foundational precedent for the summons procedure: just as Moshe dispatched a messenger to call the parties in the Korach dispute to appear, so too a *beis din* sends its official messenger to compel appearance.

### Summons Elements

The *gemara* now elaborates on the specific content of a proper summons, deriving each element from the Korach narrative. The summons must identify the judge or court before whom the defendant must appear: *likmi gavra raba* — appear before the great person — derived from the verse's continuation *lifnei Hashem*, which functions here as a reference to the judicial authority, since judges represent divine law. The summons must also identify the opposing litigant: *atah u'planya* — you and so-and-so — derived from *atah ve'heim ve'Aharon*, "you and they and Aharon." Thus a complete summons states: you are to appear before such-and-such a court, together with your opponent in this matter.

The *gemara* further derives that the summons must specify a time to appear: *dekhtiv machar* — "appear tomorrow" — and that a period is thus set. Each of these procedural requirements is rooted in the Moshe-Datan-Aviram episode, making that narrative a kind of template for rabbinic judicial procedure.

### Multiple Summons

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** זוּטְרָא בַּר טוֹבִיָּה הֲוָה קָפָסֵיק סִידְרָא קַמֵּיהּ דְּרַב יְהוּדָה, כִּי מְטָא לְהַאי פְּסוּקָא ״וְאֵלֶּה דִּבְרֵי דָוִד הָאַחֲרוֹנִים״, אֲמַר לֵיהּ: ״אַחֲרוֹנִים״ — מִכְּלָל דְּאִיכָּא רִאשׁוֹנִים, רִאשׁוֹנִים מַאי נִינְהוּ?
> **Translation:** § **Zutra bar Toviyya was** once **reading the portion** of the Bible **before Rav Yehuda. When he reached the verse: “Now these are the last words of David”** (II Samuel 23:1), Zutra bar Toviyya **said to** Rav Yehuda: If it is written that these are the **last** of David’s words, **by inference there are first** words as well. If this is the case, **what are these first** words of David? Prior to this, it mentions only David’s song, but not his words.

If the defendant fails to appear at the first summons, the *gemara* asks: *zimna batar zimna* — from where do we know a second summons is issued rather than immediately imposing sanctions? The source is the verse in Yirmiyahu (46:17): *karu sham Paroh melech Mitzrayim sha'on he'evir hamo'ed* — they have called Pharaoh king of Egypt a noise; he has let the appointed time pass. Rashi reads *sham* here as *shamta* — the Aramaic term for *niduy* — suggesting that Pharaoh was eventually declared in excommunication for having let the time pass. The implication is that one missed court date is not sufficient; the defendant is called again, and only after repeated non-compliance does the court escalate to *shamta*.

### Contempt Grounds

The *gemara* now turns to contempt: *menalan de'i mispaker bishlicha de'vei dina* — from where do we know that if a person acts disparagingly toward the court's messenger there are consequences? The verb *mispaker* shares a root with *apikores*, and the connection is not coincidental. The *gemara* in *Sanhedrin* understands an *apikores* as someone who acts with contempt toward *talmidei chakhamim*. The principle is central: rabbinic authority depends on recognition. When someone treats the messenger of the court with contempt — dismissing not just the individual messenger but the authority he represents — this fundamentally undermines the court system. The *gemara* adds that the messenger who is treated this way may report it back: *de'ati ve'amar lo mit'chazi ke'lishna bisha* — when he comes back and tells us what was said, this does not constitute *lishna bisha*, prohibited speech. The proof: by the Moshe narrative, Moshe learned what Datan and Aviram said in response — meaning the messenger brought that report back. Implicit in the narrative is that this reporting was appropriate.

Such behavior — contemptuous dismissal of the court's messenger — constitutes what we would call contempt of court, a concept that captures precisely the idea that rejecting the messenger is rejecting the authority of the institution.

### Escalation Sanctions


From where do we know that the court then places the offender in *shamta*, a state of *niduy*? The verse *oru Meroz* from *Shirat Devorah* (Shoftim 5:23) is invoked: *oru* is read as cognate with *arur* — cursed — and Meroz is taken as the name of a person (or place, or according to some, a star) who was called to participate in the war against Sisera and refused. The parallel: one called to appear in court refuses or acts contemptuously; just as Meroz was cursed for not appearing for battle when summoned, so too the non-compliant defendant is put in *niduy*.

The *gemara* continues through the entire escalation framework. For *cherem* — the more complete form of excommunication — the verse *oru arur* provides the source, with the doubling of the curse language indicating a higher-level sanction following upon a failed *niduy*. The rules governing what *cherem* entails in practice are derived as well: *de'achil veshatei behadeih* — one may not eat or drink with the person in *cherem* — and *veka'ei bearba amot dideih* — one may not stand within his *arba amot*. The source for these is *oru arur yosheveiha* — cursed are those who dwell with him, meaning one may not be in close proximity with such a person.

The *gemara* further derives that the court announces publicly why the person is being placed in this state: *ki lo ba'u le'ezrat Hashem bagiborim* — because they did not come to the aid of God among the warriors — enumerating the offense before the community. Ulla adds the dramatic detail: *be'arba mei'a shipurei shamteih Barak leMeroz* — Barak excommunicated Meroz with four hundred *shofarot*. The *shofar* blast is thus the instrument by which *niduy* is proclaimed publicly — and with full communal fanfare, not merely a private administrative act. As for who Meroz was, there is a dispute: some say he was a great person who did not show up to fight; others say *kochava hava* — it was a star, since *min shamayim nilchamu hakochavim*, the stars fought from heaven, and this star refused.

### Cherem & Property


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שְׁתֵיק וְלָא אֲמַר לֵיהּ וְלָא מִידֵּי, הֲדַר אֲמַר לֵיהּ: ״אַחֲרוֹנִים״, מִכְּלָל דְּאִיכָּא רִאשׁוֹנִים, רִאשׁוֹנִים מַאי הִיא? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מַאי דַּעְתָּךְ, דְּלָא יָדַע פֵּירוּשָׁא דְּהַאי קְרָא לָאו גַּבְרָא רַבָּה הוּא? יְדַע דִּנְקַט מִילְּתָא בְּדַעְתֵּיהּ, נְהַג נְזִיפוּתָא בְּנַפְשֵׁיהּ חַד יוֹמָא.
> **Translation:** Rav Yehuda **remained silent and said nothing to him.** Zutra bar Toviyya thought that Rav Yehuda did not hear what he had said, so he **then said to him** a second time: If it is written that these are the **last** of David’s words, **by inference there are first** words as well. If this is the case, **what are these first** words of David? **He said to him: What do you think?** Do you think that anyone **who does not know the meaning of this verse is not a great man?** Why are you stressing the fact that I do not know the answer to your question? Zutra bar Toviyya **understood that** Rav Yehuda **had taken the matter to heart** and was insulted. Therefore, **he conducted** himself as if had been **admonished for one day** as a self-imposed punishment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְדַאֲתָן עֲלַהּ, מִיהָא ״אַחֲרוֹנִים״ — מִכְּלָל דְּאִיכָּא רִאשׁוֹנִים, רִאשׁוֹנִים מַאי הִיא? ״וַיְדַבֵּר דָּוִד לַה׳ אֶת דִּבְרֵי הַשִּׁירָה הַזֹּאת בְּיוֹם הִצִּיל ה׳ אוֹתוֹ מִכַּף כׇּל אוֹיְבָיו וּמִכַּף שָׁאוּל״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** now **that we have come** to discuss this issue, since the verse mentions David’s **last** words, **by inference there are** also **first** words. **What** then **are these first** words of David? The Gemara answers: The first words are: **“And David spoke to the Lord the words of this song in the day that the Lord delivered him out of the hand of his enemies, and out of the hand of Saul”** (II Samuel 22:1), as that song is also referred to as words.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לוֹ הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְדָוִד: דָּוִד, שִׁירָה אַתָּה אוֹמֵר עַל מַפַּלְתּוֹ שֶׁל שָׁאוּל? אִלְמָלֵי אַתָּה שָׁאוּל וְהוּא דָּוִד — אִיבַּדְתִּי כַּמָּה דָּוִד מִפָּנָיו.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates: **The Holy One, Blessed be He, said to David: David, do you recite a song over the fall of Saul? Had you been Saul and he were David,** then **I would have destroyed many Davids before him.** Although I decreed that Saul’s kingdom would not continue, as an individual he was far greater and more important than you.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַיְינוּ דִּכְתִיב: ״שִׁגָּיוֹן לְדָוִד אֲשֶׁר שָׁר לַה׳ עַל דִּבְרֵי כוּשׁ בֶּן יְמִינִי״, וְכִי כּוּשׁ שְׁמוֹ? וַהֲלֹא שָׁאוּל שְׁמוֹ! אֶלָּא: מָה כּוּשִׁי מְשׁוּנֶּה בְּעוֹרוֹ — אַף שָׁאוּל מְשׁוּנֶּה בְּמַעֲשָׂיו.
> **Translation:** The response to this admonishment **is** found in the verse, **as it is written: “Shiggaion of David, which he sang to the Lord, concerning the words of Cush the Benjaminite”** (Psalms 7:1). **Is Cush his name? Saul is his name. Rather,** this is a designation that indicates: **Just as a Cushite,** a native of the ancient kingdom of Cush in eastern Africa, **is distinguished by his** dark **skin, so too, Saul was distinguished by his actions,** as he was absolutely righteous and performed many good deeds. Therefore, David uses the word *shiggaion* as an allusion to the error [*shegia*] that he had made when he sang a song of praise over Saul’s downfall.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כַּיּוֹצֵא בַּדָּבָר אַתָּה אוֹמֵר: ״עַל אוֹדוֹת הָאִשָּׁה הַכּוּשִׁית אֲשֶׁר לָקָח״, וְכִי כּוּשִׁית שְׁמָהּ? וַהֲלֹא צִיפּוֹרָה שְׁמָהּ! אֶלָּא: מָה כּוּשִׁית מְשׁוּנָּה בְּעוֹרָהּ — אַף צִיפּוֹרָה מְשׁוּנָּה בְּמַעֲשֶׂיהָ. כַּיּוֹצֵא בַּדָּבָר אַתָּה אוֹמֵר: ״וַיִּשְׁמַע עֶבֶד מֶלֶךְ הַכּוּשִׁי״, וְכִי כּוּשִׁי שְׁמוֹ? וַהֲלֹא צִדְקִיָּה שְׁמוֹ! אֶלָּא: מָה כּוּשִׁי מְשׁוּנֶּה בְּעוֹרוֹ — אַף צִדְקִיָּה מְשׁוּנֶּה בְּמַעֲשָׂיו.
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes: **Similarly, you can explain** the verse: “And Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses **due to the Cushite woman whom he had married,** for he had taken a Cushite woman” (Numbers 12:1). **But is her name Cushite? Zipporah is her name. Rather, just as a Cushite is distinguished by his** dark **skin, so too, Zipporah was distinguished by her actions.** The Gemara continues: **Similarly, you can explain** the verse: **“Now when Ebed-Melech the Cushite heard”** (Jeremiah 38:7). **Is his name Cushite? Zedekiah is his name. Rather, just as a Cushite is distinguished by his** dark **skin, so too, Zedekiah was distinguished by his** righteous **actions.**

Having established the escalating sanctions of *niduy* and *cherem*, the *gemara* asks: *menalan demafkerinan nichsei* — from where do we know that the court may seize and declare ownerless the property of someone who refuses to comply? Here the model shifts from *Shirat Devorah* to the narrative of Ezra, which serves as the source for the principle of *hefker beis din hefker* — a court's declaration of ownerlessness is legally effective. The verse reads: *vechol asher lo yavo lishloshes hayamim ba'atzat hasarim vehazekenim yocharam kol rechusho vehu yibadel mikahal hagola* — whoever does not appear within three days at the counsel of the officers and elders, all his property shall be put in *cherem* and he shall be separated from the community of the exile (Ezra 10:8).

It is worth noting the overlap in vocabulary: *cherem* in the Torah can apply to property made taboo and destroyed, as in the case of Achan and *mo'el bacherem*, but also to property made off-limits to its owner and effectively made available to others — which is the sense operative here. The verse combining property *cherem* with communal separation is precisely the template for the court's dual power: to confiscate and to ostracize.

The passage from Nechemya then provides the further escalation: *va'ariv imam va'akalalem va'akeh mehem anashim va'emretem va'ashbi'em* — I fought with them, cursed them, struck some of them, pulled their hair, and made them swear an oath (Nechemya 13:25). The *gemara* reads this as a list of additional enforcement tools available to the court: verbal rebuke, physical force, hair-pulling, and oath imposition. It should be noted that this is qualitatively different from the earlier sanctions. *Niduy*, *cherem*, and property seizure are all forms of social and economic pressure. Physical compulsion is a different category entirely — a shift from communal mechanisms to direct bodily enforcement. The broader context in which the Ezra verse is deployed is itself worth pausing on: the community was contending specifically with the issue of foreign wives — and here, at what is among the final historical moments recorded in *Nach*, they were still struggling with precisely that question of non-compliance, a point the *gemara* will return to in its discussion of the verse's further applications.

### Corporal Authority

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כַּיּוֹצֵא בַּדָּבָר אַתָּה אוֹמֵר: ״הֲלֹא כִבְנֵי כוּשִׁיִּים אַתֶּם לִי בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל״, וְכִי כּוּשִׁיִּים שְׁמָן? וַהֲלֹא יִשְׂרָאֵל שְׁמָן! אֶלָּא: מָה כּוּשִׁי מְשׁוּנֶּה בְּעוֹרוֹ — אַף יִשְׂרָאֵל מְשׁוּנִּין בְּמַעֲשֵׂיהֶן מִכׇּל הָאוּמּוֹת.
> **Translation:** **Similarly, you can explain** the verse: **“Are you not as much Mine as the children of the Cushites, O children of Israel?”** (Amos 9:7). **Is their name Cushite? Israel is their name. Rather, just as a Cushite is distinguished by his** dark **skin, so too, the Jewish people are distinguished by their actions,** and they are different **from all the** other **nations.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אֵלֶּה שְׁמוֹת הַגִּבּוֹרִים אֲשֶׁר לְדָוִד יוֹשֵׁב בַּשֶּׁבֶת וְגוֹ׳״. מַאי קָאָמַר? אָמַר רַבִּי אֲבָהוּ, הָכִי קָאָמַר: וְאֵלֶּה שְׁמוֹת גְּבוּרוֹתָיו שֶׁל דָּוִד.
> **Translation:** Similarly, the verse states there: **“These are the names of David’s warriors; Josheb-Basshebeth** a Tahchemonite, chief of the captains; the same was Adino the Eznite; he raised his spear against eight hundred, whom he slew at one time” (II Samuel 23:8). The Gemara asks: **What is** this verse **saying? Rabbi Abbahu said: This is what** the verse **is saying: These are the names of the mighty actions of David.** These expressions should not be read as names of people but instead as descriptions of David’s good deeds.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״יוֹשֵׁב בַּשֶּׁבֶת״ — בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁהָיָה יוֹשֵׁב בִּישִׁיבָה, לֹא הָיָה יוֹשֵׁב עַל גַּבֵּי כָּרִים וּכְסָתוֹת אֶלָּא עַל גַּבֵּי קַרְקַע. דְּכֹל כַּמָּה דַּהֲוָה רַבֵּיהּ עִירָא הַיָּאִירִי קַיָּים, הֲוָה מַתְנֵי לְהוּ לְרַבָּנַן עַל גַּבֵּי כָּרִים וּכְסָתוֹת. כִּי נָח נַפְשֵׁיהּ, הֲוָה מַתְנִי דָּוִד לְרַבָּנַן וַהֲוָה יָתֵיב עַל גַּבֵּי קַרְקַע. אֲמַרוּ לֵיהּ: לִיתֵּיב מָר אַכָּרִים וּכְסָתוֹת! לָא קַבֵּיל עֲלֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Josheb-Basshebeth [*yoshev bashevet*]** indicates that **when** David **would sit [*yoshev*] in the study hall, he would not sit upon pillows and cushions,** as an important person ordinarily would. **Rather,** he would sit **on the ground** like one of the students. **For as long as** David’s **teacher, Ira the Jairite, was alive,** Ira **would teach the Sages** while sitting **on pillows and cushions. When** Ira **passed away, David would teach the Sages, and he** did this while **sitting on the ground. They said to him: Master,** you **should sit upon pillows and blankets. He did not accept** their suggestions, since in his humility he did not wish to appear as the teacher of the Jewish people.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״תַּחְכְּמוֹנִי״, אָמַר רַב: אָמַר לוֹ הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, הוֹאִיל וְהִשְׁפַּלְתָּ עַצְמְךָ תְּהֵא כָּמוֹנִי. שֶׁאֲנִי גּוֹזֵר גְּזֵרָה, וְאַתָּה מְבַטְּלָהּ.
> **Translation:** In this verse, David is described as **“a Tahchemonite [*taḥkemoni*].” Rav said: The Holy One, Blessed be He, said to him: Since you have humbled yourself, be you** now **like Me [*tehe kamoni*].** How so? **As I issue a decree, and you,** owing to your righteousness, **may nullify it.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁלִישִׁים״ — תְּהֵא רֹאשׁ לִשְׁלֹשֶׁת אָבוֹת. ״הוּא עֲדִינוֹ הָעֶצְנִי״, כְּשֶׁהָיָה יוֹשֵׁב וְעוֹסֵק בַּתּוֹרָה — הָיָה מְעַדֵּן עַצְמוֹ כְּתוֹלַעַת, וּבְשָׁעָה שֶׁיּוֹצֵא לַמִּלְחָמָה — הָיָה מַקְשֶׁה עַצְמוֹ כְּעֵץ.
> **Translation:** David is also described here as **“chief of the captains [*rosh hashalishim*]”** because God said to him: **You will be the head [*rosh*] of the three [*sheloshet*] Patriarchs. “The same was Adino the Eznite”;** this alludes to the fact that **when** David **would sit and occupy himself with Torah, he would make himself soft [*me’aden*] as a worm, and when he would go out to war, he would make himself hard** and strong **as a tree [*etz*].**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״עַל שְׁמוֹנֶה מֵאוֹת חָלָל בְּפַעַם אֶחָת״. שֶׁהָיָה זוֹרֵק חֵץ, וּמַפִּיל שְׁמוֹנֶה מֵאוֹת חָלָל בְּפַעַם אֶחָת, וְהָיָה מִתְאַנֵּחַ עַל מָאתַיִם, דִּכְתִיב: ״אֵיכָה יִרְדֹּף אֶחָד אֶלֶף״.
> **Translation:** The expression: **“Against eight hundred people, which he slew at one time,”** means **that he would throw an arrow** in the air **and** with it **kill eight hundred people at one time. And** David **would sigh over the two hundred** who were missing from fulfillment of the Torah’s promise, **as it is written: “How should one man chase a thousand”** (Deuteronomy 32:30).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יָצְתָה בַּת קוֹל וְאָמְרָה: ״רַק בִּדְבַר אוּרִיָּה הַחִתִּי״.
> **Translation:** **A Divine Voice issued forth and said** by way of explanation as to why the promise was not entirely fulfilled: “Because David did that which was right in the eyes of the Lord, and turned not aside from anything that He commanded him all the days of his life, **save only the matter of Uriah the Hittite”** (I Kings 15:5). Had David not committed this sin, then all of the promises mentioned in the Torah would have been fulfilled in their entirety through him.


The *gemara* continues: *menalan d'kaftinan* — from where do we know that if necessary a person can be bound and physically restrained? And from where do we know of *hardafah*? The verse in Ezra 7:26 provides: *hen le'maves hen le'shashu hen le'onash nechasin vela'asurin* — whether to death, whether to *shashu*, whether to punishment of property, or to imprisonment. The *gemara* asks: *mai le'shashu*? A chain of tradition — Adda Mari citing Nechemya bar Baruch citing Rav Chiya bar Avin citing Rav Yehuda — identifies *le'shashu* with *hardafah*. And then Rav Yehuda the son of Rav Shmuel bar Shilas in the name of Rav clarifies: *hardafah* means *menadin le'altar* — the authority to impose *niduy* immediately, without the ordinary procedural delays, when circumstances demand it.

This verse from Ezra, incidentally, represents one of the final historical events recorded in *Nach* — and yet the community was still struggling with non-compliance in the matter of foreign wives.

The broader point is significant: we have moved from a focused discussion of *niduy* and *cherem* to a comprehensive account of rabbinic and judicial authority more generally. The *beis din* functions not only as a court in the narrow legal sense but as a comprehensive governing institution — religious authority, judicial authority, and enforcement authority combined. In a system where there is no separation between the rabbinic and the political, the court both declares the law and enforces it. The tools at its disposal range from social ostracism at one end to physical compulsion and capital authority at the other, each calibrated to the severity of the non-compliance and the needs of the moment. Emergency powers — *sha'ata tzricha lahem* — allow the court to act outside its ordinary procedural constraints when the situation demands it, a concept familiar from the discussions in *Sanhedrin* of *beis din makin ve'onshin shelo min hadin*.

At this point the *gemara* introduces a digression centered on David, and it is worth pausing on what this material is doing here. The passage depicts David sitting on the ground after succeeding Rebbi Ira as teacher of the *rabbanan*, refusing to sit on pillows even when urged — *lo kibeil aleih*, he would not accept it. The *gemara* reads *Tachkemoni*, a word from the verse describing David, as *tehei chamoni* — you shall be like Me, says the Holy One, Blessed be He, because you have lowered yourself. The reward for this self-abasement is formulated strikingly: *she'ani gozer gezeirah v'attah mevattelah* — if I were to issue a decree, you now have the power to nullify it. What is notable here is the *mida k'neged mida* logic: the one who occupied the highest position yet made himself low is granted a power that transcends ordinary human authority. The continuation reinforces the same theme — *keshehayah yoshev v'osek baTorah hayah m'aden atzmo katola'at*, when sitting and engaged in Torah he would make himself soft and lowly as a worm, yet *u'v'sha'ah sheyotzei l'milchamah hayah maksheh atzmo k'eitz*, when going out to battle he would harden himself like wood. The contrast between *Adino* and *haEtzni* encodes this duality: softness in Torah contexts, strength where strength is demanded.

What makes this digression more than a biographical aside is the implicit conversation it opens with the earlier material on *niduy*. Even as the *gemara* has been tracing the legitimate coercive authority of rabbis and courts — the rebuke, the sanctions, the physical compulsion — the figure of David is introduced as someone who conspicuously did not stand on his *kavod*. The parallel to the *nezifah* discussion, where the move away from harsher *niduy* toward the gentler rebuke was already noted, is hard to miss. And the parallel to Rebbi Yehuda HaNasi, who was famously *mochel* on his own *kavod* and was told he would receive even greater reward in consequence, reinforces the sense that this page as a whole is conducting something of a counter-voice to the logic of judicial severity. The authority exists; the question the *gemara* keeps circling is what disposition should accompany its exercise.

## Niduy Timeline



### Niduy Timing

The *gemara* now addresses the practical timeline of *niduy* imposition. The rule regarding *hardafah* — immediate *niduy* — applies specifically to cases of *afkeiruta*, contempt. However, the general rule for civil matters is different: *maz'harin be-Sheni va-Chamishi ve-Sheni* — you warn the person on Monday, Thursday, and Monday before imposing any sanction. This Monday-Thursday-Monday sequence represents a formal warning period that precedes the first *niduy*, giving the recalcitrant party ample opportunity to comply before sanctions are applied. Only after this warning period passes without compliance does the court impose *niduy*, whereas contemptuous behavior toward the court or its messenger triggers *niduy* immediately.

### Escalation Sequence

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי תַּנְחוּם בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי חִיָּיא אִישׁ כְּפַר עַכּוֹ, אָמַר רַבִּי יַעֲקֹב בַּר אַחָא אָמַר רַבִּי שִׂמְלַאי. וְאָמְרִי לַהּ, אָמַר רַבִּי תַּנְחוּם אָמַר רַב הוּנָא, וְאָמְרִי לַהּ אָמַר רַב הוּנָא לְחוֹדֵיהּ:
> **Translation:** The Gemara returns to the *halakhot* of ostracism and mentions that **Rabbi Tanḥum, son of Rabbi Ḥiyya, of the village of Akko, said** that **Rabbi Ya’akov bar Aḥa said** that **Rabbi Simlai said, and** some **say** that this tradition was transmitted in the following manner: **Rabbi Tanḥum said** that **Rav Huna said, and** others **say** that **Rav Huna himself** made this statement without the chain of transmission:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תַּלְמִיד שֶׁנִּידָּה לִכְבוֹדוֹ — נִדּוּיוֹ נִידּוּי, דְּתַנְיָא: מְנוּדֶּה לָרַב — מְנוּדֶּה לַתַּלְמִיד, מְנוּדֶּה לַתַּלְמִיד — אֵינוֹ מְנוּדֶּה לָרַב. לָרַב הוּא דְּאֵינוֹ מְנוּדֶּה, הָא לְכוּלֵּי עָלְמָא — מְנוּדֶּה.
> **Translation:** If **a student ostracized** someone else **due to** an insult to **his dignity,** and not because the ostracized person was guilty of some transgression, **his** decree of **ostracism is valid, as it is taught** in a *baraita*: **One who is ostracized by the teacher is** considered **ostracized with regard to the student.** However, **one who is ostracized by the student is not** considered **ostracized with regard to the teacher.** The Gemara attempts to draw an inference from a careful reading of this *baraita*: **He is not** considered **ostracized with regard to the teacher,** which implies **that he is** considered **ostracized with regard to everyone** else.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְמַאי? אִי בְּמִילֵּי דִשְׁמַיָּא — ״אֵין חׇכְמָה וְאֵין תְּבוּנָה וְאֵין עֵצָה לְנֶגֶד ה׳״! אֶלָּא לָאו, לִכְבוֹד עַצְמוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara clarifies the case: **For what** reason was he ostracized? **If** it was **for a matter relating to Heaven,** i.e., because he sinned, then why, if he was ostracized by the student, should he not be considered ostracized with regard to the teacher? Doesn’t the verse state: **“There is no wisdom or understanding or counsel against the Lord”** (Proverbs 21:30)? This means that when a sin is committed and God’s name is desecrated, all other considerations are ignored, even the eminence and knowledge of the teacher, and therefore he too must treat the offender as ostracized. **Rather, is it not** that the Gemara is referring to a case where the student ostracized the other person due to an insult **to his** own **dignity?** Therefore, it is apparent that his decree of ostracism is valid and binding upon all, with the exception of his teacher.

Once *niduy* is imposed, the escalation sequence runs as follows: the initial period of *niduy* lasts thirty days. If the person still does not comply, a second thirty-day period is imposed. If compliance remains absent after sixty days total, the person is elevated to *cherem*, the more severe form of excommunication. The principle is a graduated one — increasing pressure at each stage while holding open the possibility of compliance and release.

The *gemara* also makes clear that not only a fully ordained *rav* may impose *niduy*; a *talmid* — a student who has not yet received *smicha* — may do so as well, at least in certain circumstances. The operative principle is *nidah lichvodo* — he imposed *niduy* for the honor shown to him personally. A baraita is cited: *menudah l'rav menudah l'talmid, menudah l'talmid eino menudah l'rav* — one excommunicated by the *rav* is treated as excommunicated by the *talmid* as well, but one excommunicated by the *talmid* is not thereby excommunicated relative to the *rav*. The *rav* does not observe the *talmid*'s *niduy*; everyone else, however, must. The critical distinction turns on the reason the *talmid* acted: if the offense was *b'millei d'shmaya* — a matter of heaven, some broadly contemptuous behavior toward religious authority or Torah generally — then *ein chochmah v'ein tevunah v'ein eitzah leneged Hashem*, there is no standing or status that shields anyone, and even a *talmid*'s *niduy* must be universally observed. But if it was *lichvod atzmo* — purely for his own personal honor, because this individual showed disrespect specifically to him rather than manifesting a general contempt for rabbinic authority — then the *rav* is not bound by it, since it reflects a personal rather than an institutional affront.

### Butcher Case

A vivid illustration of these principles appears in the case of a certain butcher who acted with contempt — *de'ishtapkha* — toward Rabbi Tuvi bar Masna. Abaye and Rava convened a *beis din* and placed him in *niduy*. The butcher then went and made peace with the party with whom he had been in dispute — presumably resolving the underlying matter and asking forgiveness from Rabbi Tuvi bar Masna as well, though the text is not entirely explicit on this last point.

At this juncture Abaye asks: *heichi le'avid* — what should we do? The question has two sides. On one hand: *lishrei leih* — should we release him now? After all, he has complied and made peace. On the other hand: *lo chal shamta le'talsin yomin* — the *niduy* has not run for thirty days, and perhaps the full thirty-day period must be endured regardless of subsequent compliance, since the sanction represents a punishment for the contemptuous act, not merely a tool to extract compliance. On the other hand: *lo lishrei leih* — should we not release him? The problem is that the *rabbanim* need to leave; the *beis din* is about to disband. The concern is that once the court disperses, it may not be possible to reconstitute it, and then no one will be able to release the butcher.

Abaye turns to Rav Idi bar Avin: have you heard a ruling on this? Rav Idi bar Avin cites in the name of Rav Tachlifa bar Avin in the name of Shmuel: *tut asar ve'tut sharei* — the same blow of the *shofar* that bound him can release him. That is, the same formal act that imposed the *niduy* can, if the court sees fit, dissolve it. The court that imposed it has the authority to release it immediately.

### Early Release Debate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: צוּרְבָּא מֵרַבָּנַן עָבֵיד דִּינָא לְנַפְשֵׁיהּ בְּמִילְּתָא דִּפְסִיקָא לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yosef said: A Torah scholar may execute judgment for himself with regard to a matter about which he is certain,** and he is not required to first go to court and have the case decided for him. The same applies when another person behaves in a disrespectful manner toward him; he is permitted to go ahead on his own and ostracize him.

But immediately this ruling is qualified: *hani mili mamona* — that applies when the *niduy* was imposed in the context of a civil matter. *Aval afkeiruta* — but when the offense was contempt of court, perhaps the full thirty-day period must be completed regardless: *ad de'chayel shamta le'talsin yomin* — until the *niduy* has run its thirty days. Contempt is a graver offense, and perhaps it demands a fixed period of consequence that cannot be abbreviated simply because the person has since conformed. This question is left unresolved.

The *gemara* then draws an implicit inference from the entire episode: Abaye's urgency to act before the court dispersed suggests he holds that *hani bei talsa de'shamitu lo asu talsa achrinei ve'sharei leih* — the three who imposed the *niduy* cannot be replaced by a different three for purposes of releasing it. The same court that acted must act again to release. This inference sets up the next discussion.

The *gemara* at this point also introduces a difficult ruling that must be read carefully in context. *V'od af shehadavar barur ein adam dan dino shel krovo v'chol sheken shel atzmo* — and furthermore, even if the matter is clear, a person does not adjudicate the case of his relative, and certainly not his own. The plain reading of this is unsettling: it seems to permit a scholar to act unilaterally on his own behalf — imposing *niduy*, for instance, to vindicate his personal honor — and the only qualification offered is that even then he should not do so when the matter is entirely self-referential. But this is precisely what we have been discussing, namely the category of *niduy l'chavodo*, where a person may act on his own when his dignity has been treated with contempt. The *gemara* is therefore not introducing a new permission so much as situating that earlier principle within a broader concern: even where personal *niduy* is permitted, perception matters, and self-adjudication carries an inherent appearance problem. People who do not know the *halacha* will not see a scholar vindicating legitimate authority; they will see someone using communal tools to serve himself. Beyond perception, there is the deeper problem of objectivity — one is not necessarily a reliable judge of wrongs done to oneself. The *gemara* is thus adding a note of caution to what it has already permitted, and the precise nuance it contributes beyond what was said before is a question the commentators work to resolve.

## Niduy Authority



### Rav vs. Talmid

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָהוּא צוּרְבָּא מֵרַבָּנַן דַּהֲווֹ סְנוּ שׁוּמְעָנֵיהּ, אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: הֵיכִי לֶיעְבֵּיד? לְשַׁמְּתֵיהּ — צְרִיכִי לֵיהּ רַבָּנַן, לָא לְשַׁמְּתֵיהּ — קָא מִיתְּחִיל שְׁמָא דִשְׁמַיָּא.
> **Translation:** **There was a certain Torah scholar who gained a bad reputation** due to rumors about his conduct. **Rav Yehuda said: What should be done? To excommunicate him** is not an option. **The Sages need him,** as he is a great Torah authority. **Not to excommunicate him** is also not an option, as then **the name of Heaven would be desecrated.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ לְרַבָּה בַּר בַּר חָנָה: מִידֵּי שְׁמִיעַ לָךְ בְּהָא? אֲמַר לֵיהּ, הָכִי אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מַאי דִּכְתִיב: ״כִּי שִׂפְתֵי כֹהֵן יִשְׁמְרוּ דַעַת וְתוֹרָה יְבַקְשׁוּ מִפִּיהוּ כִּי מַלְאַךְ ה׳ צְבָאוֹת הוּא״. אִם דּוֹמֶה הָרַב לְמַלְאַךְ ה׳ — יְבַקְּשׁוּ תּוֹרָה מִפִּיו, וְאִם לָאו — אַל יְבַקְּשׁוּ תּוֹרָה מִפִּיו.
> **Translation:** Rav Yehuda **said to Rabba bar bar Ḥana:** Have **you heard anything with regard to this** issue? **He said to him: Rabbi Yoḥanan said as follows: What is** the meaning of that **which is written: “For the priest’s lips should keep knowledge, and they should seek Torah at his mouth; for he is a messenger [*malakh*] of the Lord of hosts”** (Malachi 2:7)? This verse teaches: **If the teacher is similar to an angel [*malakh*] of the Lord,** then **seek Torah from his mouth,** but if he is **not** pure and upright, then **do not seek Torah from his mouth;** even if he is knowledgeable about Torah, do not learn from him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שַׁמְּתֵיהּ רַב יְהוּדָה. לְסוֹף אִיחֲלַשׁ רַב יְהוּדָה. אֲתוֹ רַבָּנַן לְשַׁיּוֹלֵי בֵּיהּ וַאֲתָא אִיהוּ נָמֵי בַּהֲדַיְיהוּ, כַּד חַזְיֵיהּ רַב יְהוּדָה, חַיֵּיךְ.
> **Translation:** Based on this statement, **Rav Yehuda ostracized** that Torah scholar. **In the end,** after some time had passed, **Rav Yehuda took ill** and was on the verge of death. **The Sages came to inquire about his well-being, and** the ostracized scholar **came** along **with them as well. When Rav Yehuda saw him,** that scholar, **he laughed.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: לָא מִסָּתְיֵיהּ דְּשַׁמְּתֵיהּ לְהָהוּא גַּבְרָא, אֶלָּא אַחוֹכֵי נָמֵי חַיֵּיךְ בִּי?! אֲמַר לֵיהּ: לָאו בְּדִידָךְ מְחַיֵּיכְנָא, אֶלָּא דְּכִי אָזֵילְנָא לְהָהוּא עָלְמָא בְּדִיחָא דַּעְתַּאי, דַּאֲפִילּוּ לְגַבְרָא כְּווֹתָךְ לָא חַנֵּיפִי לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The ostracized scholar **said to him: Was it not enough that you excommunicated that man,** i.e., me, **but** now **you even laugh at me?** Rav Yehuda **said to him: I was not laughing at you; rather, I am happy as I go to that** other **world that I did not flatter even** a great **man like you,** but instead I treated you fairly in accordance with the *halakha*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נָח נַפְשֵׁיהּ דְּרַב יְהוּדָה. אֲתָא לְבֵי מִדְרְשָׁא, אֲמַר לְהוּ: שְׁרוֹ לִי! אֲמַרוּ לֵיהּ רַבָּנַן: גַּבְרָא דַּחֲשִׁיב כְּרַב יְהוּדָה לֵיכָּא הָכָא דְּלִישְׁרֵי לָךְ, אֶלָּא זִיל לְגַבֵּיהּ דְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה נְשִׂיאָה דְּלִישְׁרֵי לָךְ. אֲזַל לְקַמֵּיהּ, אֲמַר לֵיהּ לְרַבִּי אַמֵּי: פּוֹק עַיֵּין בְּדִינֵיהּ, אִי מִיבְּעֵי לְמִישְׁרֵא לֵיהּ — שְׁרִי לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yehuda died.** The ostracized scholar **came to the study hall and said** to the Sages: **Release me** from the decree of ostracism. **The Sages said to him: There is no man here as eminent as Rav Yehuda who can release you** from the ostracism. **Rather, go to Rabbi Yehuda Nesia** in Eretz Yisrael, **as** only **he can release you.** That scholar **came before** Rabbi Yehuda Nesia. Rabbi Yehuda Nesia **said to Rabbi Ami: Go and examine his case. If it is necessary to release him** from his decree of ostracism, **release him** on my behalf.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עַיֵּין רַבִּי אַמֵּי בְּדִינֵיהּ, סְבַר לְמִישְׁרֵא לֵיהּ. עָמַד רַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר נַחְמָנִי עַל רַגְלָיו וְאָמַר: וּמָה שִׁפְחָה שֶׁל בֵּית רַבִּי לֹא נָהֲגוּ חֲכָמִים קַלּוּת רֹאשׁ בְּנִידּוּיָהּ שָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנִים, יְהוּדָה חֲבֵירֵינוּ עַל אַחַת כַּמָּה וְכַמָּה.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Ami examined his case** and **thought** at first **to release him** from his ostracism. But **Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani rose up on his feet and said: If the maidservant in the house of Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi once ostracized another person, and **the Sages did not relate frivolously to her** decree of **ostracism** and did not revoke it until **three years** had passed, **all the more so, with regard to** a decree of ostracism placed by **Yehuda our colleague,** we must take it seriously and not release this scholar.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר רַבִּי זֵירָא: מַאי דְּקַמַּן דַּאֲתָא הָאִידָּנָא הַאי סָבָא בְּבֵי מִדְרְשָׁא, דְּהָא כַּמָּה שְׁנֵי לָא אֲתָא, שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לָא מִיבְּעֵי לְמִישְׁרֵא לֵיהּ. לָא שְׁרָא לֵיהּ, נְפַק כִּי קָא בָכֵי וְאָזֵיל,
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira said: What** caused **this Elder,** Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani, **to come before us in the study hall** today **though for many years he did not come,** and now he comes precisely during this discussion. **Learn from this** that **it is not necessary to release him** from his decree of ostracism, as this combination of events is certainly not a coincidence. Rather, it should be viewed as an instructive sign from Heaven. Consequently, Rabbi Ami **did not release him** from the ostracism, and the ostracized scholar **left in tears.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲתָא זִיבּוּרָא וְטַרְקֵיהּ אַאַמְּתֵיהּ וּשְׁכֵיב. עַיְּילוּהּ לִמְעָרְתָּא דַּחֲסִידֵי וְלָא קַיבְּלוּהּ, עַיְּילוּהּ לִמְעָרְתָּא דְּדַיָּינֵי וְקַיבְּלוּהּ.
> **Translation:** **A wasp came and stung** the ostracized scholar **on his penis and he died.** Because he was a great Torah scholar, **they took him into the caves in which the pious** are interred in order to bury him there, **but** the caves **did not accept him.** A snake stood at the entrance of the caves and did not let them pass. **They** then **took him into the caves of the judges, and they accepted him.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי טַעְמָא? דַּעֲבַד כְּרַבִּי אִילְעַאי. דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי אִילְעַאי אוֹמֵר: אִם רוֹאֶה אָדָם שֶׁיִּצְרוֹ מִתְגַּבֵּר עָלָיו — יֵלֵךְ לְמָקוֹם שֶׁאֵין מַכִּירִין אוֹתוֹ, וְיִלְבַּשׁ שְׁחוֹרִים וְיִתְעַטֵּף שְׁחוֹרִים, וְיַעֲשֶׂה מַה שֶּׁלִּבּוֹ חָפֵץ, וְאַל יְחַלֵּל שֵׁם שָׁמַיִם בְּפַרְהֶסְיָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is the reason** that he was accepted there? The Gemara answers: Even though he sinned, **he** still **acted in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Ilai, as it is taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi Ilai says: If a person sees that his** evil **inclination is gaining control over him** and he cannot overcome it, then **he should go to a place where he is not known. He should wear black, and he should wrap his** head **in black,** as if he were a mourner. Perhaps these changes will influence him, so that he not sin. Even if these actions do not help, **he should** at least **do as his heart desires** in private **and not desecrate the name of Heaven in public.** Although this person had sinned, he did so in private and in a manner that did not publicly desecrate God’s name, and therefore it was fitting that he be given an honorable burial.

The *gemara* now articulates the hierarchy of *niduy* authority. *Menudeh le'rav menudeh le'talmid* — if a *rav* has placed someone in *niduy*, his students must observe that *niduy* and treat the person accordingly. *Menudeh le'talmid eino menudeh le'rav* — but if a *talmid* placed someone in *niduy*, the *rav* is not obligated to observe it. This asymmetry makes sense once one understands the context: these rules apply to personal *niduy*, *niduy l'chavodo*, where someone imposes *niduy* because his own honor or dignity was treated with contempt. In such a case, those below the person who imposed it must follow suit — the *talmid* defers to the *rav*'s exercise of his personal authority — but those above him, like the *rav*'s own *rebbe*, are not required to.

A similar principle applies geographically: *menudeh le'iro menudeh le'ir acheret* — if a city places someone in *niduy*, other cities must respect it. *Menudeh le'ir acheret eino menudeh le'iro* — but if another city placed him in *niduy*, his own city is not necessarily bound. *Menudeh le'nasi menudeh le'chol Yisrael* — if the *nasi* places someone in *niduy*, all of Israel follows it. *Menudeh le'chol Yisrael eino menudeh le'nasi* — but the reverse does not follow. The hierarchy flows in one direction: those subordinate to the authority that issued the *niduy* are bound; those above it need not be.

The *gemara* then illustrates these principles through a concrete and dramatic episode. Rav Yehuda placed a certain rabbi in *niduy*, and shortly afterward fell gravely ill. When his students came to visit him for *bikur cholim*, the man who had been placed in *niduy* slipped in among the group. When Rav Yehuda caught sight of him, he smiled — which the man took as mockery, and he said: is it not enough that you placed me in *niduy*, you also laugh at me? Rav Yehuda replied that he was not laughing at him at all; rather, he was at peace, even joyful, because as he was approaching death he could say with confidence that even toward a man of standing and influence, *lo chanif lei* — he had not flattered him or given unwarranted deference when it was necessary to act. That integrity brought him contentment as he neared the end. Rav Yehuda then died. The *gemara*'s framing is significant: one might have read the sequence — he imposed *niduy*, he died — as a sign that he had acted wrongly. The *gemara* resists that reading entirely; Rav Yehuda dies unrepentant and at peace, proud of what he had done. After his death, the rabbi who had been placed in *niduy* came to the *beis medrash* and asked to be released. The rabbis told him they had no one of Rav Yehuda's stature present to release him, and directed him instead to Rav Yehuda Nesia — the grandson of Rebbe, and a *nasi* — underscoring again the authority of the *nasi* in these matters. Rav Yehuda Nesia sent Rebbe Ami to examine the case, and Rebbe Ami was inclined to release him. At that moment, Rav Shmuel bar Nachmani — who had not been seen in the *beis medrash* in years — appeared and stood up to protest: even the *shifcha*, the maidservant, of Rebbe's household once placed someone in *niduy* and the rabbis let it stand for three years. How much more so, *al achas kamma vechamma*, should we not rush to dissolve what Yehuda our colleague imposed. Rebbe Zeira observed that the very fact this elder had arrived at the *beis medrash* on this particular day, after so long an absence, at precisely this moment of deliberation, was itself a *siman min hashamayim* — a heavenly sign — that the *niduy* should not be released. And so they did not release him. He left weeping, was stung by a wasp, and died. They sought to bring him to the cave of the *chasidim* for burial, but the cave did not receive him; he was brought instead to the cave of the judges, where he was accepted — for he had been a judge, but evidently not a *chasid*, his flawed character being precisely what had brought him to this point. The *gemara* notes that his conduct followed the position of Rabbi Ilai, who taught that if a person feels his *yetzer hara* overpowering him, he should go to a place where he is unknown, dress in black, and if he still cannot resist, do what he must — at least without causing public *chilul Hashem*. Whether this is cited approvingly or merely descriptively as an explanation of his behavior is itself a question worth sitting with.

It is also worth noting what the *gemara* establishes about the practical content of *niduy*. The person in *niduy* may not be eaten or drunk with, and people may not remain within his *arba amot*. This is a serious form of social isolation — eating together being one of the most powerful expressions of communal belonging and intimacy. Yet *niduy* is not complete *cherem*: the *menudeh* may still work, hire, be hired, teach, and be taught. He is excluded from the intimate circles of community — including, according to the *Rambam*, being counted toward a *minyan* — but not from all interaction. *Cherem*, by contrast, severs all social and commercial ties entirely.

### Rabban Gamliel

Rabban Gamliel introduces a crucial ruling: *echad min hatalmidim shenidah u'mes chelko eino mufar* — if one of the students placed someone in *niduy* — meaning for personal reasons, because his own honor was violated — and then that student dies, his portion of the *niduy* is not automatically released. The person in *niduy* is not freed simply by the death of the one who imposed it. This ruling carries several important implications.

### Three Inferences

The *gemara* derives *shalosh* — three inferences — from Rabban Gamliel's ruling. First, *shema mina talmid shenidah l'chavodo niduyo niduy*: this confirms the concept of personal *niduy*, *niduy l'chavodo* — a student may impose *niduy* on someone who treated his honor with contempt, and such a *niduy* is halachically valid even without a formal institutional mandate. Second, *kol echad ve'echad mefer chelko*: each party to the original *niduy* releases his own portion, suggesting that each member of the *beis din* must participate in the release for it to take effect. Third, *shema mina hanei bei tlasa de'shamisu lo asu tlasa achrina ve'sharei leih*: the original three who imposed the *niduy* cannot simply be replaced by a different three for the release — the original court must be the releasing authority.

### Personal vs Institutional

This third inference is particularly important for distinguishing between personal *niduy* and institutional *niduy*. A personal *niduy*, *l'chavodo*, is tied to the individual who imposed it. Because it is rooted in a personal violation, the person who was wronged must personally participate in the release. An institutional *niduy* — imposed because of a broader violation of communal norms, court procedures, or religious standards — belongs to the community as a whole and is not tied to any particular individual. In that case, presumably any competent authority could release it.

### Court Release Dispute

Amemar rules as the practical *halacha*: *hanei bei tlasa de'shamisu asu bei tlasa achrina ve'sharei leih* — a different court of three may indeed release a *niduy* imposed by the original three. The practical necessity is obvious: if only the original three could ever release, then the *menudeh* could be permanently trapped if the original court disbanded or its members died. Rav Ashi challenges Amemar from Rabban Gamliel's ruling: *mai lav eino mufar klal* — does it not mean that after the student's death the *niduy* simply cannot be released at all?

### Death & Release

The *gemara* resolves this: *lo* — the ruling does not mean the *niduy* can never be released. Rather, it means release is not automatic upon death. One does not go free simply because the person who imposed the *niduy* has died. Instead, *ad d'asu bei tlasa achrina ve'sharei leih* — a new court of three must be constituted and must formally release him. This is the resolution: death does not dissolve the *niduy*, but another properly constituted body of three can authorize release. The death of the original imposing party simply means that release requires a new formal process — it does not mean perpetual imprisonment in one's status.

This resolution is necessary on both sides: without it, the *menudeh* would indeed be permanently trapped, which is untenable; but simply granting automatic release upon death would empty the institution of its force. The middle path — continued *niduy* after death, but releasable through a new three-person body — maintains both the seriousness of the sanction and the possibility of eventual restoration.
