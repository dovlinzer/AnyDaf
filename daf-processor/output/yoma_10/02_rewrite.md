# Yoma 10 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Yeridat HaDorot

### Intro: Yeridat HaDorot

 The larger context of this discussion is the debate between *Rabbi Yochanan* and *Reish Lakish* about *yeridat hadorot* — whether earlier generations were greater than later ones — and how that question connects to the absence of a rebuilt temple. Specifically, the first temple was rebuilt after seventy years, yet the second temple has been destroyed and there has been no rebuilding since. That background is essential for understanding the narrative that follows.

### Reish Lakish in Jordan

*Reish Lakish hava sachi b'Yardena* — *Reish Lakish* was bathing in the Jordan River. *Ata Rabbah bar Bar Chana* — *Rabbah bar Bar Chana*, a Babylonian *amora* who was apparently in *Eretz Yisrael* at the time (as was common, since Babylonian *amoraim* would spend time studying there), came along. *Yehav lei yada* — he stretched out his hand to help *Reish Lakish* out of the Jordan. *Amar lei* — and *Reish Lakish* said to him, as a kind of thanks: *Eloha sanei lechu* — your God hates you.

Not a gracious response to someone helping you out of a river. The basis for this remark is the verse: *Im chomah hi nivneh aleha tirat kasef v'im delet hi natzur aleha luach arez* — if she is a wall, let us build upon her a tower of silver, and if she is a door, let us build upon her a plank of cedar.

### Wall vs. Door Metaphor

The *gemara* explains: *Im asitem atzmechem k'chomah v'alitem kulchem b'yimei Ezra* — if you had made yourselves like a wall and had all come up at the time of *Ezra*. The historical background is that after the return from exile, most Jews living in *Bavel* chose to remain there rather than return to *Eretz Yisrael* for the rebuilding of the second temple. A wall represents unity and wholeness — one solid, unbroken structure. Had all of Israel ascended together, *nimshaltem k'kasef she'ein rakav sholeit bo* — you would have been like silver, into which no rot can set.

*Achshav she'alitem k'dalatot* — but now that you came up only like doors. *Rashi* explains the metaphor: a door is just a small, movable part of the wall — it opens and closes. Even if there are two doors, one swings one way and one the other, the result is only something partial. *Nimshaltem k'arez she'harakav sholeit bo* — now you are like cedar, into which rot can indeed penetrate.

The implication, as *Rashi* explains and as the *gemara* alludes to, is that this is an explanation for why the second temple was destroyed and has not been rebuilt — and this remark serves as *Reish Lakish*'s answer to the earlier debate with *Rabbi Yochanan*. The second temple stands at a lower level than the first, and there has been no rebuilding since, not because later generations are of lesser spiritual stature on account of their *Torah* learning — indeed, their learning under conditions of oppression may be at a higher level than that of earlier generations — but for a different reason entirely: the failure of the mass of *bnei Bavel* to ascend in the time of *Ezra*. There are national, historical reasons for our diminished status, not necessarily individual or generational ones.

The *Maharsha* makes an illuminating observation by reading the *gemara* in light of the book of *Ezra* itself. In that period, the returning community needed to guard the walls because of attacks from surrounding peoples — Samaritans and others who opposed the rebuilding of the second temple. The *Maharsha* notes: had so many of you come up that you yourselves would have served as a wall, there would have been no need for such defensive measures, and the story would have been very different. As it was, there were only enough returnees to guard the doors — a partial, vulnerable presence — and he connects this directly to the historical circumstances described in *Sefer Ezra*.

### Arez & Sasmagor

*Mai arez?* — What is meant by *arez*, cedar? *Amar Ulla sasmagor* — *Ulla* says it is *sasmagor*, which may simply be the Babylonian name for cedar, though *Rashi* also explains it as a type of worm that eats through the inside of cedar wood, so that the interior rots while the external shell remains intact.

*Mai sasmagor?* — And what does that metaphor signify? *Amar Rabbi Abba bat kol* — *Rabbi Abba* explains it refers to a heavenly echo, a *bat kol*. During the period of the second temple, there was no prophecy, no fullness of divine presence — but there remained something like an outer shell of it, in the form of a *bat kol*.

This is grounded in the following *baraita*: *Mishemetu nevi'im acharonim Chaggai Zechariah u'Malachi nistalka Ruach HaKodesh m'Yisrael v'adayin hayu mishtamshim b'bat kol* — once the last of the prophets, *Chaggai*, *Zechariah*, and *Malachi* — who had carried over from the first temple period into the beginning of the second — died, the *Ruach HaKodesh* departed from *Yisrael*, and yet they still made use of a *bat kol*.

So the cedar metaphor is precisely this: the outside looks like a full structure, but the interior has been eaten away. There is an outer presence, a *bat kol*, but the true divine indwelling, the *Ruach HaKodesh*, is gone. The second temple was like cedar with rot inside it.

This reading makes a crucial point: the reason the second temple was at a lower level than the first, and the reason there has been no rebuilding since, is not because of *yeridat hadorot* — the spiritual decline of the generations. Rather, it reflects other national realities: the incomplete return in the time of *Ezra*, and the consequent diminished quality of the second temple's divine presence.

It is worth noting that the great scholar Saul Lieberman pointed out a distinction between how the *Bavli* and the *Yerushalmi* use the term *bat kol*. In the *Bavli*, a *bat kol* carries genuine metaphysical weight — it is a distant heavenly voice, an echo of prophecy. In the *Yerushalmi*, by contrast, Lieberman observed that the term carries no such supernatural connotation; it simply means that a report spread, that a consensus emerged, without implying any divine force.

## Mezuzah in Mikdash

### Reish Lakish's Silence

The *gemara* now raises a different question about the story: *Reish Lakish mi mishata b'haday Rabbah bar bar Chana?* — Would *Reish Lakish* really have been speaking with *Rabbah bar Bar Chana*? This is not the obvious question a reader might have — we might wonder why *Reish Lakish* spoke so harshly to someone who helped him. The *gemara*'s question is quite different.

*U'ma Rebbi Elazar d'marei d'artza d'Yisroel hava* — *Rabbi Elazar*, who was considered the master of *Eretz Yisrael* — a title given to him in another *gemara* — was so prominent a figure, yet *v'lo hava mishata Reish Lakish badei* — *Reish Lakish* would not speak with him. Why? *D'man d'mishata Reish Lakish badei beshuka* — whoever *Reish Lakish* would speak with in public, *yavlu lei iska b'lo sa'adi* — people would do business with that person without witnesses. *Reish Lakish*'s public acknowledgment of someone was tantamount to a certification of their absolute trustworthiness and integrity. Accordingly, *Reish Lakish* was exceedingly careful about whom he would speak to, because every such interaction sent a message to the entire community.

If *Reish Lakish* would not speak with *Rabbi Elazar*, he would surely not speak with *Rabbah bar Bar Chana*, who was not at *Rabbi Elazar*'s level. *B'haday Rabbah bar bar Chana mishata?* — He would speak with *Rabbah bar Bar Chana*?

### Rav Pappa's Solution

*Amar Rav Pappa shadi gavra beinaihu* — *Rav Pappa* says: you must replace one of the figures in the story. *O Reish Lakish hava v'Zeiri* — either it was *Reish Lakish* bathing and *Zeiri* who reached out a hand to help him, *o Rabbah bar bar Chana hava v'Rebbi Elazar* — or it was *Rabbah bar Bar Chana* bathing and *Rabbi Elazar* who helped him out of the river. One of the two pairings must be adjusted for the narrative to be coherent.

It is worth noting that when the *gemara* says they came before *Rabbi Yochanan*, *Rashi* explains that this means *Reish Lakish* came to *Rabbi Yochanan* and told him the story — which would suggest *Reish Lakish* was indeed the one bathing, lending weight to one of *Rav Pappa*'s alternatives.

## Yeridat HaDorot (II)

### Rabbi Yochanan's Response

*Ki azlu l'kamei d'Rabbi Yochanan* — when they came before *Rabbi Yochanan*, *amar lei lav hai hu ta'ama* — he said: that is not the reason. The diminished status of the second temple relative to the first is not attributable to the failure of a mass *aliyah* in the time of *Ezra*. *Ee nami seliku kulhu b'yemei Ezra* — even if all of *Klal Yisrael* had come up in the time of *Ezra*, *lo havi sharya Shchina b'mikdash sheni* — the *Shchina* would still not have rested in the second temple. The reason is derived from a verse: *Yaft Elokim l'Yefet v'yishkon b'ahalei Shem* — God will beautify *Yefet*, but He will dwell in the tents of *Shem*.

## Yaft Elokim Leyafet

### Persia & Yafet

*Rabbi Yochanan* is reading this verse as a fundamental theological statement about divine presence. *Af al gav d'yaft Elokim l'Yefet* — although God will expand and beautify *Yefet*, *ein haShchina shora ella b'ahalei Shem* — the *Shchina* rests only in the tents of *Shem*.

The application to the second temple is this: the second temple was built under the auspices of *Koresh* (Cyrus), king of Persia. The *gemara* will argue in a moment that the Persians are descendants of *Yefet*. Therefore, although the second temple was in some respects larger and more beautiful — indeed, by the time of Herod's expansion it surpassed the first in grandeur, even as at its initial construction the people wept because it seemed so much smaller — it was nonetheless built through the dominion of *Yefet*. *Ein haShchina shora ella b'ahalei Shem*: the divine presence rests only where the building is the expression of *Shem*'s dominion, as was the first temple built by *Shlomo*. Not merely the identity of the builder matters, but the fact that the second temple was constructed while Israel lived under foreign subjugation, without autonomous dominion — and therefore the divine presence was necessarily diminished.

This is a striking contrast to another well-known reading of this verse. A classic *midrash* on *Yaft Elokim l'Yefet* reads it as *yafyuso shel Yefet b'ahalei Shem* — the beauty of *Yefet* [can dwell] within the tents of *Shem* — understanding the verse not as a dichotomy but as a synthesis: the beauty associated with *Yefet* can be integrated into the domain of *Shem*. Here, however, *Rabbi Yochanan* is reading the verse as a sharp opposition.

This raises an immediate question: *u'Parsayei minalan d'miYefet ka'asu* — from where do we know that the Persians are descended from *Yefet*? The verse establishes something about *Yefet* and his descendants, but we must first demonstrate that Persia falls within that category.

### Sons of Yafet

The *gemara* invokes the verse: *dichsiv bnei Yefet Gomer u'Magog u'Madai v'Yavan v'Tuval u'Meshech v'Tiras* — the sons of *Yefet*: *Gomer*, *Magog*, *Madai*, *Yavan*, *Tuval*, *Meshech*, and *Tiras*. The *gemara* proceeds to identify each of these with known nations and places.

*Gomer ze Garmamya* — *Gomer* is *Garmamya*. There is a question about this identification. The *Gra* has the reading *Germanya*, and some would want to identify this with Germany, though this seems doubtful for a *gemara* that is describing ancient Near Eastern geography. The *Steinsaltz* edition identifies it with a region north of Assyria, perhaps Phrygia or Armenia, which is far more plausible given the overall geographic context of these identifications.

*Magog ze Kandya* — *Magog* is *Kandya*. Various traditions identify this as Scythia, and there is an alternate reading of *Gothya*, which would refer to the Goths in Asia Minor. The *Steinsaltz* reads it as Scythia.

*U'Madai ze Makdonya* — *Madai* is Macedonia. This is notable: Macedonia is the homeland of Alexander the Great and is geographically distinct from Greece proper, being to its north.

*Yavan k'mashmao* — *Yavan* is Greece, as its name plainly implies.

*Tuval ze Beis Onaiki* — *Tuval* is *Beis Onaiki*, identified as Bithynia, in northwest Asia Minor.

*Meshech zu Mosya* — *Meshech* is *Mosya*, identified as a region along the Sea of Marmara and the Dardanelles, also in northwest Asia Minor.

Then comes *Tiras*: *pleigei bar Simai ve-Rabanan ve-amrei lah Rabi Simon ve-Rabanan* — there is a dispute. *Chad amar zeh Beit Taryaiki* — one opinion says it is Thrace, the region in the northeastern corner of the Greek world. *Ve-chad amar zu Pras* — and one opinion says it is Persia.

All of these nations — particularly the ones clearly identifiable — share the characteristic of speaking Indo-European languages, which provides an interesting linguistic argument for their common ancestry from *Yefet*.

*Tani Rav Yosef* — *Rav Yosef* taught in a *baraita*: *Tiras zu Pras* — *Tiras* is Persia. This is the identification that *Rabbi Yochanan*'s argument requires.

### Sakastan Geography

The *gemara* continues down the section in *Parshat Noach*, noting additional rabbinic identifications. Moving from the sons of *Yavan* to *Benei Kush*, the verse mentions *Sabta ve-Raamah ve-Sabtecha*. *Tani Rav Yosef*: *Sabta* is *Skistan Gavyata* — outer *Sakastan* — and *Sabtecha* is *Skistan Brayta* — inner *Sakastan*. (*Sakastan*, also known as *Sakastene*, is a region spanning western Afghanistan and eastern Iran.) The *gemara* explains the geographical relationship: *bein chada le-chada mea parsi* — between the outer ring and the inner circle, the gap is a hundred *parsi*. *Ve-heikefah alfa parsi* — and the circumference of the outer circle is a thousand *parsi*.

It should be noted that these figures — *Sabta*, *Raamah*, *Sabtecha* — are actually *benei Kush*, descendants of *Cham*, not of *Yefet*. They appear just after the listing of the sons of *Yavan* in the Torah's genealogical table.

### Nimrod's Cities

*Va-tehi reishit mamlachto Bavel* — the beginning of *Nimrod*'s kingdom was in *Bavel*. *Nimrod* emerges from the genealogy of *Kush* as the dominant personality of the generation. The *gemara* here takes up the enumeration of the cities he built: *ve-Erech ve-Akad ve-Calneh* — *Erech*, *Akad*, and *Calneh*.

*Bavel kemashmao* — *Bavel* is Babylonia, as its name plainly indicates.

*Erech ze Orichot* — *Erech* is *Orichot*, identified by archaeologists as Uruk, a major ancient city in what is now Iraq.

*Ve-Akad* — *Akad* is given as *zeh Bashkar* in the *gemara*'s reading, though this may simply be another name for Akkad, the ancient Semitic city from which Akkadian civilization takes its name.

*Calneh ze Nofar Ninphi* — *Calneh* is *Nofar Ninphi*, the identity of which is uncertain.

### Ashur's Cities

*Min ha-aretz ha-hi yatza Ashur* — from that land *Ashur* came forth. *Tani Rav Yosef: Ashur zeh Silak* — *Ashur* is Seleucia. After Alexander divided his empire among his generals, Seleucia became the capital of the Seleucid kingdom, which ruled over Syria, Mesopotamia, Iran, and India (though Iran and India quickly broke away). The Seleucids are, of course, the kingdom at the center of the *Chanukah* story.

*Vayiven et Ninveh* — *Ashur* built *Nineveh*. *Ve'et Rechovot Ir ve'et Kalach* — and *Rechovot Ir* and *Kalach*.

*Ninveh kema'shmao* — *Nineveh* is the famous city *Nineveh*, well known from the story of *Yonah*.

*Rechovot Ir zu Perat* — *Rechovot Ir* is the Euphrates, or more precisely, *Perat de'Meshan*, a region along the Euphrates.

*Kalach zu Perat de'Borsi* — *Kalach* is *Perat de'Borsi*, presumably another location along the Euphrates.

*Ve'et Resen bein Ninveh uvein Kalach hi ha'ir hagedolah* — and *Resen*, which is between *Nineveh* and *Kalach*, is the great city. *Zu Aktispon* — this is identified as Ctesiphon, the Persian capital — a remarkable identification.

The *gemara* then notes a question: *eini yodea im Ninveh ha'ir hagedolah im Resen ha'ir hagedolah* — I do not know whether the "great city" in the verse refers to *Nineveh* or to *Resen*. The *Maharsha* observes that *Kalach* is not a candidate because the verse would then have said *ve'et Kalach ha'ir hagedolah* directly; the phrase *hi ha'ir hagedolah* — "that is the great city" — must refer to one of the others mentioned previously. The *gemara* resolves it: *keshehu omer ve'Ninveh hayetah ir gedolah l'Elohim mahalach sheloshet yamim* — when the verse later in *Tanakh*, in *Yonah*, says that *Nineveh* was a great city to God, a three-day journey across, *hevei omer Ninveh hi ha'ir hagedolah* — we learn that *Nineveh* is the great city. Of course, the size of cities may well have changed between *Bereishit* and *Yonah*, and the two descriptions need not be in tension.

### The Giants

*Vesham Achiman Sheshai veTalmai* — now, in the context of discussing great and notable figures, the *gemara* moves forward to *Sefer Bamidbar*. When the scouts surveyed the land of Canaan, they encountered *Achiman*, *Sheshai*, and *Talmai*, the *yelidei ha'anak* — sons of the giant. The *gemara* offers interpretations of each name.

*Tana: Achiman meyuman shebe'achin* — *Achiman*, from the word *yamin* (right hand), means the strongest, most capable of the brothers.

*Sheshai she'mesim et ha'aretz keshchitut* — *Sheshai* connotes one who lays the land to waste; he was so enormous that wherever he walked he trampled and destroyed.

*Talmai she'mesim et ha'aretz telamim telamim* — *Talmai* connotes furrows; when he walked, he was so immense that the earth cracked beneath him into deep grooves.

*Davar acher* — an alternative interpretation, no longer connecting to the etymology of the names but making historical statements: *Achiman bana Anat* — *Achiman* built the city of *Anat*. *Sheshai bana Alush* — *Sheshai* built *Alush*. *Talmai bana Talbosh* — *Talmai* built *Talbosh*.

Finally: *yelidei anak she'ma'anikim hachamah bekumatan* — the children of *Anak*. *Anak* can also mean a necklace, and *Rashi* explains: they stood so tall that, looking up at them, the sun appeared to be hanging around their neck like a necklace.

## Rome & Persia

### Rome Falls to Persia

*Amar Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi amar Rabbi: atidah Romi shetipol beyad Pras* — in the end, Rome will fall at the hands of Persia.

To appreciate the weight of this statement, one must understand the context. From *Chazal*'s perspective, Rome was the supreme power, seemingly without end. The famous tradition reads *Yaakov*'s dream of the ladder as a vision of the successive kingdoms that would subjugate *Yisrael* — and Rome ascends and does not descend. When would Roman domination ever end? The Persians, meanwhile, were not a theoretical counterweight; at the time the *Bavli* was being composed, the Sassanian Persian Empire was a genuine and powerful rival to Rome on its eastern border, and indeed in the early 7th century it would conquer the entire Middle East from the Romans before eventually losing it.

*Chazal*'s identification of *Edom* with Rome also requires brief explanation. *Esav* in the *Torah* is the rival of *Yaakov*, and *Edom* is the nation descended from him. *Edom* disappeared as a distinct people in biblical times, but *Chazal* transferred the identification to Rome, because Rome was the subjugating power of Israel. Later, when Rome fell, the identification shifted to Christianity — made possible by the continuity of the Holy Roman Empire — so that in medieval rabbinic literature, *Edom* means Christendom. The *gemara* takes this transference as given.

The proof text for the prediction that Rome will fall to Persia: *Lachen shim'u atzat Hashem asher ya'atz el Edom umachshovotav asher chashav el yoshvei Teiman. Im lo yis'chavum tze'irei hatzon* — therefore hear the counsel of God which He counseled against *Edom*... if they will not sweep them away, the young of the flock. *Im lo yashim aleihem noham* — if they will not place upon them a bellowing.

The *tze'irei hatzon* — the young of the flock — is identified as Persia. But this prompts a challenge: *Matkif lah Rava bar Ulla: mai mashma dehai tze'irei hatzon Pras?* — How do we derive that the young of the flock means Persia? One might naturally think that identifying *tzon* (flock, sheep) with a nation requires a textual basis in *Daniel*, where *ha'ayil asher ra'ita ba'al hakarnayim malchei Madai uPharas* — the ram with two horns represents the kings of Media and Persia. So the sheep imagery does connect to Persia. But the question remains: *Ve'eima Yavan?* — perhaps *tzon* refers to Greece? Because *Daniel* also says *utzfir hasayir melech Yavan* — the he-goat is the king of *Yavan* — and goats too are part of the flock.

*Ki salik Rav Chaviva bar Surmaki* — when *Rav Chaviva bar Surmaki* went up to *Eretz Yisrael* and presented this line of questioning before one of the *rabbanan*, *amar lei: man delo yada perusha dikra motzi tiyufta* — he who does not know how to interpret the verses asks challenges. The question presumed that the identification of *tze'irei hatzon* with Persia was based on the *pasuk* in *Daniel*, and on that basis the question about *Yavan* was reasonable. But the identification was never based on that.

*Mai tze'irei hatzon?* — What does "young of the flock" actually mean here? *Zutra d'achavahi* — the youngest of his brothers. The youngest of the children of *Yefet* is *Tiras*, and *Tiras* is Persia, as *Rav Yosef* taught in his *baraita*: *Tiras zu Pras*. The metaphor is not about the species of animal at all; it is simply about being the youngest sibling. The *pasuk* in *Daniel* is therefore irrelevant, and the question about *Yavan* falls away.

### Kal VaComer

*Amar Rabbah bar bar Chana amar Rabbi Yochanan mishum Rabbi Yehuda be-Rabbi Ilai: atida Romi she-tipol be-yad Pras* — the same conclusion, that Rome will fall to Persia, is now restated with a *kal vachomer* argument. This is a striking *kal vachomer*:

*U-mah Mikdash Rishon she-benuhu Bnei Shem* — the first temple was built by the descendants of *Shem* — *Shlomo* — *ve-hecherivuhu Kasdim* — and the Chaldeans destroyed it. *Naflu Kasdim be-yad Parsiyim* — the Chaldean empire fell to the Persians. *Mikdash Sheni she-benuhu Parsiyim, ve-hecherivuhu Romiyim* — the second temple, built under Persian auspices, was destroyed by the Romans. *Eino din she-yipelu Romiyim be-yad Parsiyim?* — Is it not logical that the Romans should fall at the hands of the Persians?

It must be acknowledged that this *kal vachomer* is somewhat weak. Taken purely as a logical argument, one might object that the Persians were defeated by the Romans — the Persians beat the Chaldeans, but the Romans then beat the Persians — so how does one project that the Persians will now beat the Romans? The force of the argument, as best understood, is this: the Persians demonstrated their power by overthrowing the empire that destroyed a *mikdash* built by *Bnei Shem*. How much more so should the Persians be capable of overthrowing the empire that destroyed a *mikdash* built under Persian auspices — a lesser claim, as it were, than overthrowing those who destroyed *Shem*'s own temple.

### Rav's Correction

*Amar Rav: atida Pras she-tipol be-yad Romi* — *Rav* disagrees entirely. It is not Rome that will fall to Persia, but Persia that will fall to Rome.

### Builders vs. Destroyers

*Amru leh Rav Kahana ve-Rav Assi le-Rav: banuyei be-yad saturei?* — The builders will fall at the hands of the destroyers? Persia, which allowed the second temple to be built, will be defeated by Rome, which destroyed it? How can that be right?

*Amar le-hu: in, gezerat ha-melech hi* — yes, it is God's decree. That is the way history will unfold. *Aikha de-amri* — some say *Rav* added: *inhu nami ka satrei bei knishta* — they, the Persians, also destroy synagogues, so they are not entirely the good party in this equation. Though Jews fared well under Persian rule generally, and it is not entirely clear what historical context this refers to, the point is that Persia is not unambiguously righteous.

*Tanya nami hachi* — a *baraita* likewise taught: *atida Pras she-tipol be-yad Romi* — Persia will fall into the hands of Rome. Two reasons: first, *de-satrei bei knishta* — they destroy synagogues. Second, and more fundamentally, *ve-od gezerat ha-melech hi she-yipelu bonei be-yad sotrei* — it is God's will that the builders fall at the hands of the destroyers. Sometimes the destroyers prevail, because that is the divine decree.

### Mashiach & Rome

*Amar Rav Yehuda amar Rav: ein Ben David ba* — the son of David, the *mashiach*, will not come *ad she-tifshot malchut Romi ha-resha'ah be-chol ha-olam kulo* — until the evil Roman kingdom has spread its dominion over the entire world, *tish'ah chodashim* — for nine months. The proof: *lachein yitnem ad eit yoledah yaladah, ve-yeter achiv yeshuvun al Bnei Yisrael* — therefore He will give them until the time that the woman giving birth gives birth, and the remnant of his brothers will return to the children of Israel. How long does a woman carry before giving birth? Nine months. Only when Rome has subjugated the entire world for nine months will the *mashiach* come.

This is a sobering vision. One might have imagined that once Rome has reached the height of its power it would immediately be checked. *Rav Yehudah* is saying the opposite: things must become maximally desperate before redemption arrives. Only then, at the most dire moment, does *mashiach* come.

With this the *aggadata* concludes and we return to the *mishnah*.

## Mezuzah in Mikdash (II)

### Mishnah: Chambers

*Tanu Rabbanan: kol ha-lishkot she-hayu ba-mikdash lo hayah lahen mezuzah* — the Rabbis taught: all the chambers that were in the *mikdash* did not have a *mezuzah*. *Chutz milishkat parhedrin* — except for *Lishkat Parhedrin*, *shehayta bah beit dirah lekohen gadol* — which served as a residential dwelling for the *Kohen Gadol* during the seven days before *Yom Kippur*. That chamber had a *mezuzah*.

### Rabbi Yehudah's Objection

*Amar Rabbi Yehuda* — but were there not many chambers in the temple in which people actually lived? The *kohanim* on watch would sleep in rooms within the *mikdash*. Yet none of those had a *mezuzah*. So the principle cannot simply be that any lived-in room requires a *mezuzah*. *Ela Lishkat Parhedrin gzeira hayta* — *Rabbi Yehuda* holds that the *mezuzah* on *Lishkat Parhedrin* was not a biblical obligation at all; it was a rabbinic decree. And the reason for that decree will emerge shortly.

It is worth noting that in the *Tosefta*, *Rabbi Yehuda* says the exact opposite — that there were many rooms in the *mikdash* in which people lived and all of them had a *mezuzah*. This is a direct contradiction of the position attributed to him here.

The logic behind the general exemption requires examination. *Shulot* (synagogues) do not require a *mezuzah* because people do not eat and sleep in them. We do affix a *mezuzah* as a precaution, but there is no technical biblical requirement. If, however, people actually lived and slept in a room within the synagogue, it would seem that room should be obligated. So the question for the *mikdash*: why would rooms in which *kohanim* actually sleep not require a *mezuzah*? That is precisely *Rabbi Yehuda*'s argument — he points to the evidence and says: look, many rooms were lived in and had no *mezuzah*. He does not yet explain why, but he draws the conclusion that living in a room within the *mikdash* does not itself create an obligation, and that the *mezuzah* on *Lishkat Parhedrin* is therefore rabbinic in origin.

## Mezuzah Analysis

### Definition of Bayit

*Amar Rabbah kasavar Rabbi Yehuda: kol bayit she'eino asui limot hachama ulimot hageshamim, eino bayit* — *Rabbah* explains that *Rabbi Yehuda* holds that any structure not suited for year-round habitation — both summer and winter — does not have the status of a *bayit* (house), and therefore is not subject to the obligation of *mezuzah*. The rooms in the *mikdash*, however comfortable for a night's sleep, were not built for year-round residence, and thus lack the requisite status.

### Winter/Summer House

*Etvei Abaye* — *Abaye* challenges this: *vakativ vehikeiti beit hachoref al beit hakayitz* — does the verse not say "I will strike the winter house against the summer house"? Those structures are called *bayit*! A winter house is a house, even though one only lives in it part of the year!

*Amar lei: beit choref ubeit kayitz ikri, bayit stam lo ikri* — *Rabbah* responds: such a structure may be called a "winter house" or a "summer house" — it requires a modifier — but without a modifier it would not simply be called a *bayit*. A *bayit stam*, a house simpliciter, it is not, and therefore it does not carry the full halachic status of a dwelling. The modifier signals its deficiency.

### Sukkah & Mezuzah

With this answer established, *Abaye* presses further: if *Rabbi Yehuda* truly holds that a structure not fit for year-round living lacks the status of a *bayit*, then why does *Rabbi Yehuda* require a *mezuzah* in a *sukkah*? *Sukkat heChag beChag — Rabbi Yehuda mechayiv* — a *sukkah* during the festival of *Sukkot* requires a *mezuzah* according to *Rabbi Yehuda*, *veChachamim potrin* — whereas the *chachamim* exempt. Here *Rabbi Yehuda* is the more demanding opinion — exactly the reverse of his position regarding the chambers in the *mikdash*.

*Vetanya ala* — and a *baraita* elaborates on *Rabbi Yehuda*'s stringency with respect to a *sukkah*: *Rabbi Yehuda mechayiv be'eruv* — he requires the *sukkah* to participate in an *eruv* if it opens onto a courtyard, *u'vemezuzah* — it requires a *mezuzah*, *u'vema'aser* — and if one brings grain into it, the *sukkah* triggers the obligation of *ma'aser*. *Rabbi Yehuda* treats the *sukkah*, during *Sukkot*, as having the full legal status of a house.

### Ma'aser Discussion

This creates a difficulty. One might suggest that *Rabbi Yehuda*'s requirements for *eruv* and *mezuzah* are merely rabbinic enactments — perhaps he does not really consider the *sukkah* a *bayit* biblically, but imposes these obligations as rabbinic stringencies. The *gemara* addresses this: *bishlama eruv mezuzah ika l'meimar miderabbanan* — one could indeed argue that the *eruv* and *mezuzah* requirements are rabbinic. *Ella ma'aser* — but the *ma'aser* obligation is different. *Mi ika l'meimar miderabbanan?* — Can one really say this is rabbinic?

The reasoning: grain brought into a house becomes obligated in *ma'aser* by virtue of entering the house (*re'iyat penei habayit*). If the *sukkah* creates only a rabbinic obligation, we would have a situation of rabbinic and biblical obligations existing in the same category. *D'dilma ati l'afroshei min hachiyuv al hapatour umin hapatour l'chiyuv* — one might then separate *terumot u'ma'asrot* from grain that is biblically obligated on behalf of grain that is only rabbinically obligated, or vice versa, which would be a serious halachic error.

The principle is that we do not create rabbinic obligations in the category of *terumot u'ma'asrot* when doing so risks confusing them with biblical obligations, because one cannot separate from one category to fulfill the other. Since grain and grain are indistinguishable to the eye, making one batch of wheat only rabbinically obligated while another batch is biblically obligated creates a genuine risk of invalid separations.

*Tosafot* notes that this reasoning seems overstated — there are many cases of rabbinic obligations in *terumot u'ma'asrot*. The resolution *Tosafot* offers is that in most other cases, some clear distinguishing feature prevents confusion: for instance, if the rabbinic obligation applies to an entirely different species (say, vegetables), one cannot in any case separate from vegetables to fulfill an obligation on wheat, so there is no risk of confusion. But here, the wheat brought into a *sukkah* looks exactly like wheat brought into a regular house. Nothing marks it as a different category, and the risk of a person wrongly separating from one to fulfill the other is real. Therefore we would never make such a *sukkah*-wheat obligation merely rabbinic. The *ma'aser* obligation must be biblical — which means *Rabbi Yehuda* genuinely considers the *sukkah* a *bayit* in the full biblical sense.

### Rava's Clarification

*Amar lei Rava: ve-Sukkas hachag be-chag katani* — *Rava* objects to the proposed resolution that the debate between *Rabbi Yehuda* and the *chachamim* is about the rest of the year, with everyone agreeing during *Sukkot* itself that a *mezuzah* is required. *Rava* points out: the *baraita* explicitly states the dispute as applying to *the sukkah during the festival* — *Sukkat heChag beChag* is precisely the case in dispute. It is impossible to say that during *Sukkot* all agree.

*Ella amar Rava: be-sh'ar yemos hashanah kulei alma lo peligi de-petura* — the rest of the year, everyone agrees the *sukkah* is exempt. No one is living in it. *Ki peligi be-shiva* — the debate concerns only when people are actually living in it, during the seven days of *Sukkot*. *Ve-Sukkah taima le-chud ve-lishka taima le-chud* — and each of the two cases — the *sukkah* on *Sukkot* and the chambers in the *mikdash* — involves a distinct halachic reason for its respective ruling. This is why the positions of *Rabbi Yehuda* and the *chachamim* appear reversed between the two cases.

### Distinct Reasons

*Sukkah taima le-chud* — the *sukkah* debate turns on its own logic. *Rabbi Yehuda le-taimei de-amar Sukkah diras keva ba'inan* — *Rabbi Yehuda* follows his well-known position, stated at the beginning of tractate *Sukkot*, that a *sukkah* can be and indeed must be a permanent dwelling structure. *Rabbi Yehuda* famously holds that a *sukkah* may be any height, because he maintains that a *sukkah* is to be treated as a permanent dwelling. If the *sukkah* has the status of a *diras keva*, a permanent residence, then it is obligated in a *mezuzah*. *Um'chayiva bimezuzah*. *Ve-Rabbanan le-taimeihu* — the *chachamim* hold that a *sukkah* is inherently a temporary structure (*diras arai*): it is made to last for one week and then be dismantled, so it cannot possibly be a *bayit*. The entire debate about the *sukkah* turns on whether the *halakhic* character of a *sukkah* is that of a permanent or temporary dwelling, and that debate has no application beyond the *sukkah* itself. If one were to make an ordinary hut for a week, everyone would agree it is exempt — the question is uniquely about the *halakhic* character of a *sukkah* on *Sukkot*.

### Forced Dwelling

The chambers in the *mikdash* involve a completely separate question. *Rabbanan savri dira be'al korcha shemeih dira* — the *chachamim* hold that a forced dwelling still has the legal status of a dwelling, and therefore even the rooms where *kohanim* lived involuntarily, as part of their temple service, are obligated in a *mezuzah*. The *kohanim* on watch, the *Kohen Gadol* in the week before *Yom Kippur* — all are deemed legal residents. *Rabbi Yehuda savar dira be'al korcha lo shemeih dira* — *Rabbi Yehuda* holds the opposite: a forced dwelling does not have the status of a dwelling. Part of what constitutes a genuine residence is that one is there by choice, not compulsion. Living as, in effect, a prisoner — however comfortable the quarters — does not rise to the level of *bayit dira*. Accordingly, no room in the *mikdash*, where anyone residing there does so under compulsion of service rather than free choice, can be biblically obligated in a *mezuzah*.

### Decree on Lishkat

Given this, why then does *Lishkat Parhedrin* have a *mezuzah* at all, even according to *Rabbi Yehuda*? *U'miderabbanan hu detiknu lah, shelo yomru Kohen Gadol chavush be'veis ha'asurim* — it is a rabbinic enactment, instituted specifically so that people should not say that the *Kohen Gadol* is imprisoned. There is a beautiful internal consistency here: *Rabbi Yehuda*'s very reason for exempting the chamber from a biblical *mezuzah* obligation — he views the *Kohen Gadol*'s residence there as a form of confinement — is precisely what makes the rabbinic decree necessary. Because it resembles imprisonment, the rabbis mandated a *mezuzah* to dispel that impression.

The *chachamim*, by contrast, begin from the opposite premise: the *Kohen Gadol* is legally a resident, the chamber is a genuine dwelling, and the biblical obligation of *mezuzah* applies. The *mezuzah* itself thus visually communicates the same message — that this is a home, not a prison — but reaches that conclusion through entirely different reasoning.
