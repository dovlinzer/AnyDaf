## Classic Seven Mitzvot



### Overview & Context

Daf 57 continues the extended discussion of *sheva mitzvot bnei Noach*. The sugya begins approximately nine lines from the bottom of 56b, at the words *hai Tana d'bei Menashe hu*. By way of orientation, the classic seven commandments are: the three cardinal prohibitions — *avodah zarah*, *giluy arayos*, and *shfichus damim* — together with *ever min hachai*, *birchat Hashem*, *gezel*, and *dinim*. All seven are initially derived from *pesukim* concerning Adam rather than being limited to the covenant with Noach.

The preceding discussion had already touched on several major themes. First, *dinim* was explored at some length. The Rambam understands *dinim* as requiring the establishment of a court system — courts in every city and region — while the Ramban understands it more broadly as substantive law, rooted in the *pasuk* concerning Avraham: *l'maan asher yetzaveh et banav v'et beito acharav v'shamru derech Hashem la'asot tzedakah u'mishpat*, to do righteousness and justice. The *gemara* had further explored whether *knassos* — fines — are included within *dinim*. According to Rashi, *knassos* go beyond mere compensation and the righting of wrongs; they are punitive rather than restorative, which would arguably place them within the broader *tzedakah u'mishpat* framework. The conclusion of that discussion is that *dinim* encompasses substantive civil law oriented toward creating a just society.

A second theme that was established and will recur throughout the daf is the procedural distinction between how a Jewish court adjudicates and how a non-Jewish court adjudicates *sheva mitzvos*. This can be distilled into three contrasts: *edah, eidim, v'hasra'ah* — the size of the court, the requirement for witnesses, and the requirement for warning. With respect to the court: for Jews, even capital cases require a *beit din* of 23; a non-Jewish court may consist of even a single judge. With respect to witnesses: Jews require two valid witnesses; non-Jews may rely on circumstantial evidence or hearsay. With respect to warning: Jews require *hasra'ah*, prior warning to the offender, while non-Jews may be adjudicated without any such warning.

### Tana D'Bei Menashe

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא: הַאי תַּנָּא תַּנָּא דְבֵי מְנַשֶּׁה הוּא, דְּמַפֵּיק דַּ״ךְ וְעָיֵיל סַ״ךְ.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rava says: This *tanna*,** who holds that the mitzva of establishing courts of judgment is not included in the Noahide mitzvot, **is** the ***tanna* of the school of Menashe, who removes** from the list of the Noahide mitzvot two mitzvot whose mnemonic is ***dalet*, *kaf*,** which stands for judgment [*dinim*] and blessing the name of God [*birkat Hashem*], **and inserts** in their place two mitzvot whose mnemonic is ***samekh*, *kaf*,** standing for castration [*seirus*] and diverse kinds [*kilayim*].

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנָּא דְבֵי מְנַשֶּׁה: שֶׁבַע מִצְוֹת נִצְטַוּוּ בְּנֵי נֹחַ – עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה, וְגִילּוּי עֲרָיוֹת, וּשְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים, גָּזֵל, וְאֵבֶר מִן הַחַי, סֵירוּס, וְכִלְאַיִם.
> **Translation:** **As the school of Menashe taught: The descendants of Noah were commanded** to observe **seven mitzvot:** The prohibitions of **idol worship, and forbidden sexual relations, and bloodshed,** and **robbery, and** eating **a limb from a living** animal, and **castration, and diverse kinds.**

The *gemara* now identifies the opinion that *dinim* was added to the Israelites only at Marah as belonging to *Tana d'bei Menashe* — the school of Menashe — precisely because that school substitutes *dinim* and *birchat Hashem* for two other commandments. The *gemara* states: *d'mappik dach v'ayil sach* — it removes *dalet-ches* (*dinim* and *birchat Hashem*) and inserts *samech-kaf* (*sirus* and *kilayim*).

The formulation of *Tana d'bei Menashe* is thus: *sheva mitzvos nitztavu Bnei Noach* — the seven commandments given to the descendants of Noach are *avodah zarah*, *giluy arayos*, *shfichus damim*, *gezel*, and *ever min hachai*, together with *sirus* (sterilization and castration) and *kilayim* (crossbreeding of animals and grafting of different species). Both of these substitutions are deeply rooted in *Bereishis*: *sirus* connects to *peru u'revu*, the imperative to procreate, and *kilayim* to *l'minehu*, the principle of species distinction established at creation.

### R. Yehuda View

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: אָדָם הָרִאשׁוֹן לֹא נִצְטַוָּוה אֶלָּא עַל עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה בִּלְבַד, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״וַיְצַו ה׳ אֱלֹהִים עַל הָאָדָם״. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה בֶּן בְּתִירָה אוֹמֵר: אַף עַל בִּרְכַּת הַשֵּׁם. וְיֵשׁ אוֹמְרִים: אַף עַל הַדִּינִים.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yehuda says: Adam, the first** man, **was commanded only with regard to** the prohibition of **idol worship, as it is stated: “And the Lord God commanded the man”** (Genesis 2:16). **Rabbi Yehuda ben Beteira says:** He was **also** commanded **concerning blessing the name** of God. **And some say** that he was **also** commanded **concerning** establishing courts of **judgment.**

Rabbi Yehuda offers a more minimalist position: *Adam HaRishon lo nitztaveh ela Avodah Zarah bilvad* — the first human being was commanded in nothing other than *avodah zarah* alone. Rashi suggests this need not mean that the remaining commandments were not eventually given; rather, the point is that they should not all be rooted in *Adam HaRishon*. The derivation turns on the verse *vayetzav Hashem Elokim al HaAdam*: the prohibition against eating from the *etz hadaat* does not apply to later generations, but what does carry forward is the fundamental idea embedded in the very fact of divine command — that there is a God who commands. *Vayetzav Hashem Elokim* conveys belief in and acceptance of God, and the rejection of *avodah zarah*, the substitution of any other divinity, flows from that foundational moment. Additional commandments, on this view, came later at the time of Noach.

### R. Yehuda ben Beteira

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן אָזְלָא הָא דְּאָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה אָמַר רַב: ״אֱלֹהִים אֲנִי״ – לֹא תְּקַלְּלוּנִי, ״אֱלֹהִים אֲנִי״ – לֹא תְּמִירוּנִי, ״אֱלֹהִים אֲנִי״ – יְהֵא מוֹרָאִי עֲלֵיכֶם? כְּמַאן? כְּיֵשׁ אוֹמְרִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **In accordance with whose** opinion **is that which Rav Yehuda says** that **Rav says,** in interpretation of the aforementioned verse: Since **I am “God,” do not curse Me;** since **I am “God,” do not exchange Me** with another god; since **I am “God,” My fear shall be upon you?** The Gemara answers: **In accordance with whose** opinion? It is **in accordance with** what **some say,** i.e., that the phrase “and the Lord God commanded the man” includes the prohibitions against cursing God’s name and idol worship, as well as the mitzva of establishing a system of law and justice, so that the fear of God will be upon the people.

Rabbi Yehuda ben Beteira takes a similar approach but broadens it modestly. He adds *birchat Hashem* to the obligations incumbent upon *Adam HaRishon*: if the core of what was demanded at the beginning was the recognition and acceptance of God, then cursing God — attacking the very foundation of that theological relationship — is equally foundational. *Birchat Hashem* is not a separate category but flows directly from the same principle.

*V'yesh omrim af al hadinim* — some add *dinim* as well. The *gemara* then asks: *keman azla ha de'amar Rav Yehuda amar Rav* — like whom does this teaching go? The teaching expounds *vayetzav Hashem Elokim*: *Elohim ani* — know that I am your God; *lo tekaleluni* — do not curse Me; *lo temiruni* — do not replace Me with another deity. What does the third clause — *yehei morai aleichem*, let My fear be upon you — come to include? According to this, *dinim*.

The connection between fear of God and the obligation of *dinim* requires some explanation. Rashi's interpretation is that truly fearing God means judging justly, because the fear of human beings leads to the perversion of justice, while the fear of God ensures honest judgment — *lo tateh mishpat ki hamishpat l'Elohim hu*. However, that reading presupposes the obligation to judge and uses the divine fear merely as a motivator for doing so properly. A more fundamental reading would be: what kind of God are you accepting? The pagan gods are amoral — they represent no absolute moral code. The Jewish conception of God is inseparable from moral truth: *chotamo shel Hakadosh Baruch Hu emet*. If you genuinely accept a God of this character, and if the fear of that God rests upon you, then you understand that society must be structured around justice. The obligation of *dinim* flows from the theological content of the acceptance of God itself.

This connects powerfully to the *pasuk* about Avraham: *v'shamru derech Hashem la'asot tzedakah u'mishpat*. There is something profound in saying that the most basic foundation — prior to Noach, prior to *ever min hachai* or *giluy arayos* — is belief in God and the understanding that this belief entails responsibility for a just society. This resonates, incidentally, with Mendelssohn's idea of natural law.

What unites these positions — Rabbi Yehuda, Rabbi Yehuda ben Beteira, and those who add *dinim* — is the recognition that *Adam HaRishon* received only the foundational universal principles: acceptance of God, not cursing God, and perhaps the framework of justice. The more specific commandments — explicit prohibitions of murder, of *ever min hachai*, and so forth — came later, at Noach.

## Sourcing from Noach



### Individual Sources

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתַנָּא דְבֵי מְנַשֶּׁה: אִי דָּרֵישׁ ״וַיְצַו״ – אֲפִילּוּ הָנָךְ נָמֵי, אִי לָא דָּרֵישׁ ״וַיְצַו״ – הָנֵי מְנָא לֵיהּ?
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **If** the ***tanna* of the school of Menashe interprets** the verse **“and** the Lord God **commanded” homiletically, even these** mitzvot, cursing the name of God and establishing courts, should be included. And **if he does not interpret** the verse **“and** the Lord God **commanded” homiletically, from where does he** derive **these** seven mitzvot in his list?

The *gemara* now turns to *Tana d'bei Menashe*'s approach: since this school substitutes *sirus* and *kilayim* for *birchat Hashem* and *dinim*, how does it derive its list from *pesukim*? The *gemara* poses the dilemma sharply: *ei darish vayetzav* — if *Tana d'bei Menashe* learns the commandments from the *pasuk* about *Adam HaRishon* (*vayetzav Hashem Elokim*), then it should include *birchat Hashem* and *dinim*, which are derived from that very *pasuk*. *Ei lo darish vayetzav* — and if it does not derive anything from that *pasuk*, then where does it derive any of the commandments at all?

The *gemara* answers: *le'olam lo darish vayetzav* — *Tana d'bei Menashe* does not base its system on the *pasuk* by *Adam HaRishon* at all. Instead, *kol chada v'chada b'apei nafsha ketiva* — each commandment is derived individually, and strikingly, all of them are derived from *Noach* rather than from *Adam*. This raises a significant conceptual question: were the *sheva mitzvos* given to *Adam HaRishon*, or to Noach, or in two stages? The difference is not merely historical. Noach came after ten generations of human failure, after the corruption that led to the destruction of the world. One way to understand the trajectory of *Sefer Bereishis* is that humans keep failing, requiring progressively more detailed guidance — first the basic idea of God alone, then the broader Noachide code, and ultimately the 613 commandments given to Israel. *Tana d'bei Menashe* links all seven to the Noachide period rather than to *Adam*.

### Avodah Zarah & Giluy

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְעוֹלָם לָא דָּרֵישׁ ״וַיְצַו״. הָנֵי, כֹּל חֲדָא וַחֲדָא בְּאַפֵּי נַפְשַׁהּ כְּתִיבָא: עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה וְגִילּוּי עֲרָיוֹת -
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Actually, he does not interpret** the verse **“and** the Lord God **commanded” homiletically,** but with regard to **these** mitzvot in his list, **each and every one** of them **is written separately** in the Torah. The prohibitions of **idol worship and forbidden sexual relations** are stated,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּכְתִיב: ״וַתִּשָּׁחֵת הָאָרֶץ לִפְנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים״. וְתָנָא דְּבֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל: בְּכׇל מָקוֹם שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר הַשְׁחָתָה, אֵינוֹ אֶלָּא דְּבַר עֶרְוָה וַעֲבוֹדָה זָרָה. דָּבָר עֶרְוָה – שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״כִּי הִשְׁחִית כׇּל בָּשָׂר אֶת דַּרְכּוֹ״. עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה – דִּכְתִיב: ״פֶּן תַּשְׁחִתוּן וַעֲשִׂיתֶם וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** **as it is written: “And the earth was corrupt before God”** (Genesis 6:11), presumably referring to a transgression, **and the school of Rabbi Yishmael taught: Anywhere that** the term **corruption is stated, it is** referring to **nothing other than a matter of licentiousness and idol worship.** The Gemara cites proofs for this claim: Corruption refers to **a matter of licentiousness, as it is stated: “For all flesh had corrupted their way** upon the earth” (Genesis 6:12); the word “way” alludes to sexual intercourse. **And** corruption also refers to **idol worship, as it is written: “Lest you deal corruptly, and make** you a graven image” (Deuteronomy 4:16).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִידָּךְ, אוֹרְחַיְיהוּ דְּקָא מְגַלֵּי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** how do **the other** *tanna’im*, who do not derive from the verse “And the earth was corrupt before God” that the descendants of Noah are prohibited from engaging in idol worship and forbidden sexual relations, interpret this verse? The Gemara answers: In their opinion, the verse merely **exposes the behavior of** the generation of Noah.

This section begins at the top of 57a. *Avodah zarah* and *giluy arayos* are derived together from the verse *vatishachet ha'aretz lifnei Ha'Elokim* — the earth was corrupted before God. The school of Rabbi Yishmael teaches: *kol makom shene'emar hashchasa eino ela dvar ervah va'avodas kochavim* — wherever the word *hashchasa* (corruption) appears, it refers to sexual immorality and idolatry. The proof for the association with sexual immorality is: *ki hishchis kol basar es darko al ha'aretz* — all flesh corrupted its way upon the earth. Rashi explains that the word *derech* evokes natural conduct, and *basar* carries a corporeal connotation, together pointing to the corruption of the natural pattern of sexuality — cross-species coupling and similar violations. The association of *hashchasa* with idolatry is proved from the verse *pen tashchisun va'asisem lachem pesel* — lest you act corruptly and fashion for yourselves an idol.

This does not constitute an explicit commandment in the ordinary sense; rather, if people are being punished for these acts, it may be inferred that they were commanded not to do them. The underlying principle is that every punishment presupposes a prior prohibition. This logic, of course, raises a question about Kayin: if the prohibition of murder was only commanded explicitly at the time of Noach — and this is indeed where the *gemara* will derive it — how could Kayin have been held responsible? The *gemara* does not explicitly address this. One resolution, drawing on a broader philosophical framework about the sin of the *etz hadaat*, is that the sin of the Garden of Eden consisted not merely in disobeying God but in arrogating to humanity the authority to determine what is good and evil — displacing divine moral judgment with human moral judgment. If that was the sin, then its aftermath is precisely that humans accepted responsibility for moral determination and are held accountable accordingly, regardless of explicit commandment. *Kayin* cannot claim he was never told murder was wrong, because the acceptance of autonomous moral judgment carries with it full moral accountability. This reading fits with the Rambam-Mendelssohn tension between commanded law and natural law: the sense of the *gemara* here is not that the *sheva mitzvos* are self-evident natural law that humans could have figured out independently, but rather that they are genuine divine commandments — for without commandment, the punishment would be unjust.

### Shfichus Damim

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים, דִּכְתִיב: ״שֹׁפֵךְ דַּם הָאָדָם וְגוֹ׳״. וְאִידַּךְ? קְטָלַיְיהוּ הוּא דְּקָמְגַלֵּי.
> **Translation:** According to the school of Menashe, the prohibition of **bloodshed** for the descendants of Noah is stated separately in the Torah, **as it is written: “One who sheds the blood of man,** by man his blood shall be shed” (Genesis 9:6). The Gemara asks: **And** how do **the other** *tanna’im* interpret this verse? The Gemara answers: In their opinion, the verse **reveals** the type of **death** penalty administered to the descendants of Noah, but it is not the source for the prohibition of bloodshed.

*Shfichus damim* is derived from the explicit divine command to Noach: *shofech dam ha'adam ba'adam damo yishafach* — one who sheds the blood of a person, by a person shall his blood be shed. This is among the few obligations stated expressly in the Noachide context, and it is noteworthy that the source is here rather than the Kayin narrative. The school that roots the prohibition in *Adam HaRishon* would read the Noachide verse differently: *katlei hu d'ka megalei* — it is not establishing the prohibition anew, but revealing the punishment. The prohibition was already known from the time of Adam; the verse by Noach tells us that the punishment is death.

### Gezel Source

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גָּזֵל, דִּכְתִיב: ״כְּיֶרֶק עֵשֶׂב נָתַתִּי לָכֶם אֶת כֹּל״. וְאָמַר רַבִּי לֵוִי: כְּיֶרֶק עֵשֶׂב, וְלֹא כְּיֶרֶק גִּנָּה. וְאִידַּךְ? הָהוּא לְמִישְׁרֵי בָּשָׂר הוּא דַּאֲתָא.
> **Translation:** The prohibition of **robbery** is stated, according to the school of Menashe, **as it is written:** “Every moving thing that is alive shall be for food for you; **like the green herbs I have given you all”** (Genesis 9:3). **And Rabbi Levi says: Like the green herbs** that sprout all over by themselves and are ownerless, **and not like the vegetation of a garden,** which belongs to the garden’s owner alone. This indicates that robbery is prohibited. The Gemara asks: **And** how do **the other** *tanna’im* interpret this verse? The Gemara answers: In their opinion, **that** verse **comes to permit** the consumption of **meat.**

*Gezel* is derived from the verse addressed to Noach: *ki yerek esev nasati lachem et hakol* — as the green vegetation I have given you everything. Rav Levi explains: *ki yerek esev v'lo k'yerek gina* — like grass that grows wild, not like the produce of a cultivated garden. Wild vegetation is ownerless; produce of a garden belongs to its owner. The implication is that while God permits the taking of what is ownerless, what belongs to another may not be taken — hence the prohibition of *gezel*.

The school that derives *gezel* from *Adam HaRishon* reads this verse differently: *hu l'mishrei basar hu d'asa* — the verse comes to permit Noach to eat meat. The understanding is that *Adam HaRishon* was permitted only vegetation; the flesh of animals was not given to him. Here, by Noach, meat is for the first time permitted, as God now includes the animals in the category of what may be consumed. On this reading the verse is not about theft at all. It is worth pausing on the consequence this view carries: if meat was already forbidden to *Adam HaRishon* entirely, not merely its violent or premature taking, then the need for a separate prohibition of *ever min hachai* addressed to him becomes genuinely puzzling — a question that the next section addresses directly.

### Ever Min Hachai

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵבֶר מִן הַחַי, דִּכְתִיב: ״אַךְ בָּשָׂר בְּנַפְשׁוֹ דָמוֹ לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ״. וְאִידַּךְ? הָהוּא לְמִישְׁרֵי שְׁרָצִים הוּא דַּאֲתָא.
> **Translation:** The prohibition against eating **a limb from a living** animal is stated in the Torah, **as it is written: “Only flesh with its life, which is its blood, you shall not eat”** (Genesis 9:4), i.e., it is prohibited to eat flesh while the animal that it comes from is still alive. **And** how do **the other** *tanna’im* interpret this verse? In their opinion, **that** verse **comes to permit** eating a limb from living **creeping animals;** this prohibition does not apply to creeping animals (see 59b).

The prohibition of *ever min hachai* is derived from the verse: *ach basar b'nafsho damo lo socheilu* — but flesh with its life, its blood, you shall not eat. The rabbinic reading of this verse is: flesh of an animal while its blood and life force remain within it — that is, while the animal is still alive — may not be eaten. Do not eat the limb of a living animal. It is worth noting that the plain reading of the *pasuk* — and one that is entirely defensible as *peshat* — is a prohibition on eating blood: eat the flesh, but not while its blood is still within it, meaning drain the blood from the meat first. The rabbis, however, read it as a prohibition on *ever min hachai*.

The school that roots *ever min hachai* in *Adam HaRishon* reads this Noachide verse differently: *hahu l'mishrei shratzim hu d'asa* — it comes to clarify that the blood of rodents is not in the category of blood but is treated like their flesh, and therefore does not render them subject to the *ever min hachai* prohibition. The conceptual distinction underlying the prohibition of *ever min hachai* is the separateness of the life force from the body — a kind of body-soul dualism where the blood represents a life force distinct from the physical flesh. Rodents are not understood in these terms; their blood is considered like their flesh, and so the prohibition does not apply to them.

This analysis also resolves a prior difficulty: if *Adam HaRishon* was forbidden to eat meat entirely, why would a prohibition on *ever min hachai* be necessary? The answer is that *Adam HaRishon* was not required to be vegetarian in the sense of being forbidden from eating any animal flesh whatsoever. Rather, he was forbidden to kill animals for food. He was, however, permitted to eat from the flesh of animals that had died naturally. Consequently, a separate prohibition was needed to forbid tearing a limb from a living animal — even if one were not killing the animal, ripping a limb from it while alive was independently prohibited.

### Sirus & Kilayim

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סֵירוּס, דִּכְתִיב: ״שִׁרְצוּ בָאָרֶץ וּרְבוּ בָהּ״. וְאִידַּךְ? לִבְרָכָה בְּעָלְמָא.
> **Translation:** The prohibition of **castration** that applies to the descendants of Noah is stated, **as it is written:** “And you be fruitful and multiply, **swarm in the earth and multiply in it”** (Genesis 9:7), indicating that nothing may be done to prevent reproduction. **And the other** *tanna’im* hold that this verse is written **merely as a blessing,** not as a mitzva.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּלְאַיִם, דִּכְתִיב: ״מֵהָעוֹף לְמִינֵהוּ״. וְאִידַּךְ? הָהוּא לְצַוְתָּא בְּעָלְמָא.
> **Translation:** The prohibition of **diverse kinds** that applies to the descendants of Noah is stated, **as it is written: “Of the fowl after their kind** and of the cattle after their kind, of every creeping thing of the ground after its kind” (Genesis 6:20), indicating that each species must be kept separate, and that crossbreeding is prohibited. **And** according to **the other** *tanna’im*, **that** verse does not indicate a mitzva; rather, the reason for keeping the species separate in Noah’s Ark was **merely for** the sake of **companionship,** as animals are most comfortable in the company of other members of their own species.

*Sirus* is derived from the command given to Noach: *shirtzу ba'aretz ur'vu vah* — swarm upon the earth and multiply in it — a form of *peru u'revu*. The opposing school reads this verse as a blessing rather than a command, and therefore draws no prohibition from it: *l'vircha d'alma* — it is a blessing for the world, not an imperative.

*Kilayim* is derived from the verse describing the animals brought into the ark: *meh'of l'minehu* — the birds according to their species. The requirement to maintain species distinction in the ark implies a prohibition on crossbreeding. The opposing school reads this verse as reflecting companionship — animals naturally prefer the company of their own kind — rather than as a prohibition: *l'tzavsa d'alma* — for the sake of fellowship, not as a legal boundary.

What is striking about *Tana d'bei Menashe*'s list is the exclusion of *dinim* — a prohibition whose absence seems conceptually difficult. If the seven commandments are meant to constitute the foundations of a just society, the omission of any obligation to establish and maintain a system of law is puzzling. *Sirus* and *kilayim*, while certainly rooted in the deepest structures of creation — the imperatives of procreation and species integrity — seem to belong to a different register than *dinim*. One might speculate that *dinim* is excluded on the grounds that it is sufficiently self-evident as natural law that it need not be explicitly commanded — a partial accommodation between the Mendelssohn natural-law view and the Rambam's commanded-law view. However, this does not sit entirely comfortably, since *shfichus damim* — which is no less intuitive — remains among the commanded prohibitions.

## Death Penalty Issues



### Three Mitzvot View

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: אָמְרִי בֵּי רַב, עַל שָׁלֹשׁ מִצְוֹת בֶּן נֹחַ נֶהֱרָג (גֶּשֶׁר סִימָן) – עַל גִּילּוּי עֲרָיוֹת, וְעַל שְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים, וְעַל בִּרְכַּת הַשֵּׁם.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yosef says: They say** in the **study hall** that **a descendant of Noah is executed for** transgressing **three mitzvot,** which are represented by the letters ***gimmel*, *shin*, *reish*** in **a mnemonic** device: **For forbidden sexual relations, for bloodshed, and for blessing,** i.e., cursing, **the name** of God.

Having surveyed the scope and sources of the *sheva mitzvos*, the *gemara* turns to a distinct question: for how many of these commandments is a *ben Noach* actually executed? The widespread popular understanding is that the death penalty applies to all seven, and even for theft of less than a *prutah*. But the *gemara* reveals a spectrum of opinions.

*Amar Rav Yosef amar bei Rav al shalosh mitzvos ben Noach neherag* — Rav Yosef in the name of the school of Rav: a *ben Noach* is executed for only three. The mnemonic is *gimmel-shin-resh* (*gesher*): *giluy arayos*, *shfichus damim*, and *birchat Hashem*. This is a notably unusual grouping — it omits *avodah zarah*, which for Jews forms the first of the three cardinal sins, and substitutes *birchat Hashem* in its place.

### Four Mitzvot View

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְקֵיף לַהּ רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: בִּשְׁלָמָא שְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים, דִּכְתִיב ״שֹׁפֵךְ דַּם הָאָדָם וְגוֹ׳״, אֶלָּא הָנָךְ מְנָא לְהוּ?
> **Translation:** **Rav Sheshet objects to this** statement: **Granted,** a descendant of Noah is executed for **bloodshed, as it is written: “One who sheds the blood of man,** by man his blood shall be shed” (Genesis 9:6). **But** with regard to **those** other prohibitions, **from where do** the Sages derive that a descendant of Noah who transgresses them is executed?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי גָּמַר מִשְּׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים, אֲפִילּוּ כּוּלְּהוּ נָמֵי. אִי מִשּׁוּם דְּאִיתְרַבַּאי מֵ״אִישׁ אִישׁ״, עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה נָמֵי אִיתְרַבַּי מֵ״אִישׁ אִישׁ״.
> **Translation:** **If** they **derive** it **from** the punishment for **bloodshed** by means of an analogy, then descendants of Noah should be executed **even** if they transgressed **any of** the other Noahide mitzvot. **If** they are executed **because they are included in** the term **“anyone”** and similarly, the term “no one” stated with regard to these two prohibitions, as it is stated with regard to cursing the name of God: “Anyone who curses his God shall bear his sin” (Leviticus 24:15), and it is stated with regard to forbidden sexual relations: “No one shall approach any that is near of kin to him, to uncover their nakedness” (Leviticus 18:6), then gentiles should be executed for **idol worship too,** as **they are included in** the term **“anyone”** stated in that context (see Leviticus 20:2).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: אָמְרִי בֵּי רַב, עַל אַרְבַּע מִצְוֹת בֶּן נֹחַ נֶהֱרָג.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rav Sheshet says** that Rav Yosef’s version should be rejected, and that this is what **they say** in the **study hall: A descendant of Noah is executed for** transgressing **four mitzvot;** the three that were listed, and idol worship.

Rav Sheshes challenges this position. *Bishlama shfichus damim d'chtiv* — granted, murder is explicit: *shofech dam ha'adam ba'adam damo yishafach*. But for the other two — *giluy arayos* and *birchat Hashem* — where is the capital liability derived? If the answer is that we extend the death penalty from *shfichus damim* to the other *sheva mitzvos*, then *afilu kulhu nami* — extend it to all seven. If we are not doing that, we need a specific *pasuk* for each.

The *gemara* identifies the derivation: by *birchat Hashem*, the verse says *ish ish ki yekalel elohav v'nasa cheto* — followed ultimately by *ragom yirgmu*. The phrase *ish ish* is understood as including non-Jews, thereby establishing that the capital liability of the *birchat Hashem* prohibition extends to *bnei Noach*. Similarly, by *giluy arayos*, the verse reads *ish ish el kol she'er besaro lo tikrevu legalot ervah* — again, the double *ish ish* signals inclusion of non-Jews. For *avodah zarah*, there is also a verse using *ish ish*: *ish ish mi-bnei Yisrael u'min ha-ger ha-gar b'Yisrael asher yiten mi-zar'o la-Molech mos yumos* — making it four, not three.

It is worth pausing on what it means, conceptually, that all three of these derivations — *birchat Hashem*, *giluy arayos*, and *avodah zarah* — work by the same mechanism of *ish ish*. What we are not saying is that this is where *bnei Noach* were originally commanded in these prohibitions. The question of whether such a derivation reveals that *bnei Noach* were always commanded from the time of *Noach* — and the *pasuk* in the laws given to *Bnei Yisrael* is merely hinting at something already true — or whether it implies some deeper conceptual connection between *bnei Noach* and the *mitzvah* as given to *Yisrael*, is a question the *gemara* will take up as we turn the page. Here, the *ish ish* derivation is doing a narrower job: it is telling us specifically about the death penalty. Because *ish ish* appears in each of these three cases and not across all seven commandments, the mechanism itself explains why capital liability is limited and does not simply radiate outward to all the *sheva mitzvos*.

The conclusion: *ella amar Rav Sheshes hani amri bei Rav al arba mitzvos ben Noach neherag* — the correct version of the teaching in the name of Rav's school is that a *ben Noach* is executed for four commandments: the three cardinal sins of *avodah zarah*, *giluy arayos*, and *shfichus damim*, together with *birchat Hashem*, which is in a sense an extension of *avodah zarah* — the rejection of God's exclusive sovereignty.

This is conceptually satisfying: these four are precisely the commandments for which Jews are also executed, at least in their most serious forms. The remaining three — *gezel*, *ever min hachai*, and *dinim* — while prohibited, do not carry the death penalty on this view.

### Azharah Shelo Mita

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעַל עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה בֶּן נֹחַ נֶהֱרָג? וְהָתַנְיָא: בַּעֲבוֹדָה זָרָה, דְּבָרִים שֶׁבֵּית דִּין שֶׁל יִשְׂרָאֵל מְמִיתִין עֲלֵיהֶן – בֶּן נֹחַ מוּזְהָר עֲלֵיהֶן. אַזְהָרָה – אִין, מִיתָה – לָא! אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: אַזְהָרָה שֶׁלָּהֶן – זוֹ הִיא מִיתָתָן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And is a descendant of Noah executed for idol worship? But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita*: **With regard to idol worship, matters for which a Jewish court executes** the transgressor **are prohibited to a descendant of Noah.** The Gemara infers: **Yes,** there is **a prohibition** for a descendant of Noah, but there is **no death** penalty. **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak says: Their prohibition is their death** penalty. Since the only punishment mentioned in the Torah for transgressing a Noahide mitzva is execution, any descendant of Noah who transgresses is liable to be executed.

The *gemara* now tests this conclusion against a *braisa* that discusses the scope of the *avodah zarah* prohibition for *bnei Noach*. The *braisa* states: acts of *avodah zarah* for which a Jewish court would execute Jews — a *ben Noach* is *muzhar* (warned/prohibited) regarding those. Notably, the *braisa* does not say *neherag* (executed); it says *muzhar* (cautioned, prohibited). The inference is stark: *azharah in, mitah lo* — there is a prohibition, but not a death penalty. If so, the *braisa* itself seems to undercut the position that *bnei Noach* are executed for *avodah zarah*.

The answer offered is that of Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak: *azharah shelahen zu hi mitatan* — their warning is their death penalty. On its most common philosophical interpretation, this principle means that any violation of a divine prohibition is in principle deserving of death, since God who prohibits is also the source of all life; any transgression of the divine will constitutes a forfeiture of the right to life in principle, and all lesser punishments represent divine leniency. This is a harsh and austere theological stance. Rav Soloveitchik noted, in a related context, that the final passage of the *Neilah* service includes the phrase *me-chamas yadeinu* — from the *chamas* of our hands — suggesting that any sin involves a kind of theft from God, since we were given our lives to serve the divine will, and any violation represents a misappropriation of that life.

However, Rashi indicates that this is not the intended meaning here. The *gemara* at this juncture is working with the position that only four of the seven commandments carry the death penalty, not all seven. Therefore the phrase *azharah shelahen zu hi mitatan* is not a philosophical statement about the nature of divine prohibition; it is a purely textual observation. The *braisa*, by using the word *muzhar* (warned), is not carefully distinguishing between warning and execution; it is simply not being specific. When it says they are warned against *avodah zarah*, it means they are executed for it — the word *azharah* is being used loosely to encompass the full consequence.

### Seven Mitzvot Death

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב הוּנָא וְרַב יְהוּדָה וְכוּלְּהוּ תַּלְמִידֵי דְּרַב אָמְרִי: עַל שֶׁבַע מִצְוֹת בֶּן נֹחַ נֶהֱרָג. גַּלִּי רַחֲמָנָא בַּחֲדָא, וְהוּא הַדִּין לְכוּלְּהוּ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Huna, Rav Yehuda, and all of the** other **students of Rav say: A descendant of Noah is executed for** transgressing any of the **seven** Noahide **mitzvot; the Merciful One revealed** this punishment **with regard to one** mitzva, the prohibition of bloodshed, **and the same is true with regard to all of them.**

The students of Rav — *Rav Huna, Rav Yehudah, v'kulehu talmidei de-Rav* — take the broadest position: *al sheva mitzvos Bnei Noach neherag* — a *ben Noach* is executed for all seven. The logic is: *gali Rachmana b'chada v'hu ha-din le-kulehu* — the Torah reveals the death penalty explicitly for one of the commandments (murder), and the same rule extends to all of them. This is the position that is most commonly cited and that underlies the widespread understanding of the *sheva mitzvos*: any violation, even theft of less than a *prutah*, is in principle punishable by death. It is here that the phrase *azharah shelahen zu hi mitatan* takes on its fuller philosophical resonance — any divine prohibition directed at *bnei Noach* carries the implicit consequence of death.

## Gezel Scope & Liability



[DAF:57b]
### Gezel Braita

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעַל הַגָּזֵל בֶּן נֹחַ נֶהֱרָג? וְהָתַנְיָא: עַל הַגָּזֵל, גָּנַב וְגָזַל, וְכֵן יְפַת תּוֹאַר, וְכֵן כַּיּוֹצֵא בָּהֶן – גּוֹי בְּגוֹי וְגוֹי בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל אָסוּר, וְיִשְׂרָאֵל בְּגוֹי מוּתָּר. וְאִם אִיתָא, נִיתְנֵי ״חַיָּיב״!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But is a descendant of Noah executed for robbery? But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita*: **With regard to** the following types of **robbery:** One who **steals or robs, and likewise** one who engages in intercourse with a married **beautiful woman** who was taken as a prisoner of war, **and likewise** all actions **similar to these,** if they are done by **a gentile to** another **gentile, or** by **a gentile to a Jew,** the action is **prohibited; but** if **a Jew** does so **to a gentile, it is permitted?** The Gemara explains the question: **And if it is so** that a gentile is liable to be executed for robbery, and it is not merely prohibited to him, **let** the *baraita* **teach** that he is **liable** to be executed.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִשּׁוּם דְּקָבָעֵי לְמִיתְנֵי סֵיפָא: ״יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּגוֹי מוּתָּר״, תְּנָא רֵישָׁא: ״אָסוּר״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Because** the *tanna* **wanted to teach** in **the latter clause** that if **a Jew** does so **to a gentile, it is permitted, he taught** in **the former clause** that if a gentile does one of these, **it is prohibited.** If the *baraita* were to state that if a gentile does so, he is liable, it would have to state that if a Jew does so to a gentile, he is exempt, because this is the opposite of liable. That would indicate that it is actually prohibited for a Jew to do so to a gentile, and that he is merely exempt from liability, which is not the case. Therefore, the word prohibited is used with regard to a gentile. Therefore, this does not prove that a gentile is exempt from capital punishment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא כֹּל הֵיכָא דְּאִית לֵיהּ חִיּוּבָא מִיתְנָא קָתָנֵי, דְּקָתָנֵי רֵישָׁא: עַל שְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים – גּוֹי בְּגוֹי וְגוֹי בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל חַיָּיב, יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּגוֹי פָּטוּר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **But wherever there is liability** for capital punishment, this *tanna* **teaches** it; **as it is taught** in **the first clause: With regard to bloodshed,** if **a gentile** murders another **gentile, or a gentile** murders **a Jew,** he is **liable.** If **a Jew** murders **a gentile,** he is **exempt.** Evidently, the term liable is used in the *baraita*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָתָם, הֵיכִי לִיתְנֵי? לִיתְנֵי ״אָסוּר״ וּ״מוּתָּר״? וְהָתַנְיָא: גּוֹי, וְרוֹעֵי בְּהֵמָה דַּקָּה – לֹא מַעֲלִין וְלֹא מוֹרִידִין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **There,** in that case, **how should** the *tanna* **teach** it? **Should he teach** it using the terms **prohibited and permitted,** indicating that a Jew may kill a gentile *ab initio*? **But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita* that with regard to **a gentile, and** likewise with regard to Jewish **shepherds of small livestock,** who were typically robbers, **one may not raise** them out of a pit into which they fell, **and one may not lower** them into a pit? In other words, one may not rescue them from danger, but neither may one kill them *ab initio*. With regard to robbery, the term permitted is relevant, as it is permitted for a Jew to rob a gentile.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כַּיּוֹצֵא בּוֹ בְּגָזֵל, מַאי הִיא? אָמַר רַב אַחָא בַּר יַעֲקֹב: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא לְפוֹעֵל בַּכֶּרֶם.
> **Translation:** The Gemara returns to discuss the details of the prohibition of robbery mentioned in the *baraita*, which included actions similar to it. The Gemara asks: **With regard to robbery,** to **what** actions **similar to it is** the *baraita* referring? **Rav Aḥa bar Ya’akov says: It is necessary only to** teach the *halakha* of **a laborer** working **in a vineyard** who eats from the fruit of the vineyard; his action is similar to robbery, and it is prohibited for a gentile to do so.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פּוֹעֵל בַּכֶּרֶם אֵימַת? אִי בִּשְׁעַת גְּמַר מְלָאכָה – הֶתֵּירָא הוּא, אִי לָאו בִּשְׁעַת גְּמַר מְלָאכָה – גָּזֵל מְעַלְּיָא הוּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **When** does this **laborer in a vineyard** eat from the fruit? **If** he does so **at the time of the completion of** the **work,** i.e., while he is harvesting the fruit, **it is permitted** for him to do so, just as a Jew working for another Jew is allowed to do so. **If it is not at the time of the completion of** its **work,** eating the fruit **is full-fledged robbery,** and there is no novel element to this case.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא לְפָחוֹת מִשָּׁוֶה פְּרוּטָה. אִי הָכִי, גּוֹי בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל אָסוּר? הָא בַּר מְחִילָה הוּא! נְהִי דְּבָתַר הָכִי מָחֵיל לֵיהּ, צַעֲרָא בְּשַׁעְתֵּיהּ מִי לֵית לֵיהּ?
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rav Pappa says** that the mention in the *baraita* of actions similar to robbery **is necessary only to** teach the *halakha* of one who robs another of **less than the value of one *peruta*.** The Gemara asks: **If so,** why does the *baraita* state that it is **prohibited** for **a gentile** to do so **to a Jew? Isn’t** a Jew **apt to forgive** such a tiny debt? Why is this considered robbery? The Gemara answers: **Although afterward** the owner **forgives him, does he not incur distress at the time of** the robbery? Consequently, at the time of the robbery the robber commits a transgression and is liable to be punished for it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוֹי בְּגוֹי כַּיּוֹצֵא בָּהֶן, כֵּיוָן דְּלָאו בְּנֵי מְחִילָה נִינְהוּ – גָּזֵל מְעַלְּיָא הוּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: If the mention of actions **similar to** robbery is referring to the robbery of less than the value of one *peruta*, what is the novel element in the case of **a gentile** who robs **a gentile? Since they are not apt to** grant **forgiveness,** robbing a gentile of even a minuscule amount **is** considered **full-fledged robbery,** and not merely similar to robbery.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, אָמַר רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב אִיקָא: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא לְכוֹבֵשׁ שְׂכַר שָׂכִיר. גּוֹי בְּגוֹי וְגוֹי בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל – אָסוּר; יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּגוֹי – מוּתָּר.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika, says** that there is a different explanation: **It is necessary only to** teach the *halakha* of one who **withholds the wages of a hired laborer;** for **a gentile** to do so **to** another **gentile and** for **a gentile** to do so **to a Jew is prohibited,** but for **a Jew** to do so **to a gentile is permitted.** This case is less obvious than other types of robbery, as instead of taking an item from the victim, the robber withholds money that is due to the victim.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כַּיּוֹצֵא בִּיפַת תּוֹאַר, מַאי הִיא? כִּי אֲתָא רַב דִּימִי, אָמַר: רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר אָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: בֶּן נֹחַ שֶׁיִּיחֵד שִׁפְחָה לְעַבְדּוֹ וּבָא עָלֶיהָ – נֶהֱרָג עָלֶיהָ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara clarifies further: **What is** the action that is **similar to** engaging in intercourse with **a beautiful woman** who is a prisoner of war, to which the *baraita* is referring? **When Rav Dimi came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia, he said that **Rabbi Elazar says** that **Rabbi Ḥanina says:** In the case of **a descendant of Noah who designated a maidservant** as a mate **for his slave, and** then he himself **engaged in intercourse with her, he is executed on her account.** Although the maidservant is his property and is not the slave’s full-fledged wife, nevertheless, he is guilty of adultery.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כַּיּוֹצֵא בּוֹ דִּשְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים לָא תַּנְיָא. אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: אִי מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי יוֹנָתָן בֶּן שָׁאוּל הִיא. דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי יוֹנָתָן בֶּן שָׁאוּל אוֹמֵר: רוֹדֵף אַחַר חֲבֵירוֹ לְהוֹרְגוֹ, וְיָכוֹל לְהַצִּילוֹ בְּאֶחָד מֵאֵבָרָיו וְלֹא הִצִּיל -
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: The *baraita* **does not teach** that a descendant of Noah is liable for actions **similar to bloodshed. Abaye says: If you find** a *baraita* **that teaches** this, **it is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yonatan ben Shaul. As it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Yonatan ben Shaul says:** If a person **pursues another to kill him, and** the one being pursued **can save himself by** injuring **one of the limbs of** the pursuer, **but he does not save** himself in this manner and instead kills the pursuer,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נֶהֱרָג עָלָיו.
> **Translation:** he **is executed for** killing **him** even though he acted in self-defense, and a descendant of Noah is also killed for this.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַשְׁכַּח רַבִּי יַעֲקֹב בַּר אַחָא דַּהֲוָה כְּתִיב בְּסֵפֶר אַגַּדְתָּא דְּבֵי רַב: בֶּן נֹחַ נֶהֱרָג בְּדַיָּין אֶחָד, וּבְעֵד אֶחָד, שֶׁלֹּא בְּהַתְרָאָה, מִפִּי אִישׁ וְלֹא מִפִּי אִשָּׁה, וַאֲפִילּוּ קָרוֹב. מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל אָמְרוּ: אַף עַל הָעוּבָּרִין.
> **Translation:** § **Rabbi Ya’akov bar Aḥa found that it was written in a book of *Aggadot* in the study hall of Rav:** Contrary to the *halakha* with regard to a Jew, **a descendant of Noah is executed on the basis of** the verdict of even **one judge, and by** the testimony of even **one witness,** and **without** being given **forewarning** before committing the transgression. He can be judged or testified against only **by the mouth of a man and not by the mouth of a woman; but even a relative** may judge his case or testify against him. The Sages **said in the name of Rabbi Yishmael** that a descendant of Noah is executed **even for** killing **fetuses.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מְנָהָנֵי מִילֵּי? אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: דְּאָמַר קְרָא, ״אַךְ אֶת דִּמְכֶם לְנַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם אֶדְרֹשׁ״, אֲפִילּוּ בְּדַיָּין אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **From where are these matters** derived? **Rav Yehuda says:** They are derived from that **which the verse states:** “And **your blood of your lives I will require;** at the hand of every animal I will require it; and at the hand of man, even at the hand of every man’s brother, I will require the life of man” (Genesis 9:5). It is derived from the term “I will require,” which is stated in the singular, that a descendant of Noah is executed **on the basis** of the verdict of **even one judge.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״מִיָּד כׇּל חַיָּה״, אֲפִילּוּ שֶׁלֹּא בְּהַתְרָאָה. ״אֶדְרְשֶׁנּוּ וּמִיַּד הָאָדָם״, אֲפִילּוּ בְּעֵד אֶחָד. ״מִיַּד אִישׁ״, וְלֹא מִיַּד אִשָּׁה. ״אָחִיו״, אֲפִילּוּ קָרוֹב.
> **Translation:** It is derived from the phrase **“at the hand of every animal”** that one is executed **even without forewarning,** as an animal certainly cannot forewarn someone. It is derived from the phrase **“I will require it; and at the hand of man,”** with “I” stated in the singular, that the sentence is issued **on the basis** of the testimony of **even one witness.** It is derived from the phrase **“at the hand of every man,”** that the judgment and testimony must be at the hand of a man, **but not at the hand of a woman.** It is derived from the term **“his brother”** that the testimony of the witness is accepted **even** if he is **a relative** of the defendant.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל אָמְרוּ: אַף עַל הָעוּבָּרִין. מַאי טַעְמֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל? דִּכְתִיב: ״שֹׁפֵךְ דַּם הָאָדָם בָּאָדָם דָּמוֹ יִשָּׁפֵךְ״. אֵיזֶהוּ אָדָם שֶׁהוּא בָּאָדָם? הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר: זֶה עוּבָּר שֶׁבִּמְעֵי אִמּוֹ.
> **Translation:** It is stated in that book of *Aggadot* that the Sages **said in the name of Rabbi Yishmael:** A descendant of Noah is executed **even for** killing **fetuses.** The Gemara asks: **What is the reason** for the opinion **of Rabbi Yishmael?** The Gemara answers: It is derived from that **which is written: “One who sheds the blood of a person, by a person [*ba’adam*] his blood shall be shed”** (Genesis 9:6). The word *ba’adam* literally means: In a person, and is interpreted homiletically: **What is a person that is in a person? You must say: This is a fetus that is in its mother’s womb.** Accordingly, a descendant of Noah is liable for killing a fetus.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתַנָּא קַמָּא תַּנָּא דְבֵי מְנַשֶּׁה הוּא, דְּאָמַר: כׇּל מִיתָה הָאֲמוּרָה לִבְנֵי נֹחַ אֵינוֹ אֶלָּא חֶנֶק, וּשְׁדִי לֵיהּ הַאי ״בָּאָדָם״ אַסֵּיפֵיהּ דִּקְרָא, וּדְרוֹשׁ בֵּיהּ הָכִי: ״בָּאָדָם דָּמוֹ יִשָּׁפֵךְ״ – אֵיזֶהוּ שְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים שֶׁל אָדָם שֶׁהוּא בְּגוּפוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם? הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר: זֶה חֶנֶק.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: **And the first *tanna*,** who does not derive the *halakha* concerning fetuses, **is the *tanna* of the school of Menashe, who says** that **all death** penalties **stated with regard to the descendants of Noah are** referring to **nothing other than strangulation. And** he interprets this verse as follows: **Cast,** i.e., redirect, **this** term: **“In a person,”** and explain it **with regard to the latter** part **of the verse, and interpret it homiletically like this: “In a person, his blood shall be shed.”** In **what** manner **is a person’s blood shed while it is in** the **person’s body,** without external bleeding? **You must say** that **this is strangulation.** It is therefore derived that the execution of a descendant of Noah is by strangulation.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵתִיב רַב הַמְנוּנָא: וְאִשָּׁה לָא מִפַּקְדָה? וְהָכְתִיב: ״כִּי יְדַעְתִּיו לְמַעַן אֲשֶׁר יְצַוֶּה וְגוֹ׳״!
> **Translation:** **Rav Hamnuna raises an objection** to the statement in the book of *Aggadot* that a descendant of Noah can be judged or testified against only by a man and not by a woman: **And is a woman** who is a descendant of Noah **not commanded** to establish courts of judgment? **But isn’t it written** with regard to Abraham, who at that point had the status of a descendant of Noah: **“For I have known him, to the end that he may command** his sons and his household after him, that they may keep the way of the Lord, to do righteousness and justice” (Genesis 18:19). The word “household” is referring to the women, indicating that they are also commanded to execute justice.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הוּא מוֹתֵיב לַהּ וְהוּא מְפָרֵק לַהּ: ״בָּנָיו״ – לְדִין, ״בֵּיתוֹ״ – לִצְדָקָה.
> **Translation:** **He raises the objection and he resolves it:** Abraham commanded **his sons to** carry out **justice,** whereas **his household,** the women in his family, he commanded **to** give **charity;** the Hebrew word for righteousness [*tzedek*] can also mean charity [*tzedaka*].

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב אַוְיָא סָבָא לְרַב פָּפָּא: אֵימָא בַּת נֹחַ שֶׁהָרְגָה לֹא תֵּיהָרֵג, ״מִיַּד אִישׁ״, וְלֹא ״מִיַּד אִשָּׁה״ כְּתִיב? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: הָכִי אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: ״שֹׁפֵךְ דַּם הָאָדָם״ – מִכׇּל מָקוֹם.
> **Translation:** **Rav Avya the Elder said to Rav Pappa:** Why not **say** that **a female descendant of Noah who killed** someone **should not be executed;** as **it is written: “At the hand of every man,” and not “at the hand of every woman”?** Rav Pappa **said to him: This** is what **Rav Yehuda says:** It is derived from the phrase **“one who sheds the blood of a person”** that one who murders is liable to be executed **in any case,** whether that person is male or female.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵימָא: בַּת נֹחַ שֶׁזִּינְּתָה לֹא תֵּיהָרֵג, דִּכְתִיב ״עַל כֵּן יַעֲזׇב אִישׁ״ וְלֹא אִשָּׁה? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: הָכִי אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: ״וְהָיוּ לְבָשָׂר אֶחָד״ – הֲדַר עָרְבִינְהוּ קְרָא.
> **Translation:** Rav Avya asked further: Why not **say** that **a female descendant of Noah who committed adultery should not be executed, as it is written: “Therefore a man shall leave** his father and his mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and they shall be one flesh” (Genesis 2:24); a man, **but not a woman?** Rav Pappa **said to him: This** is what **Rav Yehuda says:** At the end of the verse it states: **“And they shall be one flesh.” The verse then combines** men and women, indicating that the same *halakha* applies to both.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״אִישׁ״, מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״אִישׁ אִישׁ״? לְרַבּוֹת אֶת הַגּוֹיִים, שֶׁמּוּזְהָרִין עַל הָעֲרָיוֹת כְּיִשְׂרָאֵל.
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught** in a *baraita* with regard to the verse: “No one [*ish ish*] shall approach any that is kin to him, to uncover their nakedness” (Leviticus 18:6): The verse could have stated: **One [*ish*]** shall not approach. **Why** must **the verse state “no one”?** It is **to include the gentiles, who are prohibited from** engaging in **forbidden sexual relations, as Jews** are.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא מֵהָכָא נָפְקָא? מֵהָתָם נָפְקָא! ״לֵאמֹר״ – זֶה גִּילּוּי עֲרָיוֹת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But is it derived from here? It is derived from there,** from the verse that was already interpreted as teaching this *halakha*: “And the Lord God commanded the man, **saying”** (Genesis 2:16), **this** alludes to **forbidden sexual relations** (see 56b).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָתָם בַּעֲרָיוֹת דִּידְהוּ, וְהָכָא בַּעֲרָיוֹת דִּידַן, דְּקָתָנֵי סֵיפָא: בָּא עַל עֲרָיוֹת יִשְׂרָאֵל – נִידּוֹן בְּדִינֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **There,** the verse is referring **to their** women, gentiles, **with whom relations are forbidden. And here** it is referring **to our** women, Jews, **with whom relations are forbidden.** In other words, a gentile who engages in intercourse with a married Jewish woman is liable. **As it is taught in the latter clause** of the *baraita*: If a gentile **engages in intercourse with those Jewish** women **with whom relations are forbidden,** i.e., a married Jewish woman, **he is judged according to the *halakhot* of the Jews.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְמַאי הִלְכְתָא? אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר אֲבוּהּ: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא לָעֵדָה, וְעֵדִים, וְהַתְרָאָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **With regard to what *halakha*** is this gentile judged according to the *halakhot* of the Jews? **Rav Naḥman says** that **Rabba bar Avuh says:** The statement of the *baraita* **is necessary only** to teach these *halakhot*: That he must be judged **by a Sanhedrin, and** that he is punished only if two **witnesses** testify concerning him, **and** only if he was issued **a forewarning** before his transgression.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִגְרָע גָּרַע?
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: Should the *halakha* of a gentile who engaged in intercourse with a forbidden Jewish woman be **less** stringent than that of a gentile who engaged in intercourse with a forbidden gentile woman, in which case these conditions do not apply?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַב יוֹחָנָן: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא לְנַעֲרָה הַמְאוֹרָסָה, דִּלְדִידְהוּ לֵית לְהוּ, דְּדָיְינִינַן לְהוּ בְּדִינָא דִּידַן.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rabbi Yoḥanan says:** The statement of the *baraita* **is necessary only to** teach the *halakha* in the case of a gentile who engages in intercourse with **a betrothed young** Jewish **woman, which** does **not** apply **to** gentiles. By *halakha*, only marriage applies to gentiles, not betrothal. Therefore, **we judge them according to our *halakha*** in that case.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲבָל אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ, בְּדִינָא דִּידְהוּ דָּיְינִינַן לְהוּ? וְהָתַנְיָא: בָּא עַל נַעֲרָה הַמְאוֹרָסָה – נִידּוֹן בִּסְקִילָה, עַל אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ – נִידּוֹן בְּחֶנֶק. וְאִי בְּדִינָא דִּידְהוּ, סַיִיף הוּא!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** with regard to gentiles who engage in intercourse with **a married** Jewish **woman, do we judge them according to their *halakha*? But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita*: If a gentile **engages in intercourse with a betrothed young** Jewish **woman, he is punished by stoning;** if he engages in intercourse **with a married** Jewish **woman he is punished by strangulation?** The Gemara explains its question: **And if** they are judged **according to their *halakha*,** he would **be** executed by the **sword.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: מַאי אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ דְּקָתָנֵי? כְּגוֹן שֶׁנִּכְנְסָה לְחוּפָּה וְלֹא נִבְעֲלָה, דִּלְדִידְהוּ לֵית לְהוּ. דָּיְינִינַן לְהוּ בְּדִינָא דִּידַן.
> **Translation:** **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak says: What** is the meaning of the phrase: **A married woman, which** the *tanna* **teaches?** It is referring to a case **where** the woman **had entered the wedding canopy but had not** yet **engaged in intercourse** with her husband, in which case she is considered married according to the *halakhot* that apply to Jews but not according to the *halakhot* that apply to gentiles. **Since with regard to** gentiles, marriage has **not** yet taken effect, **we judge them according to our *halakhot*.** Therefore, a gentile who engages in intercourse with such a Jewish woman is executed by strangulation.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתָנֵי רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: בְּעוּלַת בַּעַל – יֵשׁ לָהֶן, נִכְנְסָה לְחוּפָּה וְלֹא נִבְעֲלָה – אֵין לָהֶן.
> **Translation:** **As Rabbi Ḥanina teaches:** Gentiles can **have** the status of a married woman **who has engaged in intercourse with her husband,** i.e., such a woman is considered married according to their laws, but **they cannot have** the status of a married woman **who has entered the wedding canopy but has not engaged in intercourse** with her husband.

The *gemara* now questions whether the death penalty truly applies to theft: *v'al gezel ben Noach neherag?* And it cites a *braisa* that discusses the scope of the *gezel* prohibition. The *braisa* lists: *ganav* (burglary), *gezel* (robbery), *v'chen yefat toar* (taking a captive woman in war — which the *braisa* treats as a species of theft: the captor forcibly removes a woman from her family and husband, appropriating her as property), and *v'chen kayotze bahen* — similar cases. What counts as a "similar case" is a question the *gemara* will address shortly.

The *braisa* then states: a *goy* who steals from a *goy*, or a *goy* who steals from a Jew — *assur*. A Jew who steals from a *goy* — *mutar*.

This is a deeply troubling passage, and it is important not to elide its difficulty. The text uses the word *Kusi* (Samaritan) throughout rather than *goy* — a form of self-censorship that appears even in editions that restore other censored passages. The ruling that a Jew may steal from a non-Jew is stated baldly. Rashi on this passage reads the *braisa* as holding that it is entirely *mutar*, with no rabbinic or Torah-level prohibition. The *gemara* in *Bava Kamma*, which discusses *gezel goy* more extensively, at minimum adds that even if it is technically permissible, it is prohibited where *chillul Hashem* would result. But bracketing that consideration, the *braisa* here seems to permit it outright. This is a *gemara* that demands acknowledgment of its difficulty rather than apologetics. The danger of teaching such material without problematizing it is that it can be internalized as a normative attitude — that stealing from or causing harm to a non-Jew is of no moral consequence. This has real-world implications, including in contemporary Israel where such *gemaras* have been cited in the context of practical political attitudes.

Returning to the technical question: the *braisa* says the non-Jewish thief is *assur*, not *chayav*. If all seven commandments carry the death penalty, why does the *braisa* use the weaker language of prohibition rather than liability? *V'ee ita nitni chayav* — it should have said *chayav*, deserving of punishment.

The *gemara*'s answer is that the word *assur* was chosen to create the proper contrast: since the *braisa* wanted to conclude with *mutar* (permissible) in the case of a Jew stealing from a *goy*, the natural contrast for the non-Jewish case is *assur* rather than *chayav*. The word *mutar* calls for its antonym *assur*, not for *chayav*. This is confirmed — and in fact necessitated — by the *braisa*'s treatment of murder, where no such contrast is needed. The *braisa* states: a *goy* who kills a *goy*, or a *goy* who kills a Jew — *chayav*. A Jew who kills a *goy* — *patur*. There, the *braisa* explicitly says *chayav*, because it cannot say *mutar* for the Jewish case (murder of a non-Jew, while apparently not punishable by a Jewish court, is not *mutar* — *lo maalin velo moridin*), and therefore the contrast is *chayav* versus *patur* rather than *assur* versus *mutar*. Put differently: by *gezel*, the *braisa* was forced into the *assur*/*mutar* pairing by the conclusion it needed to reach; by *shefichut damim*, no such endpoint constrained it, so it used the more precise *chayav* and *patur*.

The Rambam's text in *Hilchot Rotzeach u'Shmirat Nefesh* is instructive here. He opens by stating: *kol ha-horeg nefesh harei zeh over* — anyone who kills a person transgresses the prohibition of *lo tirtzach*. This formulation applies universally, without qualification by the identity of the victim. There is, however, a scholarly debate about whether this formulation is original or represents a later editorial modification; some hold that the original text read *kol ha-horeg nefesh mi'Yisrael*. The question of whether the prohibition of murder fully encompasses the killing of a non-Jew — and if so, what the legal consequences are — is thus an ongoing and unresolved tension within the *halachic* literature.

The broader point for the *sugya*: the use of *assur* in the theft *braisa* does not indicate an absence of the death penalty. It is simply a function of the rhetorical structure of the *braisa*, which required the word *mutar* at its conclusion and therefore used *assur* as its counterpart at the outset. The death penalty remains in force; the language is not being precise.

### Less Than Prutah

Rav Pappa explains what the phrase *v'chen kayotze bahen* — similar cases — teaches in the context of *gezel*: *lo nitzrecha ela l'pachos mishaveh prutah* — the additional clause is needed only to include cases of less than a *prutah*. That is, even theft of less than the minimum denomination of currency constitutes *gezel* for a *ben Noach* and carries the attendant liability. This is the *chiddush*: one might have thought that an amount too small to be treated as money at all could not constitute theft, but the *braisa* teaches otherwise.

### Jew vs. Non-Jew Threshold

This raises an immediate question: if a non-Jew who steals from a Jew is also *assur*, why would the theft of less than a *prutah* from a Jew not be included? The *gemara* answers: *ha bar mechila hu* — a Jew will be *mochel*, will forgive the debt. This is the *gemara*'s explanation for why less than a *prutah* does not trigger liability when stolen from a Jew: the Jewish victim will simply waive the amount. Non-Jews, by contrast, will not be *mochel* even a trivial sum.

This yields a sharp and somewhat ironic contrast: the stereotyped generosity of the Jew — quick to forgive a petty debt — is precisely what limits the legal liability of a non-Jew who steals from him, while the non-Jew's insistence on full recovery even of minimal amounts means that any taking, however small, constitutes a real wrong. And yet this contrast immediately generates a further puzzle: if the Jew is going to be *mochel* anyway, why should the non-Jew who steals from him transgress at all? The resolution — that *tzara b'shaita miha ita*, there was a genuine harm at the moment of taking — and the further question of why less than a *prutah* between two non-Jews requires no special derivation at all, are taken up directly in what follows.

### Restitution vs Punishment


But this answer creates a new difficulty: if the reason less than a *prutah* from a Jew is not actionable is that the Jew will be *mochel*, why should the non-Jewish thief transgress at all? After the theft, the Jew says *machel* — forgiven — so no harm was ultimately done. The *gemara* responds: *tzara b'shaita miha ita* — at the moment of the taking, there was a genuine harm. The act of appropriating something from another, however small the amount and however willing the victim is to forgive it afterward, constitutes the violation. The subsequent *mechila* does not retroactively undo the act.

At this point, the *gemara* also addresses why the theft of less than a *prutah* from a non-Jew by another non-Jew does not need the *kayotze bahen* clause: *kevan d'lav bnei mechila ninhu gezel maalya hu* — since non-Jews are not of the type to waive small amounts, less than a *prutah* is simply ordinary theft, requiring no special derivation.

This distinction points to a deeper structural difference between how *gezel* functions for Jews and for non-Jews. By a Jew, the entire framework of theft law is oriented toward restitution: *v'heshiv et ha-gezeilah*, the verse emphasizes returning what was taken. Since the focus is on restitution, the threshold is significant — below a *prutah*, there is nothing meaningful to restore, and therefore no violation is established. A wound that causes financial injury of less than a *prutah* is handled differently: since wounding is independently culpable beyond the financial damage, lashes apply even where there is nothing to restore. But theft, which is defined entirely by the property interest, has no independent act requiring punishment when the value is below the threshold. By non-Jews, there is no restitutionary framework — the liability is purely punitive. Since the line between a *prutah* and less than a *prutah* has no operative significance in a punitive system, any taking is equally an act of *gezel* regardless of amount.

### Withholding Wages

The *gemara* offers an alternative interpretation of the *kayotze bahen* clause. Rav Acha bar Brei d'Rav Ika proposes: *lo nitzrecha ela l'koves sachar sachir* — the additional clause is needed to include withholding a worker's wages. This is a qualitatively different case: it is not an active taking but a passive failure to pay what is owed. One might have thought that *gezel* requires an affirmative act of misappropriation; withholding wages is an omission. The *braisa* teaches that it is nonetheless included. The *gemara* in *Bava Metzia* similarly holds that a Jew who withholds wages transgresses *lo tigzol*, though it is regarded as a *chiddush* there too.

The Gemara answers: The term “his father” is interpreted in a way that is **similar to** the interpretation of the term **“his mother.” Just as his mother is not** forbidden due to her **marriage** to his father, but rather due to her relationship to him, **so too,** the term **“his father”** is **not** referring to a relative who is forbidden to him due to her **marriage.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אִמּוֹ״ – אֲחוֹת אִמּוֹ. וְאֵימָא אִמּוֹ מַמָּשׁ? הַיְינוּ בְּאִשְׁתּוֹ וְלֹא בְּאֵשֶׁת חֲבֵירוֹ. וְאֵימָא לְאַחַר מִיתָה? דּוּמְיָא דְּאָבִיו: מָה אָבִיו דְּלָאו מַמָּשׁ, אַף אִמּוֹ דְּלָאו מַמָּשׁ.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Eliezer says that **“his mother”** is referring to **his mother’s sister.** The Gemara asks: **But** why not **say** that it is referring to **his mother, literally?** The Gemara answers: **This** prohibition **is** derived from the term **“to his wife,” but not to the wife of another** man; and his mother is his father’s wife. The Gemara asks: **But** why not **say** that it is referring to his mother **after** his father’s **death,** when she is no longer married? The Gemara answers: The term “his mother” is interpreted in a way that is **similar to** the interpretation of the term **“his father.” Just as** the term **“his father” is not** interpreted **literally, so too,** the term **“his mother” is not** interpreted **literally.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא אוֹמֵר: ״אָבִיו״ – אֵשֶׁת אָבִיו. וְאֵימָא: אָבִיו מַמָּשׁ? הַיְינוּ ״וְדָבַק״ – וְלֹא בְּזָכָר. אִי הָכִי, אֵשֶׁת אָבִיו נָמֵי הַיְינוּ ״בְּאִשְׁתּוֹ״ וְלֹא בְּאֵשֶׁת חֲבֵרוֹ! לְאַחַר מִיתָה.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Akiva says: “His father”** is referring to **his father’s wife.** The Gemara asks: **But** why not **say** it is referring to **his father, literally?** The Gemara answers: **That** prohibition **is** derived from the term **“and shall cleave** to his wife,” **but not to a male.** The Gemara challenges: **If so,** the prohibition of **his father’s wife is also** derived from another term: **“To his wife,” but not to the wife of another** man. The Gemara explains: The term “to his wife” is referring to his father’s wife **after** his father’s **death,** when she is no longer married.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״אִמּוֹ״ – אִמּוֹ מַמָּשׁ. הַיְינוּ ״בְּאִשְׁתּוֹ״ וְלֹא בְּאֵשֶׁת חֲבֵרוֹ! אִמּוֹ מֵאֲנוּסָתוֹ.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Akiva says: **“His mother”** is referring to **his mother, literally.** The Gemara challenges: **That** prohibition **is** derived from the term **“to his wife,” but not to the wife of another** man. The Gemara explains: According to Rabbi Akiva, the term “his mother” is referring to **his mother whom** his father **raped,** i.e., she was never his father’s wife.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּמַאי קָא מִיפַּלְגִי? רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר סָבַר:
> **Translation:** **With regard to what** principle **do** Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Akiva **disagree? Rabbi Eliezer holds**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָבִיו דּוּמְיָא דְּאִמּוֹ, וְאִמּוֹ דּוּמְיָא דְּאָבִיו. לָא מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ אֶלָּא בְּאַחְוָוה.
> **Translation:** that the term **“his father”** should be interpreted in a way that is **similar to** the term **“his mother,” and “his mother”** should be interpreted in a way that is **similar to “his father.” You find** such an interpretation **only with regard to sisterhood,** i.e., “his father” is referring to his father’s sister, and “his mother” is referring to his mother’s sister.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא: מוּטָב לְאוֹקְמֵיהּ בְּאֵשֶׁת אָבִיו, דְּאִיקְּרַי ״עֶרְוַת אָבִיו״, לְאַפּוֹקֵי אֲחוֹת אָבִיו, דִּ״שְׁאֵר אָבִיו״ אִיקְּרַי, ״עֶרְוַת אָבִיו״ לָא אִיקְּרַי.
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Akiva** holds that **it is preferable to interpret** the term “his father” as referring **to his father’s wife, who is referred to as his father’s nakedness** in the verse: “You shall not uncover the nakedness of your father’s wife; it is your father’s nakedness” (Leviticus 18:8), **to the exclusion of his father’s sister, who is referred to as his father’s kin** in the verse: “You shall not uncover the nakedness of your father’s sister; she is your father’s kin” (Leviticus 18:12), and who **is not referred to as his father’s nakedness.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: ״וַיִּקַּח עַמְרָם אֶת יוֹכֶבֶד דֹּדָתוֹ״. מַאי לָאו, דּוֹדָתוֹ מִן הָאֵם?
> **Translation:** **Come** and **hear** a proof for the opinion of Rabbi Akiva from the verse: **“And Amram took Jochebed his aunt** as a wife” (Exodus 6:20). **What,** was she **not his maternal aunt?** Presumably, Jochebed was the sister of Kohath, Amram’s father, from both of Kohath’s parents, and not from his father alone. Evidently, a descendant of Noah may marry his father’s sister.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֹא, דּוֹדָתוֹ מִן הָאָב.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this proof: **No,** she was **his paternal aunt,** Kohath’s half sister. Since she was not Kohath’s sister from his mother’s side, she was not forbidden to Amram.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: ״וְגַם אׇמְנָה אֲחֹתִי בַת אָבִי הִיא אַךְ לֹא בַת אִמִּי״ – מִכְּלָל דְּבַת הָאֵם אֲסוּרָה.
> **Translation:** **Come** and **hear** a proof for the opinion of Rabbi Eliezer from what Abraham said to Abimelech with regard to Sarah: **“And moreover, she is my sister, the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my mother;** and so she became my wife” (Genesis 20:12). **By inference, the daughter of the mother** of a descendant of Noah **is forbidden** to him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתִסְבְּרָא אֲחוֹתוֹ הֲוַאי? בַּת אָחִיו הֲוַאי! וְכֵיוָן דְּהָכִי הוּא, לָא שְׁנָא מִן הָאָב וְלָא שְׁנָא מִן הָאֵם – שַׁרְיָא. אֶלָּא הָתָם הָכִי קָאָמַר לֵיהּ: קוּרְבָּא דְּאָחוֹת אִית לִי בַּהֲדַהּ, מֵאַבָּא וְלָא מֵאִמָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this proof: **But** how can **you understand** that Sarah **was** Abraham’s **sister? She was his brother’s daughter.** By tradition, it is known that Sarah was Haran’s daughter Iscah. **And since that was so,** there **is no difference** whether they were **paternal** relatives, **and** there **is no difference** whether they were **maternal** relatives; in any event she was **permitted** to him, even according to the *halakha* of Jews. **Rather, this** is what Abraham **was saying to** Abimelech **there: She is related to me like a sister,** as the daughter of my brother is like a sister, and our relationship is **from** the side of my **father but not from** the side of my **mother.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: מִפְּנֵי מָה לֹא נָשָׂא אָדָם אֶת בִּתּוֹ? כְּדֵי שֶׁיִּשָּׂא קַיִן אֶת אֲחוֹתוֹ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״כִּי אָמַרְתִּי עוֹלָם חֶסֶד יִבָּנֶה״. הָא לָאו הָכִי – אֲסִירָא.
> **Translation:** **Come** and **hear** a proof from a *baraita*: **For what** reason **did Adam not marry his daughter? So that Cain would marry his sister** and they would procreate immediately, **as it is stated: “For I have said: The world shall be built on kindness [*ḥesed*]”** (Psalms 89:3). This verse alludes to the fact that at the beginning of the world’s existence it was permitted for men to marry their sisters, which was later forbidden in the verse: “And if a man shall take his sister…it is a shameful thing [*ḥesed*]” (Leviticus 20:17). The Gemara infers: If it had **not** been **so,** if God had not specially permitted Cain to marry his sister, she would have been **forbidden** to him. This is difficult according to the opinion of Rabbi Akiva, who deems it permitted for a gentile to marry his sister.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֵּיוָן דְּאִשְׁתְּרַי, אִשְׁתְּרַי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this proof: **Once it was permitted** for Cain to marry his sister, **it was permitted** for all descendants of Noah to do so, and it was forbidden only to Jews.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב הוּנָא: גּוֹי מוּתָּר בְּבִתּוֹ. וְאֵם תֹּאמַר: מִפְּנֵי מָה לֹא נָשָׂא אָדָם אֶת בִּתּוֹ? כְּדֵי שֶׁיִּשָּׂא קַיִן אֶת אֲחוֹתוֹ, מִשּׁוּם ״עוֹלָם חֶסֶד יִבָּנֶה״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Huna says: A gentile is permitted to** marry **his daughter. And if you say, for what** reason **did Adam not marry his daughter?** It was **so that Cain would marry his sister, because** it is stated: **“The world shall be built on kindness.”**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיכָּא דְּאָמְרִי: אָמַר רַב הוּנָא, גּוֹי אָסוּר בְּבִתּוֹ. תִּדַּע, שֶׁלֹּא נָשָׂא אָדָם אֶת בִּתּוֹ. וְלָא הִיא, הָתָם הַיְינוּ טַעְמָא: כְּדֵי שֶׁיִּשָּׂא קַיִן אֶת אֲחוֹתוֹ, מִשּׁוּם דְּ״עוֹלָם חֶסֶד יִבָּנֶה״.
> **Translation:** **And there are** those **who say** that Rav Huna did not say this; rather, **Rav Huna says: A gentile is prohibited from** marrying **his daughter. Know** that this is the *halakha*, **as Adam did not marry his daughter.** The Gemara rejects this statement: **But** that **is not so, as there, this is the reason** Adam did not marry his daughter: **So that Cain would marry his sister, because** it is stated: **“The world shall be built on kindness.”**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: עֶבֶד מוּתָּר בְּאִמּוֹ, וּמוּתָּר בְּבִתּוֹ. יָצָא מִכְּלַל גּוֹי, וְלִכְלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא בָּא.
> **Translation:** § **Rav Ḥisda says:** A Canaanite **slave** is **permitted to** marry **his mother, and** he is **permitted to** marry **his daughter.** This is because he has **left the category of a gentile** by immersing in a ritual bath for the purpose of becoming a slave to a Jew, and consequently all his previous family relationships are disregarded according to *halakha*; **but he has not entered the category of a Jew,** as evidenced by the fact that he is not obligated to observe all of the mitzvot of male Jews. Therefore, the decree of the Sages prohibiting the maternal relatives of converts does not apply to him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי אֲתָא רַב דִּימִי, אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר אָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: בֶּן נֹחַ שֶׁיִּיחֵד שִׁפְחָה לְעַבְדּוֹ, וּבָא עָלֶיהָ – נֶהֱרָג עָלֶיהָ.
> **Translation:** **When Rav Dimi came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia, he said that **Rabbi Elazar says** that **Rabbi Ḥanina says:** In the case of a **descendant of Noah who designated a maidservant** as a mate **for his slave, and** then he himself **engaged in intercourse with her, he is executed** for adultery **on her account.**

The *braisa* included *yifat toar* — taking a captive woman in war — among the cases similar to *gezel*. This is an interesting categorization: *yifat toar* can also be understood as an aspect of *giluy arayos*, particularly if she is a married woman and the captor has relations with her. The *braisa*, however, frames it as theft: the captor is forcibly removing a woman from her family, from her husband, from her community — appropriating her as property. The *gemara* extends the *kayotze bahen* of *yifat toar* as follows: *ben Noach she-yiched shifcha l'avdo uva aleha — neherag aleha*. If a *ben Noach* designated a female slave to his servant and then himself had relations with her, he is executed. This is not a case of *arayos* in the technical sense, since there is no marriage bond for slaves. But by taking a woman designated to another — appropriating her as his own — he has committed an act analogous to theft, a species of *kayotze bahen* within the *gezel* category.

### Murder Cases

*Keyotze bo d'shfichus damim lo tanya* — there are no *kayotze bahen* cases taught in connection with murder. Murder is categorical: it either is or is not the taking of human life. There are no peripheral or analogous cases that carry liability without being fully constitutive of the prohibition. The *gemara* notes, via *Abaye*, that if one were ever to find a *baraisa* that does list *kayotze bahen* cases for murder, it would be attributable to Rav Yonatan ben Shaul.

### Self-Defense Rodef

The relevant *baraisa* of Rav Yonatan ben Shaul teaches: *rodef achar chaveiro lehorgo yachol lehatzilo b'echad me'eivarav v'lo hitzilo* — if someone was pursuing another with lethal intent, and it was possible to stop the pursuer by wounding him in a limb, yet the one being pursued killed him outright, then the defender *neherag alav* — is executed. This is not a Jew versus non-Jew distinction; it applies equally to Jews and non-Jews. The case presupposes an objective assessment that the threat could have been neutralized with less than lethal force, and that the defender nonetheless chose to kill. Under those circumstances, the killing constitutes a form of unjustified homicide, even in a self-defense context.

This is a legally and practically difficult standard. The assessment of whether a reasonable alternative existed — whether one *yachol lehatzilo b'echad me'eivarav* — is inherently uncertain in the moment of threat. The IDF rules of engagement grapple with precisely this question: at what point is a threat sufficiently neutralized that continued lethal force becomes unjustified? And when, in retrospect, is it appropriate to conclude that a defender had a feasible non-lethal alternative? The *halacha* does not resolve these evidentiary difficulties, but it establishes the principle: if the objective assessment is that the threat could have been stopped without killing, then the killing is not justified, and this constitutes the *kayotze bahen* case of *shfichus damim*.

Not everyone agrees, of course. Alternative positions hold that even if one kills when a non-lethal alternative existed, it does not constitute murder in a culpable sense — it is still self-defense. But according to Rav Yonatan ben Shaul, it is precisely these borderline cases that constitute the *kayotze bahen* of *shfichus damim*.

### Non-Jew Permissions

A final and philosophically rich question emerges from the discussion of the *rodef* case: it is not self-evident that the permission to kill a *rodef* — which the *gemara* explicitly presupposes for a *ben Noach* as well — should apply to non-Jews at all. A *ben Noach* is prohibited in murder. How do we know that the exception of self-defense carries over to the non-Jewish framework?

The present *gemara* makes it clear that it does. But the question extends further: how do the various permissions and overrides that operate within Jewish law map onto *sheva mitzvos*? For example, a Jew is obligated to accept martyrdom rather than transgress *avodah zarah*, *giluy arayos*, or *shfichus damim* under duress. Does a *ben Noach* have any obligation of martyrdom? If someone holds a gun to a non-Jew's head and demands that he commit idolatry or be killed, must he refuse and die? It is possible that a *ben Noach* has no obligation of martyrdom whatsoever — that the *yehareg v'al ya'avor* framework is entirely internal to *halacha* as it applies to Jews, and that a *ben Noach* under duress simply has no such requirement. Similarly, how do concepts like *shogeg* (inadvertent violation) interact with the Noachide framework, given the harshness of *azharah shelahen zu hi mitatan*? These questions — how the internal parameters, limits, and overrides of the commandments operate within the *sheva mitzvos* context — constitute a rich and not fully mapped area of *halachic* inquiry.
