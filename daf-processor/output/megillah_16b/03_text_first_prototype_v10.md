## Yosef's Weeping



### Plural Necks

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיִּפּוֹל עַל צַוְּארֵי בִנְיָמִן אָחִיו״. כַּמָּה צַוָּארִין הֲווֹ לֵיהּ לְבִנְיָמִין? אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: בָּכָה עַל שְׁנֵי מִקְדָּשִׁים שֶׁעֲתִידִין לִהְיוֹת בְּחֶלְקוֹ שֶׁל בִּנְיָמִין וַעֲתִידִין לֵיחָרֵב. ״וּבִנְיָמִין בָּכָה עַל צַוָּארָיו״ — בָּכָה עַל מִשְׁכַּן שִׁילֹה, שֶׁעָתִיד לִהְיוֹת בְּחֶלְקוֹ שֶׁל יוֹסֵף וְעָתִיד לֵיחָרֵב.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates on certain elements in the story of Joseph and his brothers. The verse states with regard to Joseph: **“And he fell on his brother Benjamin’s neck [*tzavarei***] and wept” (Genesis 45:14). The wording of the verse gives rise to a question, as the word *tzavarei* is plural, meaning necks: **How many necks did Benjamin have,** such that the verse should use the plural *tzavarei* rather than the singular *tzavar*? **Rabbi Elazar said:** This intimates **that** Joseph **cried over the two Temples that were destined to be in the** tribal **territory of Benjamin and were destined to be destroyed.** The same verse continues: **“And Benjamin wept on his neck”** (Genesis 45:14); **he cried over the tabernacle of Shiloh that was destined to be in the** tribal **territory of Joseph and was destined to be destroyed.**

The *daf* opens with a verse from the story of Yosef's reunion with his brothers: *vayipol al tzavarei Binyamin achiv* — Yosef fell on Binyamin's neck and wept, *u'Binyamin bacha al tzavarav*. The Gemara asks: *kama tzavarin havu leh* — how many necks does he have? The word *tzavarei* is plural. As Rashi notes, this is simply how the language refers to the neck; nonetheless, the Gemara uses it as an occasion for *drasha*.

### Two Temples

Rabbi Elazar teaches: *bacha al shnei mikdashim she'atidin lihyot b'chelko shel Binyamin v'atidin l'yecharev* — Yosef wept over the two *mikdashim* that would stand in Binyamin's portion of the Land of Israel and would eventually be destroyed. This is striking in light of the broader context: the *Megillah* itself is situated, according to *Chazal*, between these two *mikdashim* — so that the symbolism of Mordechai's *levush malchus* and Yosef's gifts intersects with the destruction of the *batei mikdash* in Binyamin's territory.

### Mishkan Shiloh

*U'Binyamin bacha al tzavarav* — and Binyamin wept on Yosef's necks. Here the Gemara passes over the plural without comment, and instead explains that Binyamin wept *al mishkan Shiloh she'atid lihyot b'chelko shel Yosef v'atid l'yecharev* — over the destruction of the *mishkan* at Shiloh, which would stand in Yosef's portion and likewise be destroyed. Thus the mutual weeping was not merely an expression of joy at reunion, but carried the weight of future destruction.

### Yosef's Sincerity

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְהִנֵּה עֵינֵיכֶם רוֹאוֹת וְעֵינֵי אָחִי בִנְיָמִין״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר, אָמַר לָהֶם: כְּשֵׁם שֶׁאֵין בְּלִבִּי עַל בִּנְיָמִין אָחִי, שֶׁלֹּא הָיָה בִּמְכִירָתִי, כָּךְ אֵין בְּלִבִּי עֲלֵיכֶם. ״כִּי פִי הַמְדַבֵּר אֲלֵיכֶם״. כְּפִי — כֵּן לִבִּי.
> **Translation:** The verse states: **“And behold, your eyes see, and the eyes of my brother Benjamin”** (Genesis 45:12). **Rabbi Elazar said:** Joseph **said to** his brothers as follows: **Just as I** certainly **harbor no** resentment **in my heart toward my brother Benjamin, for he was not** even **present when I was sold, so too, I harbor no** resentment **toward you.** The verse continues: **“That it is my mouth [*ki fi*] that speaks to you”** (Genesis 45:12), i.e., **As my mouth [*kefi*] is, so is my heart.**

The verse continues: *v'hinei eyneichem ra'ot v'eynei achi Binyamin ki pi hamedaber aleichem* — behold, your eyes see and the eyes of my brother Binyamin that it is my mouth speaking to you. Rabbi Elazar explains: why single out *eynei achi Binyamin* separately? Yosef is making a point. Just as he clearly harbors nothing against Binyamin — who had no part in the plot to sell him — so too, *kach ein li b'libi aleichem*, he holds no grudge against the brothers either. *Ki pi hamedaber aleichem — k'fi ken libi*: what his mouth says is precisely what is in his heart. He is being entirely sincere.

### Gifts to Father

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וּלְאָבִיו שָׁלַח כְּזֹאת עֲשָׂרָה חֲמוֹרִים נוֹשְׂאִים מִטּוּב מִצְרָיִם״. מַאי ״מִטּוּב מִצְרָיִם״? אָמַר רַבִּי בִּנְיָמִין בַּר יֶפֶת אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: שָׁלַח לוֹ יַיִן [יָשָׁן], שֶׁדַּעַת זְקֵנִים נוֹחָה הֵימֶנּוּ.
> **Translation:** The verse states: **“And to his father he sent after this manner ten donkeys laden with the good things of Egypt”** (Genesis 45:23). The Gemara asks: **What are “the good things of Egypt”** that are mentioned but not specified here? **Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet said** that **Rabbi Elazar said: He sent him aged wine, which the elderly find pleasing.**

The verse states that Yosef sent his father ten donkeys laden with *mi'tuv Mitzrayim* — the good of Egypt. The Gemara asks: what is the good of Egypt? Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet said in the name of Rabbi Elazar: *shalach lo yayin yashan she'da'at zekenim nocha heymenu* — he sent him old wine, which elderly people truly appreciate and which puts them in a settled, contented state of mind.

## Prostration & Power



### Brothers Prostrate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיֵּלְכוּ גַּם אֶחָיו וַיִּפְּלוּ לְפָנָיו״. אָמַר רַבִּי בִּנְיָמִין בַּר יֶפֶת אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר, הַיְינוּ דְּאָמְרִי אִינָשֵׁי: תַּעֲלָא בְּעִידָּנֵיהּ סְגֵיד לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** Following Jacob’s death, it states concerning Joseph: **“And his brothers even went and fell down before him”** (Genesis 50:18). **Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet said** that **Rabbi Elazar said: This** explains the folk saying **that people say: When the fox is in its hour, bow down to it,** i.e., if a fox is appointed king, one must bow down before and submit oneself to it.

After Yaakov dies, the brothers fear that Yosef will now take his revenge, and they prostrate themselves before him. Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet said in the name of Rabbi Elazar — and one notes that many of the teachings in this passage are transmitted through this same chain — *hainu d'amri inshi, ta'ala b'idneih sagid leh*: this is what people say: a fox in his time, you must bow down to him. They prostrated themselves before Yosef because Yosef held the power.

### Fox Metaphor

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תַּעֲלָא? מַאי בְּצִירוּתֵיהּ מֵאֲחווֹהַּ? אֶלָּא, אִי אִיתְּמַר הָכִי אִיתְּמַר: ״וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל עַל רֹאשׁ הַמִּטָּה״, אָמַר רַבִּי בִּנְיָמִין בַּר יֶפֶת אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: תַּעֲלָא בְּעִידָּנֵיהּ סְגֵיד לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara expresses astonishment at the use of this parable: Are you calling Joseph **a fox? What, was he inferior to his brothers** such that in relation to them you call him a fox? **Rather, if such a statement was stated, it was stated as follows,** not in connection with this verse, but rather in connection with a different verse. The verse states: **“And Israel bowed himself upon the head of the bed”** (Genesis 47:31). With regard to this, **Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet said** that **Rabbi Elazar said: When the fox is in its hour, bow down to it,** as Jacob had to bow down before his son Joseph, who had reached greatness.

The Gemara immediately questions this application: *mai betzirosieh me'achvoh* — in what way was Yosef inferior to his brothers, like a fox? He was their equal by birth; the fox metaphor implies someone at a lower station who happens to have power. Therefore, the Gemara says, *ela ei ismar hachi ismar* — if this saying was transmitted, it was transmitted in a different context. The better context is *vayishtachu Yisrael al rosh hamitah* — when Yaakov bowed down on his bed as Yosef came to receive his blessing and bring Ephraim and Menashe. There Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet in the name of Rabbi Elazar applies the saying: *ta'ala b'idneih sagid leh* — even a fox has his day, and since Yosef held temporal power, even Yaakov, his father, acknowledged that.

### Yaakov Bows

The phrase *vayishtachu Yisrael al rosh hamitah* thus receives its proper context. Yaakov bowing before Yosef was not mere deference to a son; it reflected the recognition that Yosef's position of authority warranted acknowledgment — even from his father. The proverb is not demeaning to Yosef but descriptive of the reality of worldly power.

### Candles Metaphor

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיְנַחֵם אוֹתָם וַיְדַבֵּר עַל לִבָּם״. אָמַר רַבִּי בִּנְיָמִין בַּר יֶפֶת אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאָמַר לָהֶם דְּבָרִים שֶׁמִּתְקַבְּלִין עַל הַלֵּב: וּמָה עֲשָׂרָה נֵרוֹת לֹא יָכְלוּ לְכַבּוֹת נֵר אֶחָד, נֵר אֶחָד הֵיאַךְ יָכוֹל לְכַבּוֹת עֲשָׂרָה נֵרוֹת?
> **Translation:** It says with regard to Joseph’s remarks to his brothers: **“And he comforted them and spoke to their hearts”** (Genesis 50:21). **Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet said** that **Rabbi Elazar said:** This **teaches that he spoke to them words that are acceptable to the heart,** and alleviated their fears. This is what he said: **If ten lights could not put out one light,** as all of you were unable to do me harm, **how can one light put out ten lights?**

Returning to the brothers who have prostrated themselves, the verse says *vaynachim otam vayidaber al libam* — he consoled them and spoke to their hearts. Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet in the name of Rabbi Elazar explains: *vayidaber al libam* means he spoke *devarim she'miskablim al halev*, words that could actually be received by the heart. What did he say? *Umah asarah nerot lo yakhlu lekhabot ner echad, ner echad heikh yakhol lekhabot asarah nerot* — if ten candles could not extinguish one candle, how could one candle extinguish ten? By framing his reassurance in terms of *nerot*, Yosef elevated the conversation to the spiritual plane. Not only would he not harm them — he could not, even were he to try. The metaphor suggests that on the level of the soul, such a thing is impossible.

## Four Themes



### Four Purim Themes

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״לַיְּהוּדִים הָיְתָה אוֹרָה וְשִׂמְחָה וְשָׂשׂוֹן וִיקָר״. אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: ״אוֹרָה״ — זוֹ תּוֹרָה, וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר: ״כִּי נֵר מִצְוָה וְתוֹרָה אוֹר״. ״שִׂמְחָה״ — זֶה יוֹם טוֹב, וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר: ״וְשָׂמַחְתָּ בְּחַגֶּךָ״. ״שָׂשׂוֹן״ — זוֹ מִילָה, וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר: ״שָׂשׂ אָנֹכִי עַל אִמְרָתֶךָ״.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara returns to its explanation of the Megilla. The verse states: **“The Jews had light and gladness, and joy and honor”** (Esther 8:16). **Rav Yehuda said: “Light”; this** is referring to the **Torah** that they once again studied. **And similarly it says: “For the mitzva is a lamp and the Torah is light”** (Proverbs 6:23). **“Gladness” [*simḥa*]; this** is referring to **the Festivals** that they once again observed. **And similarly it says: “And you shall be glad [*vesamakhta*] on your Festival”** (Deuteronomy 16:14). **“Joy” [*sasson*]; this** is referring to **circumcision,** as they once again circumcised their sons. **And similarly it says: “I rejoice [*sas*] at Your word”** (Psalms 119:162), which the Sages understood as referring to David’s rejoicing over the mitzva of circumcision.

The *Megillah* states: *layehudim hayetah orah vesimkhah vesason vikar* — to the Jews there was light, joy, rejoicing, and honor. The Gemara interprets each of these four terms as referring to a specific religious institution.

### Light = Torah

Rav Yehudah teaches: *orah zo Torah* — the light is *Torah*. The proof text is *ki ner mitzvah ve-Torah or* — a *mitzvah* is a candle and *Torah* is light. This verse, with its reference to the *ner*, connects back as well to the earlier *nerot* imagery.

### Joy, Rejoicing, Honor

*Simkhah zeh Yom Tov* — joy refers to *Yom Tov*, as the verse says *vesamakhta be-khagekha*. *Sason zo milah* — rejoicing refers to *milah*, derived from the verse *sas anokhi al imratekha kemotze shalal rav*, which is recited at a *bris milah*. Rashi adds that when God commands *bris milah* to Avraham, the Torah uses the word *vayomer* — *amiraha* — which connects to *imratekha*. *Vikar elu tefillin* — honor refers to *tefillin*, from the verse *verau kol amei ha-aretz ki shem Hashem nikra alekha ve-yar'u mimmeka* — all the peoples of the earth will see and be in awe of you.

### Haman's Decrees

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וִיקָר״ — אֵלּוּ תְּפִלִּין, וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר: ״וְרָאוּ כׇּל עַמֵּי הָאָרֶץ כִּי שֵׁם ה׳ נִקְרָא עָלֶיךָ וְיָרְאוּ מִמֶּךָּ״, וְתַנְיָא, רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר הַגָּדוֹל אוֹמֵר: אֵלּוּ תְּפִלִּין שֶׁבָּרֹאשׁ.
> **Translation:** **“Honor”; this is** referring to **phylacteries,** which they once again donned. **And similarly it says: “And all peoples of the earth will see that you are called by the name of the Lord; and they will be afraid of you”** (Deuteronomy 28:10). **And it was taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi Eliezer the Great said: This** is referring to **the phylacteries worn on the head.** Haman had banned the fulfillment of all the mitzvot mentioned, but upon Haman’s demise the Jews returned to their observance.

What does all of this mean in context? Why would the *Megillah*'s celebration be expressed specifically through *Torah*, *Yom Tov*, *milah*, and *tefillin*? Rashi explains that Haman did not merely decree to destroy the Jews physically; he also, as in the period of the *Chanukah* story, decreed against Jewish religious practice. Thus: *orah zo Torah shegazar aleihem Haman shelo yaasku ba-Torah* — Haman decreed they should not engage in *Torah* study. Similarly he forbade observance of *Yamim Tovim*, the practice of *milah*, and the wearing of *tefillin*. The Gemara is thus, to some degree, overlapping the themes of *Chanukah* and *Purim* — both involved decrees not only against Jewish life but against Jewish religious identity.

## Ten Sons Names



### One Breath

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְאֵת פַּרְשַׁנְדָּתָא וְגוֹ׳ עֲשֶׂרֶת בְּנֵי הָמָן״. אָמַר רַב אַדָּא דְּמִן יָפוֹ: ״עֲשֶׂרֶת בְּנֵי הָמָן״ וַ״עֲשֶׂרֶת״ צָרִיךְ לְמֵמְרִינְהוּ בִּנְשִׁימָה אַחַת, מַאי טַעְמָא — כּוּלְּהוּ בַּהֲדֵי הֲדָדֵי נְפַקוּ נִשְׁמְתַיְיהוּ. אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: וָיו דְּ״וַיְזָתָא״ צָרִיךְ לְמִימְתְּחַהּ בִּזְקִיפָא כְּמוּרְדְּיָא דְלִבְרוּת. מַאי טַעְמָא — כּוּלְּהוּ בְּחַד זְקִיפָא אִזְדְּקִיפוּ.
> **Translation:** The verse states: “And in Shushan the capital the Jews slew and destroyed five hundred men. **And Parshandatha…**and Vaizatha, **the ten sons of Haman”** (Esther 9:6–10). **Rav Adda from Jaffa said:** When reading the Megilla, the names of **the ten sons of Haman and** the word **“ten” must be said in one breath. What is the reason** for this? It is that **their souls all departed together. Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** The letter ***vav* in** the name **“Vaizatha”** is a lengthened *vav* and **must be elongated as a pole, like a steering oar of a ship [*liberot*]. What is the reason** for this? To indicate that **they were all hanged on one pole.**

The Gemara turns to the public reading of the names of the ten sons of Haman: *ve'et Parshandasa* — *aseret benei Haman*. Rav Adda of Yafo teaches: *aseret benei Haman ve-aseret, tzarikh lemimreinhu beneshimah akhat* — the ten sons of Haman and the word *aseret* must all be said in a single breath. *Mai taama* — what is the reason? *Kulu behadi hadadi nafka nishmatayhu* — because their souls all departed simultaneously. They were all hanged at the same time, and saying the names in one breath symbolizes that simultaneity. There is something significant, apparently, in the fact that they all died in a single instant.

### Tosafot Opinion

Tosafot notes that saying the names in one breath is *le-khatchilah*; *aval be-diavad yatza* — if one did not manage it, the obligation is nonetheless fulfilled after the fact. This raises a practical question: in many communities the *ba'al korei* pauses and the congregation recites the ten names themselves. This practice may reflect the principle of *shomea k'oneh* — hearing is like saying — but holding one's breath is not the same as the listener holding their breath. Therefore, many hold that one should recite the names oneself in a single breath, or at minimum hold one's breath while the *chazzan* recites them.

### Stretched Vav

Rabbi Yochanan teaches: *vav shel Vaizasa tzarikh lemimtechah bezikifa kemordya dilvros* — the *vav* of *Vaizasa* must be stretched out, written elongated like the pole used on the river called Livros. *Mai taama* — *kulu bechad zikifa izdakaifu* — they were all hanged on the same pole. Therefore the *vav* is written to visually symbolize the gallows. This is a striking tradition, for it implies a change in the actual manner of writing the letter based on symbolic considerations — something unusual, since ordinarily the way letters are written derives from an independent *masorah*, not from interpretive associations.

### Brick Pattern

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא בַּר פָּפָּא, דָּרֵשׁ רַבִּי שֵׁילָא אִישׁ כְּפַר תְּמַרְתָּא: כׇּל הַשִּׁירוֹת כּוּלָּן נִכְתָּבוֹת אָרִיחַ עַל גַּבֵּי לְבֵינָה וּלְבֵינָה עַל גַּבֵּי אָרִיחַ —
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Ḥanina bar Pappa said** that **Rabbi Sheila, a man of the village of Timarta, interpreted** a verse **homiletically: All** of **the songs** in the Bible **are written** in the form of **a half brick arranged upon a whole brick and a whole brick arranged upon a half brick,** i.e., each line of the song is divided into a stitch of text, referred to as a half brick, which is separated by a blank space, referred to as a whole brick, from the concluding stitch of that line of text.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חוּץ מִשִּׁירָה זוֹ וּמַלְכֵי כְנַעַן, שֶׁאָרִיחַ עַל גַּבֵּי אָרִיחַ וּלְבֵינָה עַל גַּבֵּי לְבֵינָה. מַאי טַעְמָא — שֶׁלֹּא תְּהֵא תְּקוּמָה לְמַפַּלְתָּן.
> **Translation:** The next line of the song inverts the sequence. **This is the principle for all songs in the Bible except** for **this song,** referring to the list of Haman’s sons, **and** the song listing **the kings of Canaan** who were defeated by Joshua. These two songs are written in the form of **a half brick arranged upon a half brick and a whole brick arranged upon a whole brick,** i.e., one stitch of text over another, and one blank space over another. **What is the reason** that these two songs are written in this anomalous fashion? **So that they should never rise from their downfall.** Just as a wall that is built in this manner will not stand, so too, these individuals should have no resurgence.

Rabbi Chanina bar Pappa taught in the name of a scholar from the village of Timra: *kol hashirot kulan nichtavot ariach al gabei levina, ulevina al gabei ariach* — all the poems and songs in *Tanach* are written in a staggered pattern, like bricks: a half-brick over a full brick, and a full brick over a half-brick. This is the familiar pattern of *shirat hayam* (Az Yashir) and other *shirot*, where the text alternates in a staggered arrangement.

### Staggered Exception

The exceptions are *aseret benei Haman* and the listing of the kings of Kena'an — *she'ariach al gabei ariach ulevina al gabei levina* — where the text is arranged in straight columns, column directly over column, without staggering. One might also note that *Ha'azinu* presents a similar straight-column format with a central space, though the Gemara does not incorporate it into this particular point.

### No Rising

*Mai ta'ama* — what is the reason for this departure from the standard pattern of *shirot*? *Shelo tehe tekuma lemapaltan* — so that there should be no rising up from their fall. The instability of a staggered pattern is precisely why bricks are normally laid that way — it creates structural strength. Here, by placing column directly over column, the text visually enacts complete, irreversible collapse. There should be nothing left standing; the arrangement signals total destruction with no possibility of recovery.

A further scribal tradition, not discussed in the Gemara itself but relevant to the practice, holds that the names of the ten sons must fill an entire column. Most *soferim* accomplish this by writing the letters of *aseret benei Haman* in a larger size. The Vilna Gaon, however, strongly objected to this practice, arguing that we have no *masorah* to write these names in a font larger than the surrounding text — the only attested scribal anomaly is the elongated *vav* of *Vaizasa*. A *megillah* written with eleven lines per column allows the ten names plus the word *va-aseret* to fill a column in standard-size script, thus satisfying the columnar requirement without departing from the accepted *masorah* of letter size.

## Achashverosh's Shift



### Tone Shift

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וַיֹּאמֶר הַמֶּלֶךְ לְאֶסְתֵּר הַמַּלְכָּה בְּשׁוּשַׁן הַבִּירָה הָרְגוּ הַיְּהוּדִים״. אָמַר רַבִּי אֲבָהוּ: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא מַלְאָךְ וּסְטָרוֹ עַל פִּיו.
> **Translation:** The verse states: **“And the king said to Esther the queen: The Jews have slain** and destroyed five hundred men **in Shushan the capital,** and also the ten sons of Haman; what have they done in the rest of the king’s provinces? Now what is your petition and it shall be granted to you; and what more do you request, and it shall be done” (Esther 9:12). **Rabbi Abbahu said: This teaches that an angel came and slapped him on his mouth,** so that he was unable to finish what he was saying; he started with a complaint about what the Jews were doing, but ended on an entirely different note.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וּבְבֹאָהּ לִפְנֵי הַמֶּלֶךְ אָמַר עִם הַסֵּפֶר״. ״אָמַר״ — ״אָמְרָה״ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ! אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן, אָמְרָה לוֹ: יֵאָמֵר בַּפֶּה מַה שֶּׁכָּתוּב בַּסֵּפֶר.
> **Translation:** The verse states: **“But when she came before the king, he said with a letter”** (Esther 9:25). Why does it say: **“He said”? It should have said: “She said,”** as it was Esther who changed the decree. **Rabbi Yoḥanan said: She said to** Ahasuerus: **Let it be said by** word of **mouth,** indicating that **that which is written in the letter** should also be ordered verbally.

The verse reads: *vayomer hamelech l'Esther ha-malka b'Shushan ha-bira hargu hayehudim ve'ibed chamesh meot ish...u-mah she'elatekh vetinatein lakh* — the king informed Esther of the number slain in Shushan and throughout the provinces, and then in the very same verse offered her whatever she wished. The shift in tone is dramatic and jarring: the verse begins in what appears to be an expression of alarm or even accusation, and ends with a generous open-ended offer. This is a textually strange *pasuk*.

### Angel's Slap

Rabbi Abbahu teaches: *melamed sheba malach v'staho al piv* — an angel came and struck Achashverosh on his mouth, changing the words he was about to say. Just as an angel had guided Esther's approach at a critical moment, so here an angel intervened mid-sentence to alter Achashverosh's speech, producing the abrupt shift in tone visible in the verse itself.

### Read From Scroll

The verse later states: *uvevo'ah lifnei hamelech amar im hasefer yashuv machshavto hara'ah asher chashav al hayehudim al rosho.* Rabbi Yochanan interprets *amar im hasefer* as: *yomar bepeh mah shekatuv basefer* — what is written in the scroll should be declared aloud. Rashi's reading (*haichi garsinan*) is that this refers to Mordechai's role: the text is tracing how the events of Purim were to be recited and recorded annually, with the instruction that what is written in the *sefer* — the *Megillah* — should be proclaimed orally. In this reading, *amar im hasefer* becomes a foundational reference to the annual reading of the *Megillah* itself.

## Scroll Writing



### Scribal Lines

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״דִּבְרֵי שָׁלוֹם וֶאֱמֶת״. אָמַר רַבִּי תַּנְחוּם, וְאָמְרִי לַהּ אָמַר רַבִּי אַסִּי: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁצְּרִיכָה שִׂרְטוּט כַּאֲמִיתָּה שֶׁל תּוֹרָה.
> **Translation:** With regard to what is stated: **“Words of peace and truth”** (Esther 9:30), **Rabbi Tanḥum said, and some say** that **Rabbi Asi** said: This **teaches that** a Megilla scroll **requires scoring,** i.e., that the lines for the text must be scored onto the parchment, **as the Torah itself,** i.e., as is done in a Torah scroll.

The verse *divrei shalom ve'emet* prompts a *halachic* teaching. Rabbi Tanchum — and some say Rabbi Assi — teaches: *melamed shetzricha sirtut ka'amittah shel Torah* — the *Megillah* requires *sirtut*, the scored lines that a scribe makes on the parchment before writing, just as is required by *amittah shel Torah*, the truth of the *Torah*.

### Sirtut Debate

Rashi understands *amittah shel Torah* to mean a *Sefer Torah* itself, meaning the *Megillah* must have *sirtut* just as a *Sefer Torah* does. Tosafot disagrees, holding that technically a *Sefer Torah* does not require *sirtut*, and that *amittah shel Torah* here refers to a *mezuzah*. Either way, the teaching situates the *Megillah* alongside the most formally demanding categories of *sifrei kodesh*. This connects to a broader theme: just as the Gemara will shortly establish that the *Megillah* must be written in Hebrew — despite the *mishnah*'s more permissive stance toward other *sefarim* — so too here the *Megillah* is given weight comparable to, or even exceeding, other portions of the *Torah*. Tosafot reads the same stringency regarding *sirtut* in the same direction: the *Megillah* shares this demanding standard with the *mezuzah* rather than with the more lenient category of *Sefer Torah*.

## Acceptance of Purim



### Esther's Ordinance

The verse reads: *um'amar Esther kiyam divrei ha-Purim ha-eleh*. The Gemara immediately questions: *um'amar Esther in divrei ha-tzomot lo?* — and Esther's word established the Purim days, but what of the reference to *divrei ha-tzomot ve-za'akatam* earlier in the same passage?

### Fasts & Cries

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וּמַאֲמַר אֶסְתֵּר קִיַּים״. ״מַאֲמַר אֶסְתֵּר״ — אִין, ״דִּבְרֵי הַצּוֹמוֹת״ — לָא?! אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: ״דִּבְרֵי הַצּוֹמוֹת ... וּמַאֲמַר אֶסְתֵּר קִיַּים (אֵת יְמֵי) הַפּוּרִים הָאֵלֶּה״.
> **Translation:** The verses say: “The matters of the fasts and their cry. **And the decree of Esther confirmed** these matters of Purim” (Esther 9:31–32). The Gemara asks: Should we say that **“the decree of Esther” indeed** confirmed these matters of Purim, but **“the matters of the fasts”** did **not?** But didn’t the fasts also contribute to the miracle? **Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** These two verses, **“The matters of the fasts** and their cry. **And the decree of Esther confirmed these matters of Purim,”** should be read as one.

The full verse runs: *lekayem et divrei ha-Purim ha-eleh bizmaneihem ka'asher kiyam aleihem Mordechai ha-Yehudi ve-Esther ha-malka ve-ka'asher kiyemu al nafsham ve-al zar'am divrei ha-tzomot u-za'akatam* — to establish these Purim days in their times, just as Mordechai and Esther had established them, and just as they had accepted upon themselves and their descendants the matter of the fasts and their crying out.

### Validation

Rabbi Yochanan resolves the apparent difficulty by reading *divrei ha-tzomot ve-za'akatam u-ma'amar Esther* as a continuous phrase rather than placing the break at *ve-za'akatam*. The meaning then becomes: it was through *divrei ha-tzomot ve-za'akatam u-ma'amar Esther* — through the fasting, the crying out, and Esther's proclamation together — that *kiyam divrei ha-Purim ha-eleh*, these days of Purim were validated and brought about. The Gemara is thus reading the verse as a historical reference to what produced the Purim miracle in the first place, not as an institution of a future fast day. (The later practice of *Taanit Esther* is post-Talmudic, and Rashi's reading does not understand the verse as establishing such a fast.)

## Mordechai's Status



### Mishneh Status

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״כִּי מׇרְדֳּכַי הַיְּהוּדִי מִשְׁנֶה לַמֶּלֶךְ אֲחַשְׁוֵרוֹשׁ וְגָדוֹל לַיְּהוּדִים וְרָצוּי לְרוֹב אֶחָיו״. ״לְרוֹב אֶחָיו״, וְלֹא לְכׇל אֶחָיו. מְלַמֵּד שֶׁפֵּירְשׁוּ מִמֶּנּוּ מִקְצָת סַנְהֶדְרִין.
> **Translation:** The verse states: **“For Mordecai the Jew was second to the king Ahasuerus, and great among the Jews, and accepted by the majority of his brethren”** (Esther 10:3). The Gemara comments: The verse indicates that Mordecai was accepted only **“By the majority of his brethren,” but not by all his brethren.** This **teaches that some** members **of the Sanhedrin parted from him,** because he occupied himself with community needs, and was therefore compelled to neglect his Torah study. They felt that this was a mistake and that he should have remained active on the Sanhedrin.

The *Megillah* concludes: *ki Mordechai ha-Yehudi mishneh la-melech Achashverosh ve-gadol la-Yehudim ve-ratzui le-rov echav* — second to the king and great among the Jews and accepted by most of his brothers. *Le-rov echav ve-lo le-chol echav* — most but not all. The famous *midrash*: *melamed she-pirshu mimenu miktzat Sanhedrin* — some members of the *Sanhedrin* distanced themselves from him, because he had departed from the *beit midrash*.

### Torah Priority

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: גָּדוֹל תַּלְמוּד תּוֹרָה יוֹתֵר מֵהַצָּלַת נְפָשׁוֹת, דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא חָשֵׁיב לֵיהּ לְמׇרְדֳּכַי בָּתַר אַרְבְּעָה, וּלְבַסּוֹף בָּתַר חַמְשָׁה. מֵעִיקָּרָא כְּתִיב: ״אֲשֶׁר בָּאוּ עִם זְרוּבָּבֶל יֵשׁוּעַ נְחֶמְיָה שְׂרָיָה רְעֵלָיָה מׇרְדֳּכַי בִּלְשָׁן״, וּלְבַסּוֹף כְּתִיב: ״הַבָּאִים עִם זְרוּבָּבֶל יֵשׁוּעַ נְחֶמְיָה עֲזַרְיָה רַעַמְיָה נַחֲמָנִי מׇרְדֳּכַי בִּלְשָׁן״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yosef said: Studying Torah is greater than saving lives, as initially,** when listing the Jewish leaders who came to Eretz Yisrael, **Mordecai was mentioned after four** other people, **but at the end** he was listed **after five.** This is taken to indicate that his involvement in governmental affairs instead of in Torah study lowered his stature one notch. The Gemara proves this: **At first it is written: “Who came with Zerubbabel, Jeshua, Nehemiah, Seraiah, Reelaiah, Mordecai, Bilshan”** (Ezra 2:2); **but in the end** in a later list **it is written: “Who came with Zerubbabel, Jeshua, Nehemiah, Azariah, Raamiah, Nahmani, Mordecai, Bilshan”** (Nehemiah 7:7).

Rav Yosef draws the principle: *gadol talmud Torah yoter me-hatzalat nefashot* — *talmud Torah* is greater than saving lives. *De-me'ikara chashev leh le-Mordechai batar arba'ah* — originally, in Ezra, Mordechai is listed after four others, making him fifth on the list. *U-levasof batar chamishah* — later in the text, after the Purim story, he appears only after five others, falling to sixth place. He descended in standing precisely because his involvement in political affairs and saving lives drew him away from *Torah* study.

### Rank Change

The *pessukim* bear this out. Initially it is written: *asher ba'u im Zerubavel Yeshua Nechemiah Serayah Re'elayah Mordechai Bilshan* — Mordechai is fifth. Later: *ha-ba'im im Zerubavel Yeshua Nechemiah Azaryah Ra'amyah Nachamani Mordechai Bilshan* — Mordechai is sixth, having dropped one position. This is a remarkable statement in context, because the *Gemara* opened this tractate by saying that one suspends *talmud Torah* in favor of *mikra megillah* — thus seemingly elevating *mikra megillah* over *Torah* study — and yet here the Gemara is moving in the opposite direction, suggesting that Mordechai's very heroism in saving his people came at a cost to his standing in *Torah*. And crucially, the *Gemara* does not leave the matter at the level of popular perception — as though people simply viewed Mordechai as a man who had gone into politics and was therefore no longer regarded as a great *talmid chacham*. Rather, the *Gemara* affirms the point substantively: his involvement in affairs beyond *Torah* genuinely diminished his standing. One should also note that the formulation *gadol talmud Torah yoter me-hatzalat nefashot* — that *talmud Torah* is greater even than saving lives — is, taken literally, a startling claim. It cannot be meant as a practical *halachic* ruling; clearly one does not decline to save a life on the grounds that one must sit and learn *Torah*. These statements are better understood as rhetorical assertions about the primacy of *Torah*, meant to capture an ongoing tension: the absolute centrality of *Torah* study must be affirmed even while acknowledging that other obligations do, at times, legitimately take precedence.

## Torah Primacy



### Torah > Temple

Rava (and some say Rav Shmuel bar Marta) states: *gadol talmud Torah yoter mi-binyan Beit ha-Mikdash* — *talmud Torah* is greater than building the *Beit ha-Mikdash*.

### Ezra's Choice

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב וְאִיתֵּימָא רַב שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר מָרְתָּא: גָּדוֹל תַּלְמוּד תּוֹרָה יוֹתֵר מִבִּנְיַן בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ, שֶׁכׇּל זְמַן שֶׁבָּרוּךְ בֶּן נֵרִיָּה קַיָּים — לֹא הִנִּיחוֹ עֶזְרָא וְעָלָה.
> **Translation:** **Rav said, and some say** that **Rav Shmuel bar Marta** said: **Studying Torah is greater** and more important **than building the Temple.** A proof of this is that **for as long as Baruch ben Neriah was alive** in Babylonia, **Ezra,** who was his disciple, **did not leave him and go up** to Eretz Yisrael to build the Temple.

The proof: *she-kol zman she-Baruch ben Neriah kayam lo hinicho Ezra ve'ala* — as long as Baruch ben Neriah was alive in Bavel, Ezra did not leave to go up to the Land of Israel. Ezra, the paradigmatic figure of *Torah* transmission — *Ezra Hasofer*, the proto-rabbinic figure — only came in the second wave of returnees. The Gemara's explanation: his *rebbe* was still alive in Bavel, and he was not yet free to leave. The construction of the *Beit ha-Mikdash* was underway, and yet Ezra remained in *yeshiva*.

### Halakha/Aggada

This creates a striking tension that the *Gemara* leaves unresolved. The *halacha* is clear: one suspends *talmud Torah* for *mikra megillah*, and equally one suspends *talmud Torah* for *binyan Beit ha-Mikdash*. And yet the closing *sugyot* of this *perek* — centered on the very themes of *mikra megillah* and *Beit ha-Mikdash* — insist that *talmud Torah* surpasses them all. The *aggadeta* functions here as a counter-voice to the *halacha*, shaping attitudes and maintaining *Torah* study's primacy in the hierarchy of values even where the formal legal ruling goes the other way. This kind of juxtaposition — where *aggadeta* is placed deliberately alongside a *halacha* that points in the opposite direction — is a recognizable pattern in the *Gemara*, similar to the tension in *Berachot* around *tefilla* and *talmud Torah*, or the famous tension around *ad de-lo yada* and the story of Rava and Rabbi Zeira.

### Torah > Parents

Rava bar bar Rav Yitzchak states: *gadol talmud Torah yoter mi-kibbud av va-em* — *talmud Torah* is greater than *kibbud av va-em*. The proof: *she-kol otan shanim she-haya Yaakov Avinu b'veit Ever lo ne'enash* — during all the years that Yaakov our father was in the *yeshiva* of Ever, he was not punished.

### Yaakov's Years

This teaching presupposes that Yaakov spent fourteen hidden years learning in the *yeshiva* of Ever before his journey to Lavan in Aram Naharayim — a tradition found in Rashi on the *Torah*. The claim is that those fourteen years, spent away from his parents in order to study, were not counted against him.

### Age Math

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבָּה אָמַר רַב יִצְחָק בַּר שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר מָרְתָּא: גָּדוֹל תַּלְמוּד תּוֹרָה יוֹתֵר מִכִּבּוּד אָב וָאֵם, שֶׁכׇּל אוֹתָן שָׁנִים שֶׁהָיָה יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ בְּבֵית עֵבֶר לֹא נֶעֱנַשׁ, דְּאָמַר מָר:
> **Translation:** **Rabba said** that **Rav Yitzḥak bar Shmuel bar Marta said: Studying Torah is** greater and **more** important **than honoring one’s father and mother,** and a proof of this is **that for all those years that our father Jacob spent in the house of Eber** and studied Torah there **he was not punished** for having neglected to fulfill the mitzva of honoring one’s parents. **As the Master said:**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָמָּה נִמְנוּ שְׁנוֹתָיו שֶׁל יִשְׁמָעֵאל? כְּדֵי לְיַחֵס בָּהֶן שְׁנוֹתָיו שֶׁל יַעֲקֹב. דִּכְתִיב: ״וְאֵלֶּה שְׁנֵי חַיֵּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל מְאַת שָׁנָה וּשְׁלֹשִׁים שָׁנָה וְשֶׁבַע שָׁנִים״. כַּמָּה קַשִּׁישׁ יִשְׁמָעֵאל מִיִּצְחָק? אַרְבֵּיסַר שְׁנִין, דִּכְתִיב: ״וְאַבְרָם בֶּן שְׁמוֹנִים שָׁנָה וְשֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים בְּלֶדֶת הָגָר אֶת יִשְׁמָעֵאל לְאַבְרָם״, וּכְתִיב: ״וְאַבְרָהָם בֶּן מְאַת שָׁנָה בְּהִוָּלֶד לוֹ אֵת יִצְחָק בְּנוֹ״, וּכְתִיב: ״וְיִצְחָק בֶּן שִׁשִּׁים שָׁנָה בְּלֶדֶת אוֹתָם״, בַּר כַּמָּה הֲוָה יִשְׁמָעֵאל כִּדְאִתְיְלִיד יַעֲקֹב? בַּר שִׁבְעִים וְאַרְבְּעָה, כַּמָּה פָּיְישָׁן מִשְּׁנֵיהּ — שִׁתִּין וּתְלָת.
> **Translation:** **Why were the years of Ishmael mentioned** in the Torah? For what purpose were we told the life span of that wicked man? **In order to reckon through them the years of Jacob. As it is written: “And these are the years of the life of Ishmael, a hundred and thirty-seven years”** (Genesis 25:17). **How much older was Ishmael than Isaac? Fourteen years. As it is written: “And Abram was eighty-six years old when Hagar bore Ishmael to Abram”** (Genesis 16:16). **And it is written: “And Abraham was a hundred years old when his son Isaac was born to him”** (Genesis 21:5). **And it is written** with regard to Jacob and Esau: **“And Isaac was sixty years old when she bore them”** (Genesis 25:26). Based on these verses, **how old was Ishmael when Jacob was born? Seventy-four. How many of his years remained** then until his death? **Sixty-three,** as Ishmael died at the age of a hundred and thirty-seven.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתַנְיָא: הָיָה יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁנִּתְבָּרֵךְ מֵאָבִיו בֶּן שִׁשִּׁים וְשָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנָה, וּבוֹ בַּפֶּרֶק מֵת יִשְׁמָעֵאל. דִּכְתִיב: ״וַיַּרְא עֵשָׂו כִּי בֵרַךְ וְגוֹ׳ וַיֵּלֶךְ עֵשָׂו אֶל יִשְׁמָעֵאל וַיִּקַּח אֶת מָחֲלַת בַּת יִשְׁמָעֵאל אֲחוֹת נְבָיוֹת״, מִמַּשְׁמַע שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״בַּת יִשְׁמָעֵאל״, אֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ שֶׁהִיא אֲחוֹת נְבָיוֹת? מְלַמֵּד שֶׁקִּידְּשָׁהּ יִשְׁמָעֵאל וָמֵת, וְהִשִּׂיאָהּ נְבָיוֹת אָחִיהָ.
> **Translation:** **And it was taught** in a *baraita*: **Jacob our father was sixty-three years old at the time he was blessed by his father, and at that same time Ishmael died.** How is it known that these two events occurred at the same time? **As it is written: “When Esau saw that Isaac had blessed** Jacob…**then Esau went to Ishmael and took for a wife Mahalath, the daughter of Ishmael,** Abraham’s son, **the sister of Nebaioth”** (Genesis 28:6–9). **From the fact that** it **is stated: “the daughter of Ishmael,” do I not know that she was the sister of Nebaioth?** For what purpose then does the verse say this explicitly? **This teaches that Ishmael betrothed her** to Esau **and** in the meantime he **died, and Nebaioth her brother married her off.** Therefore, special mention is made of Nebaioth. Consequently, it is understood that Jacob was sixty-three years old when he received his blessing and left his father’s house.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שִׁתִּין וּתְלָת וְאַרְבֵּיסַר עַד דְּמִתְיְלִיד יוֹסֵף, הָא שִׁבְעִין וְשִׁבְעָה, וּכְתִיב: ״וְיוֹסֵף בֶּן שְׁלֹשִׁים שָׁנָה בְּעׇמְדוֹ לִפְנֵי פַּרְעֹה״, הָא מְאָה וּשְׁבַע, שַׁב דְּשִׂבְעָא וְתַרְתֵּי דְּכַפְנָא — הָא מְאָה וְשִׁיתְּסַר,
> **Translation:** If we calculate these **sixty-three** years **and** the **fourteen until Joseph was born, this** means that Jacob should have been **seventy-seven** at the time of Joseph’s birth. **And it is written: “And Joseph was thirty years old when he stood before Pharaoh”** (Genesis 41:46). **This** indicates that Jacob should have then been at least **a hundred and seven** years old when Joseph was thirty. Add the **seven** years **of plenty and** the **two of famine,** and **this** would then indicate that Jacob should have been **a hundred and sixteen** years old when he arrived in Egypt in the second year of the famine.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּכְתִיב: ״וַיֹּאמֶר פַּרְעֹה אֶל יַעֲקֹב כַּמָּה יְמֵי שְׁנֵי חַיֶּיךָ. וַיֹּאמֶר יַעֲקֹב אֶל פַּרְעֹה יְמֵי שְׁנֵי מְגוּרַי שְׁלֹשִׁים וּמְאַת שָׁנָה״, מְאָה וְשִׁיתְּסַר הָוְיָין,
> **Translation:** **But it is written: “And Pharaoh said to Jacob, How many are the days of the years of your life? And Jacob said to Pharaoh, The days of the years of my sojournings are a hundred and thirty years”** (Genesis 47:8–9). Jacob indicated that he was a hundred and thirty when he arrived in Egypt, **which is** different from the **hundred and sixteen** years calculated previously. Where are the missing fourteen years from Jacob’s lifetime?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ אַרְבַּע עֶשְׂרֵה שְׁנִין דַּהֲוָה בְּבֵית עֵבֶר לָא חָשֵׁיב לְהוּ. דְּתַנְיָא: הָיָה יַעֲקֹב בְּבֵית עֵבֶר מוּטְמָן אַרְבַּע עֶשְׂרֵה שָׁנָה. עֵבֶר מֵת לְאַחַר שֶׁיָּרַד יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ לַאֲרַם נַהֲרַיִם שְׁתֵּי שָׁנִים, יָצָא מִשָּׁם וּבָא לוֹ לַאֲרַם נַהֲרַיִם, נִמְצָא כְּשֶׁעָמַד עַל הַבְּאֵר — בֶּן שִׁבְעִים וָשֶׁבַע שָׁנָה.
> **Translation:** **Rather, learn from here** that the fourteen years that Jacob spent in the house of Eber are not counted here. **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: **Jacob was** studying **in the house of Eber for fourteen years** while **in hiding** from his brother Esau. If we were to calculate the life spans recorded in the Torah, we would find that **Eber died** when Jacob was seventy-nine years old, **two years after Jacob our father went down to Aram-naharaim,** to the house of Laban. When Jacob **left** after completing his studying **there,** he **then went** immediately **to Aram-naharaim.** Therefore, **when** Jacob **stood at the well** upon his arrival in Aram-naharaim, **he was seventy-seven years old.**

The Gemara establishes this through a careful reconstruction of Yaakov's age. The Torah records that Yishmael died at 137. Yishmael was 14 years older than Yitzchak, since Avraham was 86 at Yishmael's birth and 100 at Yitzchak's birth. Yitzchak was 60 when Yaakov and Esav were born, making Yishmael 74 at that point. Since Yishmael died at 137, he had 63 more years to live from the time of Yaakov's birth. A *baraita* teaches that Yaakov was 63 when he received his father's blessing and departed — and at that very time, Yishmael died.

The Gemara establishes this through the verse that describes Eisav going to marry Machalat, the daughter of Yishmael, sister of Nevayot. Since she is Yishmael's daughter, why specify that she is the sister of Nevayot? *Melamed shekiddshah Yishmael umet* — Yishmael had betrothed her to Eisav and then died, so that at the actual *nisuin* it was her brother Nevayot who gave her away. This establishes that Yishmael died at precisely the time these events occurred — when Yaakov was 63. Worth noting is the way the Gemara uses what seems like a redundant phrase in the verse — "sister of Nevayot" — as the precise hook for the chronological inference. The apparently unnecessary specification is the very thing that tells us Nevayot had to step in because Yishmael was no longer alive, which anchors the date of Yishmael's death, which in turn anchors Yaakov's age at departure. The Gemara is reading quite closely.

From age 63, fourteen more years until Yosef was born (*arba esreh ad demitayallid Yosef*), making Yaakov 77 at Yosef's birth. Yosef was 30 when he stood before Paroh, making Yaakov 107. Seven years of plenty and two years of famine add nine more years, bringing Yaakov to 116. But when Paroh asks Yaakov his age, Yaakov says he is 130. The discrepancy is 14 years — precisely the fourteen years of *Beit Ever*. *Shema mina arba esreh dehavah b'veit Ever lo chashiv lehu* — those fourteen years in the *yeshiva* of Ever were not counted in the tally, which is itself the proof that he was not punished for them.

### Parallel Years

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמְנָלַן דְּלָא אִיעֲנַשׁ — דְּתַנְיָא: נִמְצָא יוֹסֵף שֶׁפֵּירַשׁ מֵאָבִיו עֶשְׂרִים וּשְׁתַּיִם שָׁנָה, כְּשֵׁם שֶׁפֵּירַשׁ יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ מֵאָבִיו, דְּיַעֲקֹב תְּלָתִין וְשִׁיתָּא הָוְיָין! אֶלָּא: אַרְבֵּיסַר דַּהֲוָה בְּבֵית עֵבֶר לָא חָשֵׁיב לְהוּ.
> **Translation:** **And from where do we** derive **that** Jacob **was not punished** for the fourteen years that he was in the house of Eber, during which time he failed to fulfill the mitzva of honoring one’s parents? **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: **It turns out that Joseph was away from his father for twenty-two years, just as Jacob our father was away from his** own **father** for that same period of time. According to the previous calculation, however, the *baraita* is difficult, as **Jacob** was absent for **thirty-six** years. Rather, conclude from here that **the fourteen** years **that he was in the house of Eber are not counted,** as he was not punished for them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סוֹף סוֹף דְּבֵית לָבָן עֶשְׂרִין שְׁנִין הָוְיָין! אֶלָּא מִשּׁוּם דְּאִשְׁתַּהִי בְּאוֹרְחָא תַּרְתֵּין שְׁנִין, דְּתַנְיָא: יָצָא מֵאֲרַם נַהֲרַיִם וּבָא לוֹ לְסֻכּוֹת, וְעָשָׂה שָׁם שְׁמוֹנָה עָשָׂר חוֹדֶשׁ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְיַעֲקֹב נָסַע סֻכּוֹתָה וַיִּבֶן לוֹ בָּיִת וּלְמִקְנֵהוּ עָשָׂה סֻכּוֹת״, וּבְבֵית אֵל עָשָׂה שִׁשָּׁה חֳדָשִׁים, וְהִקְרִיב זְבָחִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises an objection: But **ultimately,** Jacob was in **Laban’s house for** only **twenty years.** Why, then, is he faulted for being away from his father for twenty-two years? **Rather,** he was punished **because on his journey** back from Aram-naharaim **he tarried** another **two years** before returning home to his parents, **as it is taught** in a *baraita*: Jacob **left Aram-naharaim and came to Sukkot, and spent eighteen months there, as it is stated: “And Jacob journeyed to Sukkot, built himself a house, and made booths [*sukkot*] for his cattle”** (Genesis 33:17). The Gemara understands this verse to mean that first he made booths [*Sukkot*], to live in during the summer, and then he built a house in the winter, and afterward he again made booths [*sukkot*] during the next summer, indicating that he must have been there for eighteen months. **He then was in Bethel for six months, and he brought offerings,** totaling two years in all. In this way, all the various calculations of years are reconciled.

The Gemara then connects this to Yosef's absence from Yaakov. A *baraita* teaches: *nimtza Yosef sheperash me'aviv esrim ushtayim shanah* — Yosef was separated from his father for twenty-two years. He was 17 when sold, and we have established through the calculations above that he was 39 when reunited with Yaakov (30 before Paroh, plus seven years of plenty and two of famine) — indeed twenty-two years. *Keshem shepeirash Yaakov Avinu me'aviv* — this parallels the twenty-two years Yaakov was not with his own father. But wait: the math for Yaakov yields 36 years away (14 for Rachel and Leah, 6 for the flocks, plus the 14 of *Beit Ever*, plus 2 additional years of travel). Subtracting the 14 of *Beit Ever* leaves 22 — the parallel is exact. *Ella arbisar d'havavei bei Ever lo chashiv lehu* — the fourteen of *Beit Ever* are not counted, and the remaining twenty-two correspond precisely to the twenty-two years Yosef was absent. The symmetry itself demonstrates that the fourteen years of *Torah* study were not reckoned as a period of negligence toward his father.

The remaining discrepancy — the 20 years in Lavan's house, not yet 22 — is resolved by adding the two years Yaakov spent traveling home: eighteen months at Succoth (*vayiven lo bayit ulemiknehu asah Succoth*, implying a summer, winter, and summer — eighteen months), and six months at *Beit El* where he brought offerings. Together these account for the additional two years, bringing the total to twenty-two.

One should note a nuance in how to understand this teaching. Yaakov was told by both parents to go to Lavan; his departure was at their direction. The question is therefore not simply whether he should have been home but whether the specific circumstances — the fourteen years in *Beit Ever*, the additional six years working for flocks, the two years of travel — represent a failure of *kibbud av va-em*. The Gemara's point is subtler: even when one has good reason to be away, even when the *halacha* permits or even mandates the departure, the absence from parents carries a real-world cost. This tension between the *halakhic* rule and its real-world dimension is itself instructive. In *halacha*, *talmud Torah* taking precedence over *kibbud av va-em* expresses itself in cases such as one who wishes to study at a particular *yeshiva* over a parent's objection — the *halakha* sides with going to learn. And yet, if one is sitting and learning and a parent asks for a glass of water, one stops learning and brings it; in that moment *kibbud av va-em* takes precedence over the act of *talmud Torah*. The two values exist in an ongoing dialectic rather than a simple hierarchy. The *halacha* regarding one who moves away for *Torah* study or livelihood or even *aliyah* may well permit it, but the *aggadata* here preserves the awareness that something of *kibbud av va-em* is nonetheless not being fulfilled in the fullest sense.

## Reading Rules



### Forward Only

The *mishnah* of the next *perek* opens: *hakorei et ha-Megillah lemafrea lo yatza* — one who reads the *Megillah* out of order, reading later passages before earlier ones, has not fulfilled the obligation.

### Language Rules

*Kara al peh* — if one read it from memory without looking at a scroll, *kara Targum* — if one read it in Aramaic translation, *bechol lashon* — or in any other language, *lo yatza* — one has not fulfilled the obligation.

### Language Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֲדַרַן עֲלָךְ מְגִילָּה נִקְרֵאת
> **Translation:** May we return to you Chapter “The Megillah is Read”

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַקּוֹרֵא אֶת הַמְּגִילָּה לְמַפְרֵעַ — לֹא יָצָא. קְרָאָהּ עַל פֶּה, קְרָאָהּ תַּרְגּוּם בְּכׇל לָשׁוֹן — לֹא יָצָא. אֲבָל קוֹרִין אוֹתָהּ לַלּוֹעֲזוֹת בְּלַעַז, וְהַלּוֹעֵז שֶׁשָּׁמַע אַשּׁוּרִית — יָצָא.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: With regard to **one who reads the Megilla out of order,** reading a later section first, and then going back to the earlier section, **he has not fulfilled** his obligation. If **he read it by heart,** or **if he read it in** Aramaic **translation** or **in any** other **language** that he does not understand, **he has not fulfilled** his obligation. **However, for those who speak a foreign language, one may read** the Megilla **in** that **foreign language. And one who speaks a foreign language who heard** the Megilla read in ***Ashurit*,** i.e., in Hebrew, **has fulfilled** his obligation.

*Aval korin osa liloazos bilaaz* — however, one may read the *Megillah* to a non-Hebrew speaker in their own foreign language. And conversely: *vehaloez sheshama Ashuris yatza* — a non-Hebrew speaker who hears the *Megillah* read in Hebrew has fulfilled the obligation, even without understanding. The Gemara will explore what distinguishes these cases.

### Breaks Allowed

*Karaah seirugin* — if one read with pauses and interruptions — *umisnamnem* — or while dozing off — *yatza*, one has nonetheless fulfilled the obligation.

### Intent Required

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קְרָאָהּ סֵירוּגִין וּמִתְנַמְנֵם — יָצָא. הָיָה כּוֹתְבָהּ, דּוֹרְשָׁהּ וּמַגִּיהָהּ, אִם כִּוֵּון לִבּוֹ — יָצָא, וְאִם לָאו — לֹא יָצָא.
> **Translation:** **If one read** the Megilla **at intervals,** pausing and resuming, **or** while **he is dozing off, he has fulfilled his obligation. If one was writing** a Megilla, **or expounding upon it, or correcting it,** and he read all its words as he was doing so, the following distinction applies: **If he had intent** to fulfill his obligation with that reading **he has fulfilled his obligation, but if not, he has not fulfilled** his obligation.

*Haya koshva dorsha umagiha* — if one was in the process of writing the *Megillah*, or expounding on it (*darshan*-style), or proofreading it, and in doing so verbalized the words — *im kiven libo yatza* — if one directed one's mind toward the act of reading as a *mitzvah*, one has fulfilled the obligation. *Ve-im lav lo yatza* — if not, one has not. At minimum, one must be aware that one is performing an act of reading; a question remains whether one must additionally have conscious intent to fulfill the *mitzvah*.

### Material Rules

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָיְתָה כְּתוּבָה בְּסַם וּבְסִיקְרָא וּבְקוֹמוֹס וּבְקַנְקַנְתּוֹם, עַל הַנְּיָיר וְעַל הַדִּפְתְּרָא — לֹא יָצָא, עַד שֶׁתְּהֵא כְּתוּבָה אַשּׁוּרִית, עַל הַסֵּפֶר, וּבִדְיוֹ.
> **Translation:** **If** one reads from a Megilla that **was written** not with ink but **with *sam* or with *sikra* or with *komos* or with *kankantom*,** or from a Megilla that was written not on parchment but **on *neyar* or on *diftera*,** a kind of unprocessed leather, **he has not fulfilled** his obligation. He does not fulfill his obligation **unless** he reads from a Megilla that **is written in *Ashurit*,** i.e., in the Hebrew language and using the Hebrew script, **upon parchment and with ink.**

*Haysa kesuva besam uvesikra uvekumus uvekankantum* — if the *Megillah* was written in various types of dye or pigment other than *dyo*, *al hanyar* — on paper, which in antiquity was a cloth-based material — *ve-al hadiphtera* — or on processed leather that is not *klaf* — *lo yatza*, one has not fulfilled the obligation.

### Script & Ink

*Ad shetehei kesuva Ashuris al hasefer u-vidyo* — until it is written in the Hebrew script, on proper parchment (*klaf*), and with *dyo*, the recognized *halachic* ink. This requirement — Hebrew script, *klaf*, and *dyo* — connects back directly to the earlier discussion: the *Megillah* is treated with the same formality as the most demanding categories of *sifrei kodesh*.

## Backward Prohibition



### Verse Source

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי? אָמַר רָבָא, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״כִּכְתָבָם וְכִזְמַנָּם״ — מָה זְמַנָּם לְמַפְרֵעַ לָא, אַף כְּתָבָם לְמַפְרֵעַ לָא.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: It was taught in the mishna that one who reads the Megilla out of order has not fulfilled his obligation. The Gemara asks: **From where are these matters** derived? **Rava said: The verse states** concerning Purim: “That they should unfailingly observe these two days **according to their writing, and according to their appointed times** every year” (Esther 9:27), and the word “times” is referring to the two days of Purim, the fourteenth and the fifteenth of Adar. And we learn by way of analogy: **Just as their appointed times cannot be out of order,** as the fifteenth of Adar cannot possibly come before the fourteenth, **so too, their writing must not be out of order.**

The Gemara asks: *manah hanei milei* — from where is the prohibition against reading the *Megillah* out of order derived? Rabba cites the verse: *keichsavim u-chezmanim* — as written and in their times.

### Time Logic

The analogy: just as time does not go backward — one does not observe the 15th celebration before the 14th — so too *af ksavam lemafrea lo* — what is written about them is not read backward.

### Action Not Reading

The Gemara immediately objects: *midi keria kesiv hacha? Asiya kesiv hacha* — is it reading that the verse refers to? The verse says *lihyot osim* — to perform these days *keichsavim u-chezmanim*. It is speaking about the act of observance, not about reading. The verse does not address *keria* at all.

### Memory & Action

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִידֵּי ״קְרִיאָה״ כְּתִיבָה הָכָא? ״עֲשִׂיָּיה״ כְּתִיבָה, דִּכְתִיב: ״לִהְיוֹת עוֹשִׂים אֵת שְׁנֵי הַיָּמִים״, אֶלָּא מֵהָכָא, דִּכְתִיב: ״וְהַיָּמִים הָאֵלֶּה נִזְכָּרִים וְנַעֲשִׂים״, אִיתַּקַּשׁ זְכִירָה לַעֲשִׂיָּיה: מָה עֲשִׂיָּיה לְמַפְרֵעַ לָא, אַף זְכִירָה לְמַפְרֵעַ לָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this derivation: **Is reading written here at all?** It is “**observing”** that **is written** here in this verse, not reading, **as it is written: “That they should unfailingly observe these two days** according to their writing, and according to their appointed times.” **Rather,** the proof is **from here, as it is written: “And that these days should be remembered and observed** throughout every generation” (Esther 9:28). **Remembering is juxtaposed to observing,** indicating: **Just as observing cannot be out of order,** as was derived from the words “That they should unfailingly observe these two days according to their writing, and according to their appointed times,” **so too, remembering,** by reading the Megilla, **may not be out of order.**

The Gemara therefore redirects: *ela mehacha* — rather, derive it from the verse *vehayamim ha-eleh nizkarim venaasim* — these days are remembered and performed. *Iskash zchira la-asiya* — the verse links *zechira* (memory/reading) to *asiya* (observance). Just as *asiya lemafrea lo* — one does not observe Purim out of order — so too *zchira lemafrea lo* — one does not read it out of order.

### Other Mitzvot

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנָא: וְכֵן בְּהַלֵּל, וְכֵן בִּקְרִיאַת שְׁמַע וּבִתְפִלָּה.
> **Translation:** § The Sages **taught** in a *baraita*: This *halakha* of not reading out of order applies **also to *hallel*, and also to the recitation of *Shema*, and** also **to** the *Amida* **prayer,** meaning that to fulfill one’s obligation he must recite the text of each of these in order.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַלֵּל מְנָלַן? רַבָּה אָמַר, דִּכְתִיב: ״מִמִּזְרַח שֶׁמֶשׁ עַד מְבוֹאוֹ״. רַב יוֹסֵף אָמַר: ״זֶה הַיּוֹם עָשָׂה ה׳״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **From where do we** derive that ***hallel*** may not be recited out of order? **Rabba said: As it is written** in *hallel*: **“From the rising of the sun until its setting** the Lord’s name is to be praised” (Psalms 113:3). Just as the sunrise and sunset cannot be reversed, so too, *hallel* may not be recited out of order. **Rav Yosef said:** It is derived from the verse in *hallel* that states: **“This is the day that the Lord has made”** (Psalms 118:24); just as the day follows a certain order, so too, *hallel* must be recited in its proper order.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אַוְיָא אָמַר: ״יְהִי שֵׁם ה׳ מְבוֹרָךְ״. וְרַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק, וְאִיתֵּימָא רַב אַחָא בַּר יַעֲקֹב אָמַר: מֵהָכָא: ״מֵעַתָּה וְעַד עוֹלָם״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Avya said:** It is derived from the verse in *hallel*: **“Blessed be the name of the Lord”** (Psalms 113:2), indicating that the blessing of God must “be” just as it is written. **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said, and some say** that it was **Rav Aḥa bar Ya’akov** who said: It is derived from **here,** the end of the aforementioned verse: **“From now and for evermore”** (Psalms 113:2), i.e., it should be like time, which cannot be reversed.

A *baraita* teaches that the same principle applies to *Hallel*, *krias Shema*, and *tefilla* — all must be performed in their proper order. The Gemara then asks for the source in each case. Rabba cites *mimizrach shemesh ad mevo'o* — from sunrise to sunset — which describes a natural sequence and applies to the *psukim* of *Hallel*. Rav Yosef cites *zeh hayom asah Hashem* — a day unfolds in sequence. Rav Avya cites *yehi shem Hashem mevorach*, which Rashi reads as implying natural sequential order. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak and some say Rav Acha bar Yaakov cite *me'atah ve'ad olam* — from now to forever — again implying an irreversible sequence.

## Shema Language



### Language Dispute

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע, דְּתַנְיָא: קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע כִּכְתָבָהּ, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי, וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: בְּכׇל לָשׁוֹן. מַאי טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי? אָמַר קְרָא:
> **Translation:** From where do we know one has not fulfilled his obligation of **reciting the *Shema*** if he recited it out of order? **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: **The recital of the *Shema* must be as it is written,** i.e., in Hebrew; this is **the statement of Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi. **But the Rabbis say:** It may be recited **in any language.** The Gemara asks: **What is the reason of Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi? **The verse states:**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְהָיוּ״, בַּהֲוָיָתָן יְהוּ. וְרַבָּנַן, מַאי טַעְמָא? אָמַר קְרָא: ״שְׁמַע״, בְּכׇל לָשׁוֹן שֶׁאַתָּה שׁוֹמֵעַ.
> **Translation:** **“And** these words…**shall be”** (Deuteronomy 6:6), teaching that these words, the words of the *Shema*, always **“shall be” as they are,** i.e., in the Hebrew language. The Gemara asks: **And** as for **the Sages, what is the reason** for their opinion? **The verse states: “Hear,** O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4), which could also be translated, “Understand, O Israel,” indicating that you may recite these words **in any language that you hear,** i.e., understand.

A *baraita* states: *Krias Shema ke'chsavah* — *Krias Shema* must be recited as it is written, meaning in Hebrew. This is the position of Rabbi. The Rabbis (*Rabbanan*) disagree: *bechol lashon* — it may be recited in any language.

### Rabbi's Source

Rabbi's source is the verse *ve'hayu ve'hayu ha-devarim ha-eleh* — *ve'hayu* implies that the words must remain exactly as they are, in their original Hebrew form.

### Rabbanan Source

The Rabbis derive from the word *Shema* itself: *bechol lashon she'atah shome'a* — however you are able to hear and understand. The obligation is to hear and comprehend, which can be accomplished in any language.

### Hearing Dispute

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי נָמֵי, הָא כְּתִיב: ״שְׁמַע״? הַהוּא מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ: הַשְׁמַע לְאָזְנֶיךָ מַה שֶּׁאַתָּה מוֹצִיא מִפִּיךָ. וְרַבָּנַן — סָבְרִי כְּמַאן דְּאָמַר הַקּוֹרֵא אֶת שְׁמַע וְלֹא הִשְׁמִיעַ לְאׇזְנוֹ יָצָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** according to **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **as well, isn’t it** indeed **written, “hear”?** What does he learn from this word, if not that the *Shema* may be recited in any language? The Gemara answers: **This** word **is necessary** to teach something else: **Make heard to your ears what your mouth is saying,** i.e., the *Shema* must be recited audibly, not merely thought in one’s heart. The Gemara asks: **And** how do **the Sages** know this? The Gemara explains: They **hold like the one who said** that if **one recites the *Shema* but does not make it audible to his ears, he has** nevertheless **fulfilled** his obligation.

Rabbi further derives from *Shema* that one must verbalize the words audibly enough to hear them oneself: *hashmi'a le'oznecha mah she'atah motzi mipicha* — make your ears hear what your mouth utters. The Rabbis, by contrast, hold with the view that *hakorei Shema velo hishmia le'ozno yatza* — even if one did not hear one's own words, the obligation is fulfilled.

### Ritual vs Meaning

There is a coherent conceptual link between Rabbi's two positions. Rabbi focuses on the ritual, objective dimensions of the act: the specific Hebrew text must be used, and the words must be verbalized audibly. The Rabbis, by contrast, orient toward meaning and comprehension: what matters is that one is communicating the content to oneself, whether in Hebrew or another language, and whether or not one can physically hear one's own voice. Rabbi demands more of the formal ritual act; the Rabbis are more attuned to the act of understanding and inner address.

### Resolution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן נָמֵי, הָכְתִיב ״וְהָיוּ״? הַהוּא מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ שֶׁלֹּא יִקְרָא לְמַפְרֵעַ. וְרַבִּי, שֶׁלֹּא יִקְרָא לְמַפְרֵעַ מְנָא לֵיהּ? מִ״דְּבָרִים״ ״הַדְּבָרִים״. וְרַבָּנַן — ״דְּבָרִים״ ״הַדְּבָרִים״ לָא מַשְׁמַע לְהוּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** according to **the Sages as well, isn’t it** indeed **written, “And** these words **shall be”?** What do they learn from this, if not that the *Shema* must be recited in Hebrew? The Gemara answers: **That** word **is necessary** to teach **that one must not recite** the words of the *Shema* **out of order,** but they “shall be” as they are, in the proper order. The Gemara asks: **And from where does Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi learn **that one must not recite** the *Shema* **out of order?** The Gemara answers: He derives it from the fact that the verse does not say just: **Words,** but **“the words,”** referring to specific words, which teaches that they must be recited in their proper order without any variation. The Gemara asks: **And** what do **the Sages** learn from the phrase “the words”? The difference between **words** and **“the words” is inconsequential according to them.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֵימָא קָסָבַר רַבִּי כׇּל הַתּוֹרָה כּוּלָּהּ בְּכׇל לָשׁוֹן נֶאֶמְרָה, דְּאִי סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ בִּלְשׁוֹן הַקּוֹדֶשׁ נֶאֶמְרָה — לְמָה לִי לְמִכְתַּב ״וְהָיוּ״!
> **Translation:** The Gemara analyzes the dispute: **Shall we say that Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **maintains** that **the entire Torah may be recited in any language? As, if it enters your mind** to say that the entire Torah **may be recited only in the sacred tongue,** Hebrew, and not in any other language, **why do I** need the Torah **to write “and** these words **shall be”** with respect to the *Shema*? Why would I think that the *Shema* is different from the rest of the Torah?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִצְטְרִיךְ, סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ ״שְׁמַע״ — כְּרַבָּנַן, כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״וְהָיוּ״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this argument: There is no proof from here, as even if the Torah must generally be recited in Hebrew **it is** nevertheless **necessary** to specify the matter here, since without such specification **it might have entered your mind** to say that in this context **“hear”** means understand, **as** maintained by **the Sages,** and that the *Shema* may be recited in any language. Therefore **the Merciful One writes** in the Torah, **“and** these words **shall be,”** to teach us that the *Shema* may be recited only in the original Hebrew.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֵימָא קָסָבְרִי רַבָּנַן כׇּל הַתּוֹרָה בִּלְשׁוֹן הַקּוֹדֶשׁ נֶאֶמְרָה, דְּאִי סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ בְּכׇל לָשׁוֹן נֶאֶמְרָה — לְמָה לִי לְמִכְתַּב ״שְׁמַע״!
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Shall we say** then **that the Sages maintain** that **the entire Torah must be recited** specifically **in the sacred tongue,** Hebrew? **As, if it enters your mind** to say that the entire Torah **may be recited in any language, why do I** need the Torah **to write “hear”** with respect to the *Shema*? Why would one think that the *Shema* is different from the rest of the Torah?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיצְטְרִיךְ, סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ אָמֵינָא ״וְהָיוּ״ — כְּרַבִּי, כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״שְׁמַע״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this argument: Even if the Torah may generally be recited in any language, **it was** nevertheless **necessary** to specify the matter here. Without such specification **it could enter your mind to say** that the words **“and** these words **shall be”** teach that the *Shema* may be recited only in Hebrew, **as** asserted by **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi. Therefore **the Merciful One writes** the word **“hear”** in the Torah, to teach us that the *Shema* may be recited in any language.

The Gemara then asks: given that *Rabbanan* allow any language, why does the verse say *ve'hayu* at all? And given that Rabbi requires Hebrew, why does the verse say *Shema*? The Gemara resolves both questions. For Rabbanan: even if the default were any language, *ve'hayu* was still necessary — otherwise one might have concluded from *Shema* that Hebrew was required, following Rabbi's reasoning. So *kasav rachmana ve'hayu* to insist that any language suffices. For Rabbi: even if the default were Hebrew, *Shema* was still necessary — because without it one might have misread *ve'hayu* to require an additional condition beyond Hebrew. Each verse addresses a potential misreading, not the baseline assumption.

The Gemara also uses this discussion to raise a broader question: does Rabbi hold that the entire *Torah* was given to be recited in any language (*bechol lashon ne'emrah*), making the default permissive, with *ve'hayu* functioning as a specific restriction? Or do the Rabbis hold that the default for all *Torah* recitations is Hebrew (*bilshon hakodesh ne'emrah*), with *Shema* functioning as a specific permission? The Gemara works through both possibilities and concludes that neither position can be simply identified with one side of the broader question about default language for *Torah* recitations generally, since each verse serves a necessary corrective function regardless of what the underlying default might be. This question of a general default is not merely technical: throughout the *Torah* there are commanded recitations — *mikra bikkurim*, the declarations on *Har Gerizim ve'Har Eival*, and others — and it is not obvious whether such passages carry a presumption of Hebrew or a presumption of any language. The debate over *Shema* may reflect, or may simply run parallel to, that wider disagreement; the two need not be identical disputes.

What is striking, and worth noting, is that it is Rabbi — who demands Hebrew — who also demands that the words be articulated in a manner audible to oneself. The same figure who insists on the formal, specific vehicle of *lashon hakodesh* equally insists on a particular, concrete mode of verbal performance. This internal coherence in Rabbi's position is worth holding onto, a question of what *zachira* — remembering through recitation — actually requires as an act, which the *Gemara* will return to in a different context.

## Tefilla Order



### Shimon Hapakuli

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּפִלָּה מְנָא לַן? דְּתַנְיָא: שִׁמְעוֹן הַפָּקוֹלִי הִסְדִּיר שְׁמוֹנֶה עֶשְׂרֵה בְּרָכוֹת לִפְנֵי רַבָּן גַּמְלִיאֵל עַל הַסֵּדֶר בְּיַבְנֶה. אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן, וְאָמְרִי לַהּ בְּמַתְנִיתָא תָּנָא: מֵאָה וְעֶשְׂרִים זְקֵנִים, וּבָהֶם כַּמָּה נְבִיאִים, תִּיקְּנוּ שְׁמוֹנֶה עֶשְׂרֵה בְּרָכוֹת עַל הַסֵּדֶר.
> **Translation:** § The *baraita* cited previously taught that the *halakha* against reciting a text out of order applies to the *Amida* **prayer** as well. The Gemara asks: **From where do we** derive this? **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: **Shimon HaPakuli arranged the eighteen blessings** of the *Amida* prayer **before Rabban Gamliel in their** fixed **order in Yavne,** which indicates that there is a specific order to these blessings that must not be changed. **Rabbi Yoḥanan said, and some say that it was taught in a *baraita*: A hundred and twenty Elders,** i.e., the Men of the Great Assembly, and **among them several prophets, established** the **eighteen blessings** of the *Amida* **in their** fixed **order,** which also shows that the order of these blessings may not be changed.

A *baraita* states: *Shimon Hapakuli hesdir shemoneh esrei berachot lifnei Rabban Gamliel al ha-seder be-Yavneh* — Shimon Hapakuli (apparently a tradesman, possibly a cotton merchant) organized the 18 *berachot* in their order before Rabban Gamliel at Yavneh. He did not author them; he arranged the existing *berachot* into a fixed order and fixed text, presenting this arrangement before Rabban Gamliel for ratification.

### Elder Tradition

Rabbi Yochanan teaches an alternative tradition: *me'ah ve-esrim zekenim uvahem kama nevi'im tiknu shmoneh esrei berachot al ha-seder* — 120 elders, including among them several prophets, instituted the 18 *berachot* in their order. The Gemara will work to reconcile these two traditions, but in either case the tradition points back to the prophetic and elder-led institution of communal prayer in the early Second Temple period.

### Opening Praises

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁאוֹמְרִים אָבוֹת — שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״הָבוּ לַה׳ בְּנֵי אֵלִים״. וּמִנַּיִן שֶׁאוֹמְרִים גְּבוּרוֹת — שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״הָבוּ לַה׳ כָּבוֹד וָעוֹז״. וּמִנַּיִן שֶׁאוֹמְרִים קְדוּשּׁוֹת — שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״הָבוּ לַה׳ כְּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ לַה׳ בְּהַדְרַת קֹדֶשׁ״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara proceeds to explain this order: **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: **From where** is it derived **that one says** the blessing of **the Patriarchs,** the first blessing of the *Amida*? **As it is stated: “Ascribe to the Lord, mighty ones”** (Psalms 29:1), which means that one should mention before the Lord the mighty ones of the world, i.e., the Patriarchs. **And from where** is it derived **that one** then **says** the blessing of **mighty deeds? As it is stated** in the continuation of that verse: **“Ascribe to the Lord glory and strength”** (Psalms 29:1). **And from where** is it derived **that one** then **says** the blessing of **holiness? As it is stated** in the next verse: **“Give to the Lord the glory due to His name; worship the Lord in the beauty of holiness”** (Psalms 29:2).

The *baraita* proceeds to ground the three opening *berachot* — *avot*, *gevurot*, and *kedushot* — in Scripture. *Minayin she'omrim avot* — from where do we derive the blessing of *avot*? *Havu la-Hashem bnei eilim* — give to God, sons of the mighty, i.e., the forebears: Avraham, Yitzchak, and Yaakov. *Minayin she'omrim gevurot* — from where *gevurot*? *Havu la-Hashem kavod va-oz* — give to God honor and strength. *Minayin she'omrim kedushot* — from where *kedushot*? *Havu la-Hashem kvod shemo hishtachavu la-Hashem behadrat kodesh* — give to God the honor of His name, bow before God in holy splendor.

### Avot's Role

The structure of these opening *berachot* addresses a fundamental challenge of prayer: how can a finite, corporeal human being presume to approach an infinite God? The *berachah* of *avot* provides the entry point — we are not coming on our own authority but as inheritors of a relationship already established by the *avot*, who have already, in some sense, legitimated our standing before God. Then *gevurot* and *kedushot* acknowledge the immense gap: God's power is beyond measure, and God's *kedushah* marks the absolute difference between Creator and creature. These three *berachot* together constitute both an acknowledgment of that gap and the inherited claim that allows one nonetheless to stand in prayer. We will continue with this discussion in the next session.
