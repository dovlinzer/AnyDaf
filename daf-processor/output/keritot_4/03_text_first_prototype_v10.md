## Karet vs. Malkus



### Correction: Karet

Before proceeding into the new material, a correction from the previous session is in order. The position discussed there — *karet b'choteh* — was mischaracterized. That opinion, which we do not rule by, holds that for transgressions carrying *karet*, there is no concept of *beis din*-administered *malkus*; the punishment is *karet* alone. The position we do rule by is the opposite: one can receive *malkus*, and if one does, one is exempt from *karet*. These are distinct and should not be conflated.

Returning to the main thread: we were examining how many *chata'ot* one brings for various categories of transgression. For *Shabbos* and *avodah zarah*, one brings a single *chatas* if one erred about the fundamental prohibition, and separate *chata'ot* for each individual violation when one knew the general prohibition but forgot particular details. If one forgot *Shabbos* entirely and violated ten *melachos*, one brings only one *chatas*. If one knew about *Shabbos* but forgot that a particular *melacha* was forbidden, one brings a separate *chatas* for each. Analogously for *avodah zarah*: if one forgot the individual *avodos* — bowing, slaughtering, and so forth — one brings separately for each, but if one made a fundamental error about *avodah zarah* itself and performed multiple *avodos*, only one *chatas* is brought.

The *Gemara* had already asked how someone can make a fundamental error about *avodah zarah* while still performing an act of *avodah zarah*, and answered: *omer mutar* — one who considers it permitted. This renders even a categorical ignorance a *shogeg* sufficient for a *chatas*. The *Gemara* then raised the further question: if one forgot both the category and all the details, do we treat that as multiple errors or one? The ruling is that it constitutes one fundamental mistake, and only one *chatas* is brought.

### Tinok Shenishba

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב פָּפָּא אָמַר: מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ בְּתִינוֹק שֶׁנִּשְׁבָּה לְבֵין הַגּוֹיִם, דְּיָדַע דַּאֲסִירָא עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה, וְהָנֵי עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה לָא יָדַע דַּאֲסִירָן.
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa says: You find** an act performed unwittingly with regard to idol worship **in** the case of **a child who was taken captive among the gentiles** and never taught about the Torah, **as** he **knows that idol worship** in general is **prohibited, but** with regard to **these** particular objects of **idol worship** that he is worshipping, he does **not know that they are prohibited.**

The *Gemara* at the top of 3b continues within this vein. *Rav Pappa* offers an illustration: *mishkachat lah b'tinok shenishba l'vein ha'akum* — the paradigm case is that of an infant taken captive among non-Jews, someone who grew up not knowing he was Jewish. This is where one can genuinely not know the prohibition in the most fundamental sense. It is, in essence, an extreme form of *omer mutar*.

The *Gemara* notes that there is actually a debate elsewhere, in *Shabbos*, whether this constitutes *ones* or *shogeg*. The conclusion is *shogeg* in the sense that one must bring a *korban chatas*. This is striking, because *shogeg* ordinarily implies some degree of fault, and it is difficult to assign fault to someone raised from infancy with no knowledge of his Jewish identity. What this really points to is the principle that a *chatas* is not simply a guilt offering — it atones for the objective fact that a sin was committed. To the degree that one is an *ones* in the sense that a force external to oneself compelled the act entirely, one could argue it was not one's action at all. But when one is choosing and acting — even if entirely blameless — there is a dimension of the sin that requires atonement, and one brings a *chatas*. The case of *tinok shenishba*, by this reasoning, is enough of an act of doing to obligate a *chatas*.

### Omer Mutar

The *Gemara* then qualifies the case and distinguishes it from a straightforward *omer mutar*. The scenario is: *d'yada d'asira avodah zarah* — one knows that *avodah zarah* is forbidden (perhaps through the *sheva mitzvos bnei Noach*, which raises interesting questions if the person turns out to be Jewish), *v'hai avodah zarah lo yada d'asira* — but one did not know that this particular form of *avodah zarah* was forbidden. The *Gemara* imagines one who grew up knowing that worshipping the *Asherah* was forbidden but believing that the *Baal* was permitted, and who therefore worshipped the *Baal* through all its various *avodos*. That is a fundamental mistake even if one performed every possible *avoda*, and thus only one *chatas* is brought.

### Fundamental vs. Detail

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי בָּעֵית אֵימָא: אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא בְּגָדוֹל – כְּגוֹן דְּקָא טָעֵי בְּהָדֵין קְרָא: ״לֹא תַעֲשׂוּן אִתִּי אֱלֹהֵי כֶסֶף וֵאלֹהֵי זָהָב וְגוֹ׳״ סָבַר: כִּי אֲסִירָא הִשְׁתַּחֲוָאָה לַעֲבוֹדָה זָרָה דְּכֶסֶף וְזָהָב, אֲבָל דְּמִינֵי אַחֲרִינֵי שַׁרְיָא, דְּהַיְינוּ שִׁגְגַת עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה וּזְדוֹן עֲבוֹדוֹת.
> **Translation:** **And if you wish, say** instead: **You** may **even say** this ruling is stated **with regard to an adult,** i.e., one who was not captured by gentiles as a child, and it is speaking of a case **where he erred with regard to this verse: “You shall not make with Me gods of silver and gods of gold,** you shall not make for yourselves” (Exodus 20:20). This person **thinks** that **when bowing** to an idol is **prohibited** it applies only **to** an object of **idol worship** fashioned **of silver or gold; but** with regard to objects fashioned **of other substances,** such as wood or stone, it is **permitted. And that is** a case of an action that is **unwitting with regard to idol worship but intentional with regard to** the prohibited **rites.**

The *Gemara* also offers a different scenario not involving someone raised among non-Jews but an ordinary adult Jew who misread the verse: *lo ta'asuni iti elohei chesef ve-elohei zahav lo ta'asu lachem* — do not make gods of gold and silver. One might conclude from this that worship of idols made of other materials is permitted. That constitutes a fundamental mistake about the prohibition — *shegagat karkavan*, as the *Gemara* terms it — even though one performed individual *avodos* knowingly, since one believed those *avodos* directed at a non-gold, non-silver idol were permitted. Again, only one *chatas* is brought.

## Category Consolidation



### Rav Pappa

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב אִיקָא מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַב בִּיבִי אָמַר: תַּנָּא שֵׁם שַׁבָּת וְשֵׁם עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה קָתָנֵי. מִמַּאי? מִדְּקָתָנֵי: הַבָּא עַל אִשָּׁה וּבִתָּהּ וְעַל אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ, וְהָא יֵשׁ בִּתּוֹ מֵאֲנוּסָתוֹ, דְּלָא קָתָנֵי לַהּ!
> **Translation:** **Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika, said** a different interpretation of the mishna **in the name of Rav Beivai:** The ***tanna* teaches the** general **category of Shabbat and the** general **category of idol worship,** without mentioning the details of how many sin offerings one would be liable to bring for transgressing each prohibition. The Gemara asks: **From where** can one infer this is the case? **From** the fact **that** the mishna **teaches: One who engages in intercourse with a woman and her daughter, or with a married woman. But there is** the case of **one’s daughter from the woman he raped, which** the mishna **does not teach.** The case of a daughter from the woman one raped is included in the category of intercourse with a woman and her daughter, yet it is not taught explicitly. Evidently, it is included in the general category of intercourse with a woman and her daughter.

*Rav Pappa brei de-Rav Ika* then cites a ruling: *tanna shem Shabbos ve-shem avodat kochavim katani* — the *tanna* is listing the categories of *Shabbos* and *avodah zarah*, not trying to enumerate the maximum number of *chata'ot* that can be produced within each category. One should not be troubled by the fact that the *mishna* implies a single *chatas* for *Shabbos* when in reality thirty-nine *chata'ot* are possible, or implies a single *chatas* for *avodah zarah* when four or more are possible. The *mishna* is identifying categories, not maximizing counts. It is worth noting, though, that there is something conceptually distinctive about *Shabbos* and *avodah zarah* compared to *arayos*: in those two domains the underlying prohibition is a single *lav* — not to perform *melacha*, or not to be *oved avodah zarah* — and the *chiddush* the *mishna* conveys is precisely that performing that one prohibition through separate acts can generate separate *chata'ot*. With *arayos*, by contrast, the various forbidden relationships are themselves completely distinct prohibitions, not subdivisions of a single one, which is why the *mishna*'s consolidation there follows a somewhat different logic, as the *gemara* goes on to illustrate.

### Shabbat, Avodah Zarah

This principle is reinforced by a comparison to *arayos*. The *mishna* lists a woman and her daughter and an *eshet ish*, but it does not list every possible *chiyuv karet* within *arayos*. This demonstrates that the *mishna* does not aim to maximize the number of *chata'ot*.

### Arayot Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרִי: דִּכְתִיבָן קָתָנֵי, דְּלָא כְּתִיבָן לָא קָתָנֵי.
> **Translation:** The Sages **say** in response to this inference: The *tanna* of the mishna **teaches** those prohibitions **that are written** explicitly in the Torah; he **does not teach** those prohibitions **that are not written** explicitly but are derived from the verses. That is the reason he does not teach the case of intercourse with a daughter from the woman one raped; it is not because he is teaching only general categories.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָאִיכָּא בַּת אִשְׁתּוֹ וּבַת בִּתָּהּ וּבַת בְּנָהּ, דִּכְתִיבָא, וְלָא קָתָנֵי! אֶלָּא: שֵׁם אִשָּׁה וּבִתָּהּ קָתָנֵי? הָכִי נָמֵי: שֵׁם שַׁבָּת וְשֵׁם עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה קָתָנֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges this explanation: **But there is** the case of **one’s wife’s daughter, and the daughter of her daughter, and the daughter of her son, which are written** explicitly, **and** yet the *tanna* **does not teach** them. **Rather,** you must say that **he teaches the** general **category of** intercourse with **a woman and her daughter,** which includes those cases. **So too, he teaches the** general **category of Shabbat and the** general **category of idol worship.**

The *Gemara* asks why a man's biological daughter from a woman he assaulted is not listed, given that it carries its own *chiyuv karet*. One reason offered is that the daughter is not made explicit in the *pesukim* in the way that granddaughter is. A further answer: *shem isha u-vita katani* — the *mishna* consolidates everything into the category of a woman and her daughter. Even though there is a halachic distinction between a wife's daughter and a biological daughter from another woman, they belong to the same conceptual category, and the *mishna* consolidates them even at the cost of omitting additional *chata'ot* that could have been listed. Here too: *hacha nami shem Shabbos ve-shem avodah zarah katani* — the point in our *mishna* is likewise one of consolidation by category, not maximization.

## Shechutei Chutz



### Shechita, Ha'ala

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב אִיקָא רָמֵי דִּילֵיהּ אַדִּילֵיהּ: מִי אָמַר רַב בִּיבִי בַּר אַבָּיֵי הָכִי, שֵׁם שַׁבָּת קָתָנֵי וְשֵׁם עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה קָתָנֵי? וְהָאִיתְּמַר: הַמַּעֲלֶה אֵבְרֵי פְּנִים בַּחוּץ – חַיָּיב, אֵבְרֵי חוּץ בַּחוּץ – חַיָּיב.
> **Translation:** **Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika, raises a contradiction** between one statement **of** Rav Beivai bar Abaye **and** another statement **of his: Did Rav Beivai bar Abaye** actually **say this,** that the *tanna* **teaches the** general **category of Shabbat and teaches the** general **category of idol worship? But wasn’t it stated: One who offers up the limbs of** an offering slaughtered **inside** the Temple **outside** the Temple, i.e., he slaughtered the animal within the Sanctuary in accordance with *halakha* but sacrificed its limbs on an altar outside the Temple, he is **liable** to bring a sin offering if he did so unwittingly. Likewise, if he sacrifices **limbs of** an offering slaughtered **outside** the Temple **outside** the Temple, he is **liable.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְקַשְׁיָא לֵיהּ לְרַב בִּיבִי בַּר אַבָּיֵי: ״אִי הָכִי, הָא דִּתְנַן: שְׁלֹשִׁים וָשֵׁשׁ כָּרֵיתוֹת בְּתוֹרָה – תְּלָתִין וּשְׁבַע נִינְהוּ, דְּאִיכָּא הַמַּעֲלֶה וְהַמַּעֲלֶה!״ מַאי קַשְׁיָא לֵיהּ? נִישְׁנֵי: שֵׁם הַעֲלָאָה קָתָנֵי!
> **Translation:** **And Rav Beivai bar Abaye** raised **a difficulty: If so,** consider **that which we learned** in the mishna: There are **thirty-six** cases **in the Torah** with regard to which one who performs the action intentionally is liable to receive ***karet*. They are** actually **thirty-seven, as there is** the case of **one who offers up** the limbs of an offering slaughtered inside the Temple outside the Temple, **and one who offers up** limbs of an offering slaughtered outside the Temple outside the Temple. Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika, explains the contradiction: If Rav Beivai bar Abaye maintains that the *tanna* of the mishna does not tally all the relevant prohibitions but states general categories, **what** is **difficult to him? Let him answer** that **he teaches the category of offering up** in the clause: One who slaughters offerings and who sacrifices them outside the Temple. The *tanna* would not then need to list these individual transgressions.

*Rav Acha brei de-Rav Ika* points out an apparent contradiction in the positions of *Rav Bibi bar Abaye*. On the one hand, *Rav Bibi bar Abaye* himself said that our *mishna* consolidates categories and is not trying to maximize numbers. Yet there is a *braisa* that teaches: *ha-ma'aleh evarei pnim bachutz chayav, evarei chutz bachutz chayav* — one who brings up on an altar outside the *Beis HaMikdash* the limbs of an animal slaughtered inside is liable, and one who does the same with an animal slaughtered outside is also liable. These are two forms of the prohibition of *shechutei chutz*. *Rav Bibi bar Abaye* understood them to be distinct categories, each producing a separate *chatas*. But if that is so, then since the *mishna* lists 36 *kareisin*, it should have counted both forms — why list only one, given that they are distinct?

### Location Principle

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִי דָּמֵי? שַׁבָּת וַעֲבוֹדָה זָרָה תְּנָא יָתְהוֹן אַבִּינְכֵיהוֹן, גַּבַּי כָּרֵיתוֹת, דְּאִירְיָא, מְשַׁנֵּי: ״שֵׁם שַׁבָּת קָתָנֵי שֵׁם עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה קָתָנֵי״, גַּבֵּי הַעֲלָאָה, מִי תְּנָא יָתְהוֹן בִּינְכֵיהוֹן דְּשַׁנִּי הָכִי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **Are** these cases **comparable?** In the cases of **Shabbat and idol worship** the *tanna* **taught** their details **in their places [*abeinekheihon*],** on *Shabbat* 73a and *Sanhedrin* 60b, respectively. Consequently, **with regard to *karet*, where it is necessary** to mention them as part of a list of prohibitions that are punishable by *karet*, Rav Beivai bar Abaye **answers** that the *tanna* **teaches the category of Shabbat and teaches the category of idol worship,** but he did not need to specify the details in this manner. By contrast, **with regard to bringing up** an offering, **does** the *tanna* **teach them** in **their place,** so **that** Rav Beivai bar Abaye can **answer** in **this** manner?

The *Gemara* answers by drawing a distinction based on where these matters are elaborated. *Shabbos* and *avodah zarah* are discussed at length elsewhere — *Shabbos* has its own tractate and *avodah zarah* is elaborated in *Sanhedrin* — and therefore our *mishna* can consolidate without spelling out all the details, since the reader will encounter those details in their proper place. The laws of *shechutei chutz*, however, have no other dedicated place of discussion. If those two forms were genuinely distinct categories producing separate *chata'ot*, one would have expected them to be listed separately here in *Keritot*, which is precisely where that discussion belongs. The fact that the *mishna* does list both *ha-shocheit* and *ha-ma'aleh* separately confirms that this is the appropriate place to articulate any distinctions within that prohibition. The underlying principle is thus that consolidation is appropriate when elaboration will occur elsewhere; when a topic finds its primary home in *Keritot*, the *mishna* does provide separate entries.

## Two Karetin, One Lav



### Ein Onshin

*Rami bar Chama* posed a question to *Rav Chisda*: what is the rule when there are two *kareisin* and one *lav*? The preceding discussion had addressed the case of two *lavin* and one *karet* — the prohibition of preparing the anointing oil and anointing oneself with it, *mefatem ve-sach shemen hamishcha*, where two distinct *lavin* share a single explicit *karet*, and the ruling is that the two *lavin* generate separate *chata'ot*. What if the situation is reversed: a single prohibition but two explicit *kareisin*?

### Gezeirah Shavah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּעָא מִינֵּיהּ רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה מֵרַבִּי זֵירָא: שְׁתֵּי כָּרֵיתוֹת וְלָאו אֶחָד, מַהוּ? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: שׁוֹחֵט וְהֶעֱלָה קָאָמְרַתְּ? הָנֵי שְׁנֵי לָאוִין נִינְהוּ,
> **Translation:** § It was stated earlier that Rabbi Elazar says that Rabbi Oshaya says: Anywhere you find two different prohibitions but only one punishment of *karet* is stated for both of them you should divide the sin offering between them, i.e., if one transgressed both prohibitions unwittingly he is liable to bring a sin offering for each. In a similar vein, **Rabbi Yirmeya raised a dilemma before Rabbi Zeira:** If there are **two** punishments of ***karet* and** only **one prohibition** written with regard to a certain act, **what is** the *halakha*? Is one liable to bring a separate sin offering for each act? Rabbi Zeira **said to him: Are you saying** this in reference to one **who slaughters** offerings outside the Temple **and** one **who offered up** the limbs of offerings outside the Temple? These do not match your description, as **these are two** separate **prohibitions.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי לְמַאן דְּגָמַר מִגְּזֵרָה שָׁוָה – נֶאֱמַר כָּאן הֲבָאָה וְנֶאֱמַר לְהַלָּן הֲבָאָה,
> **Translation:** Rabbi Zeira elaborates: **If** one considers the matter **according to the one who derives** the prohibition **from a verbal analogy,** it is as though both prohibitions are written explicitly. As **it is stated here** an expression of **bringing:** “Or who slaughters it outside the camp, and to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting he did not bring it” (Leviticus 17:3–4), **and it is stated there** an expression of **bringing:** “Who sacrifices a burnt offering or sacrifice, and to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting he did not bring it” (Leviticus 17:8–9).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה לְהַלָּן לֹא עָנַשׁ אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן הִזְהִיר – אַף כָּאן לֹא עָנַשׁ, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן הִזְהִיר.
> **Translation:** Since there is a verbal analogy linking slaughtering an offering outside the Temple to sacrificing one outside the Temple, it follows that **just as there,** with regard to sacrificing, the Torah **did not** prescribe **punishment unless** it explicitly **prohibited** the act, as it is written: “Take heed to yourself, lest you sacrifice your burnt offerings in every place that you see” (Deuteronomy 12:13), **so too here,** with regard to slaughtering, the Torah **did not** prescribe **punishment unless** it explicitly **prohibited** the act as a separate prohibition.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְמַאן דְּמַיְיתֵי לַהּ בְּהֶיקֵּשׁ – אָמַר קְרָא: ״שָׁם תַּעֲלֶה... וְשָׁם תַּעֲשֶׂה״, מַקִּישׁ שְׁחִיטָה לְהַעֲלָאָה, מָה הַעֲלָאָה לֹא עָנַשׁ אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן הִזְהִיר, אַף שְׁחִיטָה לֹא עָנַשׁ אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן הִזְהִיר.
> **Translation:** If one considers the matter **according to the one who derives** the prohibition **from a juxtaposition,** it is also as though both prohibitions are written explicitly. The juxtaposition is that **the verse states: “There you shall sacrifice** your burnt offerings **and there you shall do** all that I command you” (Deuteronomy 12:14). Here the verse **juxtaposes slaughtering,** in the phrase: “And there you shall do,” **to offering up.** This teaches that **just as there,** with regard to offering up, the Torah **did not** prescribe **punishment unless** it explicitly **prohibited** the act, **so too here,** with regard to slaughtering, the Torah **did not** prescribe **punishment unless** it explicitly **prohibited** the act. Whether one derives the prohibition by verbal analogy or juxtaposition, it is considered as though the Torah explicitly wrote a prohibition with regard to slaughtering, and therefore this would not meet the criteria of Rabbi Yirmeya’s dilemma.

The question is initially deflected: *shocheit* and *ma'aleh* outside the *Beis HaMikdash* would seem to be the relevant example, but the *Gemara* points out that these actually carry two separate *lavin*, not one. The explicit prohibition — *hishamer lecha pen ta'aleh olotecha b'chol hamakom asher tir'eh* — mentions only *ha'ala*, the burning of limbs. How is *shechita* prohibited? One opinion derives it through a *gezeirah shavah*: both *shechita* outside the *Beis HaMikdash* and *ha'ala* outside employ the phrase *lo hevi'o* in the relevant *pesukim*. On the basis of *ein onshin ela im kein mazhirin* — the Torah will not impose a punishment without a prohibition — we must discover a prohibition for *shechita*. The *gezeirah shavah* supplies it: just as there is a prohibition for *ha'ala*, so there is a prohibition for *shechita*.

### Hekkesh

A second opinion derives the prohibition from a *hekkesh* rather than a *gezeirah shavah*. The verse states *sham ta'aleh ve-sham ta'aseh* — "there shall you offer up and there shall you do," with the latter phrase encompassing *shechita* and other acts of the sacrificial service. This comparison (*hekkesh*) between *shechita* and *ha'ala* means that just as there is an explicit prohibition for *ha'ala*, so too for *shechita*. By either derivation — *gezeirah shavah* or *hekkesh* — *shechita* outside the *Beis HaMikdash* carries its own distinct *lav*, and the case of two *kareisin* with only one *lav* remains without a clear example.

## Ov and Yidoni



### One Lav, Separate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּלְמָא שְׁתֵּי מִיתוֹת וְלָאו אֶחָד קָאָמְרַתְּ? מָה הִיא – אוֹב וְיִדְּעוֹנִי.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Zeira continues to address Rabbi Yirmeya: **Perhaps you are stating** your dilemma with regard to **two** prohibitions punishable by the **death** penalty **and** only **one prohibition** is written with regard to them, and your dilemma is whether one brings two sin offerings if one transgressed both unwittingly, despite the fact that only one prohibition is written with regard to both acts. **What is** such a circumstance? This applies in the case of **a necromancer,** who raises up the spirit of the dead, **and a sorcerer,** who places a certain bone in his mouth for it to speak of its own accord (see Leviticus 20:6, 27).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ דְּהָא פְּלוּגְתָּא דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ. דְּתָנוּ גַּבֵּי סַנְהֶדְרִין: בַּעַל אוֹב וְיִדְּעוֹנִי, וְקַשְׁיָא לַן, וּמַאי שְׁנָא גַּבֵּי סְקִילוֹת תָּנֵי יִדְּעוֹנִי, וְגַבֵּי כָּרֵיתוֹת לָא תָּנֵי יִדְּעוֹנִי?
> **Translation:** Rabbi Zeira further **said to** Rabbi Yirmeya: This should not be a dilemma, **as this** matter is **a dispute between Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish. As** the Sages **taught** a *baraita*, where they list those liable to be executed by stoning, **in** tractate ***Sanhedrin*** (53a): **A necromancer and a sorcerer, and** the following presents **a difficulty to us: And what is different with regard to stonings** that the *tanna* **teaches** the case of **a sorcerer, but** the *tanna* **does not teach** the case of **a sorcerer with regard to** the list of transgressions punishable by ***karet*,** thereby indicating that one is liable to bring only a single sin offering for both transgressions?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: הוֹאִיל וּשְׁנֵיהֶם בְּלָאו אֶחָד נֶאֶמְרוּ. וְנֵימָא יִדְּעוֹנִי, וְלָא נֵימָא בַּעַל אוֹב! קָסָבַר: הוֹאִיל וּפָתַח הַכָּתוּב בְּבַעַל אוֹב.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** The *tanna* in tractate *Karetot* does not count the case of the sorcerer on its own **since both** the sorcerer and the necromancer **were stated in one prohibition:** “Do not turn to the necromancers and the sorcerers” (Leviticus 19:31); therefore, there is no separate obligation to bring an offering for each of them. The Gemara clarifies: **And let** the *tanna* **say** the case of **a sorcerer and let him not say** the case of **a necromancer,** rather than the reverse. The Gemara explains: The *tanna* **maintains** that **since the verse opened with** the case of **a necromancer,** it is proper to list that transgression and omit the case of a sorcerer.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ אָמַר: הוֹאִיל וְאֵין בּוֹ מַעֲשֶׂה. וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ מַאי טַעְמָא לָא אָמַר כְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן?
> **Translation:** **And Reish Lakish says:** The case of the sorcerer is not included in the list of transgressions punishable by *karet* **since it does not involve an action,** but only speech, through the use of a bone, and one does not bring a sin offering for a prohibition that does not involve an action. The Gemara asks: **And** as for **Reish Lakish, what is the reason he does not state** an explanation **in accordance with** the explanation of **Rabbi Yoḥanan?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: דְּהָא חֲלוּקִין הֵן בְּמִיתוֹת. וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר לְךָ: חֲלוּקָּה דְלָאו – הָוֵי חֲלוּקָּה, חֲלוּקָּה דְמִיתָה – לָא הָוְיָא חֲלוּקָּה.
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa said:** Reish Lakish disagrees with the explanation of Rabbi Yoḥanan **because** the cases of a necromancer and a sorcerer **are divided** in the verse **with regard to** the issue of the **death** penalty, i.e., one is liable to receive the death penalty for transgressing either prohibition, as it is stated: “And a man also or a woman, who are a necromancer or a sorcerer shall be put to death” (Leviticus 20:27). Consequently, one would be liable to bring a separate sin offering for the unwitting transgression of either prohibition were it not for the fact that sorcery does not involve an action. **And Rabbi Yoḥanan** could **say to you** that **a division of a prohibition is** considered **a division** that leads to separate sin offerings, whereas **a division of** separate **death** penalties **is not** considered **a division** in this regard.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן מַאי טַעְמָא לָא אָמַר כְּרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ? קָסָבַר: מַאן תַּנָּא כָּרֵיתוֹת – רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא הִיא, דְּאָמַר לָא בָּעִינַן מַעֲשֶׂה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara further asks: **And** as for **Rabbi Yoḥanan, what is the reason he does not state** an explanation **in accordance with** the explanation of **Reish Lakish,** who says the prohibition with regard to a sorcerer is not counted because it does not involve an action? The Gemara answers: Rabbi Yoḥanan **maintains: Who** is the *tanna* who **taught** the list of those liable to receive ***karet*? It is Rabbi Akiva, who says: An action is not required** for one to be liable to bring a sin offering.

The *Gemara* then considers a parallel case involving not *karet* but two separate *mitos*: the prohibitions of *ov* and *yidoni*, two forms of necromancy. The verse *lo yimatze vecha shoel ov ve-yidoni* contains a single prohibition covering both. Yet the verse *ov o yidoni mos yumas* — because it says "A or B shall be put to death" — is understood to impose a separate death penalty on each. So we have one *lav* and two *mitos*. If one performed both in a single state of forgetfulness, does one bring two *chata'ot*? This is the unresolved form of the question: one prohibition, two separate punishments.

There is, however, a further textual observation that sharpens the question. Our *mishna* lists only *ov* and not *yidoni*, while the *mishna* in *Sanhedrin* in the chapter of *haniskalin* lists both *ba'al ov* and *yidoni* together as carrying *sekila*. This asymmetry is itself suggestive: the reason *yidoni* is omitted here is precisely because both fall under a single *lav*, and therefore there is only one *karet* and only one *korban* — so our *mishna*, which is organizing categories for the purpose of *chatas*, has no reason to list them separately. But this answer, it turns out, is itself the subject of a *machlokes*, and the *Gemara* now attributes that debate to *Rabbi Yochanan* and *Resh Lakish* — a question the *Gemara* will resolve in the section that follows.

## Ov and Yidoni (II)



### Rabbi Yochanan

*Rabbi Yochanan* holds: *hen u-shneihen be-lav echad ne'emru* — both *ov* and *yidoni* were stated within a single prohibition, and therefore even though there are two separate *mitos*, only one *chatas* is brought. The principle as *Rabbi Yochanan* understands it is that it is the *lav* that defines the category for the purpose of *chatas*. Two separate *lavin* with different names generate separate *chata'ot*, as in the case of *mefatem ve-sach*; a single *lav* with two punishments does not.

### Reish Lakish

*Reish Lakish* disagrees. He holds that the fact that each — *ov* and *yidoni* — carries its own distinct death penalty is sufficient to render them separate categories, such that performing both in one state of forgetfulness would in principle require two *chata'ot*. The debate thus crystallizes: according to *Rabbi Yochanan*, the unit of analysis for *chatas* is the *lav*; according to *Reish Lakish*, a separate punishment, even under a shared *lav*, constitutes a separate category.

The *Gemara* also notes that our *mishna* lists only *ov* and not *yidoni*. *Rabbi Yochanan* explains simply: since it is one *lav* and only one *chatas* is brought, the *mishna* lists one entry, and it chose *ov* because that is the first in the *pasuk*. *Reish Lakish* offers a different explanation, which carries a distinct halachic implication.

## Ma'aseh Requirement



### Yidoni Excluded

*Reish Lakish* explains why *yidoni* is absent from the *mishna*: *hoil ve-ein bo ma'aseh* — because it involves no action. The *yidoni* practice involves placing a bone in one's mouth and somehow producing sounds through it; one performs no meaningful physical act. Since there is a requirement of *ma'aseh* — an act — for the obligation of *chatas*, and *yidoni* lacks one, it is excluded from the *mishna* regardless of whether one *lav* or two are involved.

### Akiva Positions

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ? נְהִי דְּרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא לָא בָּעֵי מַעֲשֵׂה רַבָּה, מַעֲשֵׂה זוּטָא בָּעֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** as for **Reish Lakish,** how does he respond to this claim? He could say that **although Rabbi Akiva does not require a significant action** for one to be liable to bring a sin offering, nevertheless he **does require a minor action.** The transgression of a sorcerer involves no action, as the voice emerges of its own accord, and therefore even Rabbi Akiva would not hold one liable to bring a sin offering for the unwitting violation of this prohibition.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בַּעַל אוֹב, מַאי מַעֲשֶׂה אִית בֵּיהּ? הֲקָשַׁת זְרוֹעוֹתָיו הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה. מְגַדֵּף, מַאי מַעֲשֶׂה אִית בֵּיהּ? עֲקִימַת שְׂפָתָיו הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: In the case of **a necromancer, what action is there in** this transgression? The Gemara answers: **The striking of his arms** against each other to raise the spirit of the dead **is** considered **an action.** The Gemara further asks: In the case of one who **blasphemes,** who merely speaks, **what action is there in** this transgression? Since Rabbi Akiva holds that an unwitting blasphemer is liable to bring a sin offering, this apparently indicates that no action at all is necessary. The Gemara answers: **The twisting of his lips** while he speaks **is** considered **an action.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָא סָלְקָא דַּעְתִּין: הֲקָשַׁת זְרוֹעוֹתָיו הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה זוּטָא אֲפִילּוּ לְרַבָּנַן. מֵיתִיבִי: בַּעֲבוֹדָה זָרָה אֵינוֹ חַיָּיב אֶלָּא עַל דָּבָר שֶׁיֵּשׁ בּוֹ מַעֲשֶׂה, כְּגוֹן שֶׁזִּיבַּח, וְקִיטֵּר, וְנִיסֵּךְ, וְהִשְׁתַּחֲוָה. וְקַשְׁיָא לַן, הִשְׁתַּחֲוָאָה לֵית בַּהּ מַעֲשֶׂה!
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: It **enters our mind** that **the striking of** the necromancer’s **arms** against each other **is** considered **a minor action even according to** the opinion of **the Rabbis,** and they would deem him liable. The Gemara **raises an objection** from a *baraita*: **With regard to idol worship, one is liable** to be punished **only for a matter that involves an action, for example where one slaughtered** an offering, **or burned** incense, **or poured** a libation, **or bowed. And** the following matter poses **a difficulty to us: Bowing does not involve an action.**

This explanation generates a question for *Rabbi Yochanan*: if *Rabbi Yochanan* holds that no action is required, how can *Reish Lakish's* reasoning hold? *Rabbi Yochanan's* response is: *man tanna Keritot Rabbi Akiva hi de-amar lo ba'inan ma'aseh* — the *tanna* of our *mishna* in *Keritot* is *Rabbi Akiva*, who holds no action is required, as evidenced by the fact that the *mishna* includes *megadef* — blasphemy — which involves no act in the conventional sense. If even *megadef* brings a *chatas*, one cannot exclude *yidoni* on the grounds of lacking an act. According to *Rabbi Yochanan*, therefore, *yidoni* is excluded solely because it is subsumed under one *lav* with *ov*, and only one *chatas* is brought.

This raises an interesting conceptual question about *Rabbi Akiva's* position: when he rules that *megadef* brings a *chatas*, is he denying that any act is needed, or is he saying that speech itself constitutes an act? The distinction matters, because *Reish Lakish* reads *Rabbi Akiva* as taking the latter position — *ma'aseh zuta ba'ei*, a minimal act is sufficient, and moving one's lips is precisely that kind of minimal act. On this reading, *Rabbi Akiva* never abandons the requirement of an act; he merely sets the threshold low enough to include verbal acts. *Yidoni*, in which no movement of the lips occurs and no comparable minimal act is performed, falls below even that threshold — a point the *gemara* will develop further as it probes what exactly counts as an act.

## Ma'aseh Requirement (II)



### Minimal Action

*Rav Pappa* notes that regardless of the debate about *chatas*, both *karet* and *misa* can be incurred without an act — there are cases of *chiyuv misa* for which everyone agrees, yet a *chatas* is still not brought. The requirement of *ma'aseh* is specific to *chatas*. It is, as the *Gemara* observes, somewhat counterintuitive that one can receive the most severe punishments without an act, but not bring a *chatas* without one.

*Reish Lakish* responds to the question of why he does not follow *Rabbi Yochanan*: *de-ha chalukin hen be-mitoséihen* — because the two forms carry distinct deaths, he regards them as distinct categories. His response to the *ma'aseh* question is different from *Rabbi Yochanan's*: *Rav Pappa* clarifies that *Reish Lakish* holds *Rabbi Akiva* does require some form of action, but sets the threshold very low — *ma'aseh zuta ba'ei*, a minimal act is needed. Moving the lips constitutes such an act. *Yidoni*, in which nothing of that kind occurs, falls below even *Rabbi Akiva's* minimal threshold, which is why even according to the *tanna* of our *mishna* it produces no *chatas*.

## What Is Action



### Ba'al Ov

The *Gemara* then examines what constitutes a *ma'aseh* in each relevant case. By *ba'al ov*: *hakashat zero'otav havei ma'aseh* — the flapping or tensing of the arms in the conjuring act is considered an action. That is a clear enough physical movement that all opinions can agree it constitutes an act.

By *megadef*: *akimat sefatav havei ma'aseh* — moving one's lips is considered an action. This is *Rabbi Akiva's* position, or, as *Reish Lakish* frames it, the basis on which *Rabbi Akiva* holds that a minimal act suffices.

### Hishtachava'ah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ: הָא מַנִּי? רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא הִיא, דְּאָמַר לָא בָּעִינַן מַעֲשֶׂה. וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא רַבָּנַן, כְּפִיפַת קוֹמָתוֹ הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה. מִכְּלָל דִּסְבִירָא לֵיהּ לְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ אַלִּיבָּא דְרַבָּנַן כְּפִיפַת קוֹמָתוֹ לְרַבָּנַן לָא הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה. הֲקָשַׁת זְרוֹעוֹתָיו הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה?!
> **Translation:** **And Reish Lakish said:** In accordance with **whose** opinion **is this** *baraita*? **It is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Akiva, who says: An action is not required** for one to be liable to bring a sin offering for transgressing a prohibition. **And Rabbi Yoḥanan said: You** may **even say** that the *baraita* is in accordance with the opinion of **the Rabbis,** as **the bending of his height** when he bows **is** considered **an action.** One can conclude from here **by inference that Reish Lakish maintains** that **according to** the opinion **of the Rabbis, the bending of his height is not** considered **an action.** If so, can it be said that **the striking of his arms** against each other, which is less of a movement than bending one’s height, **is** considered **an action?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֶלָּא כִּי קָאָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ הֲקָשַׁת זְרוֹעוֹתָיו הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה זוּטָא, אַלִּיבָּא דְּרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא, אֲבָל לְרַבָּנַן לָא הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה.
> **Translation:** **But rather,** you must explain that **when Reish Lakish says the striking of his arms** against each other **is** considered **a minor action,** he is speaking only **in accordance with** the opinion **of Rabbi Akiva; but according to** the opinion **of the Rabbis** it **is not** considered **an action.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, אַמַּאי תָּנֵי: יָצָא מְגַדֵּף שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ מַעֲשֶׂה? נִיתְנֵי: יָצָא מְגַדֵּף וּבַעַל אוֹב! חֲדָא מִתַּרְתֵּי קָתָנֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **If so,** that the Rabbis hold that the necromancer does not perform an action, **why** does the mishna **teach** that the Rabbis say: The list in the mishna is **excluding** one who **blasphemes, as he does not perform an action,** but rather he sins with speech. **Let it teach** instead: The list in the mishna is **excluding** one who **blasphemes and a necromancer.** The Gemara answers: The *tanna* **teaches one of two** examples that the Rabbis could have mentioned.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִיתְנֵי בַּעַל אוֹב וְלָא לִיתְנֵי מְגַדֵּף! מְגַדֵּף אִיצְטְרִיךְ לֵיהּ, סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ אָמֵינָא: הוֹאִיל וּכְתִיב כָּרֵת דִּילֵיהּ בִּמְקוֹם קׇרְבָּן, אֵימָא מוֹדֶה לֵיהּ לְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן דְּלָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara questions this explanation: **But** if that is so, **let** the mishna **teach** the statement of the Rabbis with regard to **a necromancer and let it not teach** the statement of the Rabbis with regard to one who **blasphemes.** The Gemara answers: It **was necessary for** the *tanna* to mention one who **blasphemes,** as it might **enter your mind to say: Since its** punishment of ***karet* is written in the place** where the verse discusses **an offering** (see Numbers 15:28–30), one might **say** that in this case the Rabbis **concede to Rabbi Akiva** that one is liable to bring a sin offering despite the fact that no action is involved. To counter this, the mishna specifically mentions the exclusion of one who blasphemes to **teach us that** this is **not** the case.

The *Gemara* raises a challenge from a separate discussion in *Sanhedrin*: by *avodah zarah*, one is only liable if one performs an act — *kegon shezivach ve-kiter ve-nisach ve-hishtachavah*. Among these, *hishtachavayah* — bowing down — is listed as an act. But the *Gemara* asks: is bowing down really an act? One is simply moving one's body, not interacting with anything in the physical world. The same conceptual question applies to flapping one's arms and moving one's lips.

*Reish Lakish* answers: *ha mani Rabbi Akiva hi de-amar lo ba'inan ma'aseh* — including *hishtachavayah* as an act reflects *Rabbi Akiva's* position, which accepts minimal action. *Rabbi Yochanan* disagrees and says: *afilu teima Rabanan, kfifas komaso havei ma'aseh* — even the *Rabbanan* would agree that bowing one's entire body is an act, even if they would not accept merely moving the lips. The degree of full-body physical movement crosses a threshold that lip-movement does not.

This generates a further inference. According to *Reish Lakish*, who says bowing is only *Rabbi Akiva's* standard and not the *Rabbanan's*, flapping the arms should also fall below the *Rabbanan's* threshold — and therefore the *Rabbanan* should have explicitly disagreed with the inclusion of *ba'al ov* in the *mishna*, just as they disagree with *megadef*. The *Gemara* answers: they do — *chada mi-tartei katani*, the *Rabbanan* only specified one objection, but their reasoning applies equally to both *megadef* and *ba'al ov*.

## Ba'al Ov Incense



### Ulla Position

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עוּלָּא אָמַר: בַּעַל אוֹב דְּקָתָנֵי בִּמְקַטֵּר לַשֵּׁד. מַתְקֵיף לַהּ רָבָא: אִם כֵּן, הַיְינוּ עוֹבֵד עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה! אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא: מְקַטֵּר לַשֵּׁד עַל מְנָת לְחַבְּרוֹ.
> **Translation:** **Ulla said:** The reason the Rabbis did not state: Excluding one who blasphemes and a necromancer, is that the action of the **necromancer that** the mishna **teaches** is referring **to** one who **burns** incense **to a demon** in order to raise the dead, which is a full-fledged action. **Rava objects to this** answer from Ulla: **If so,** i.e., if the necromancer burns incense to a demon, **this is** the same as **an idol worshipper,** which is already mentioned in the mishna. **Rather, Rava said:** The mishna is referring to one who **burns** incense **to a demon in order to gather it** together with other demons, i.e., he does not burn incense to a demon by means of a rite but in a manner of sorcery, to bring the demons to one place to then make use of them.

*Ulla* offers an entirely different account of the *ba'al ov* act. He says: *ba'al ov de-katni be-makter le-shed* — the *ba'al ov* in the *mishna* refers specifically to one who burns incense to invoke a spirit. This is not the flapping of the arms but a real, concrete action: the burning of incense. If so, the question of whether minimal physical movement constitutes an act becomes irrelevant for *ba'al ov* — it involves a genuine material act.

### Rava Response

*Rava* challenges: if one is burning incense to a spirit, is that not simply *avodah zarah*? *Rava* answers his own question: *mekater le-shed al menat le-chavro* — one is not burning incense to the spirit as an act of worship but as a means of invoking it, of making contact with or exercising control over the spirit world. The intent is not worship but invocation. That distinction takes it outside the category of *avodah zarah*.

### Chover Chaver

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי: אִם כֵּן, הָיִינוּ חוֹבֵר חָבֶר! אֲמַר לֵיהּ: הַתּוֹרָה אָמְרָה חוֹבֵר חָבֶר כְּגוֹן זֶה, בִּסְקִילָה. וְאֶלָּא חוֹבֵר חֶבֶר דִּבְלָאו – הֵדֵין הוּא?
> **Translation:** **Abaye said to** Rava: **If so, this is** the same as **a *ḥover ḥever*,** one who gathers together, which is stated in the Torah as a separate prohibition (Deuteronomy 18:11) and which is a prohibition that does not carry the punishment of *karet*. Rava **said to him:** It is true that such an individual is also a *ḥover ḥever*, but **the Torah stated: A *ḥover ḥever* such as this,** who gathers demons together, is included in the category of a necromancer and therefore his punishment is death **by stoning,** and transgressing this prohibition also results in *karet*. Abaye inquired: **But** then the case of **a *ḥover ḥever*, which** is **a prohibition** that does not entail *karet*, **what** is **it?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: כִּדְתַנְיָא: ״וְחוֹבֵר חָבֶר״ – אֶחָד חוֹבֵר גָּדוֹל וְאֶחָד חוֹבֵר קָטָן, וַאֲפִילּוּ חוֹבֵר לִנְחָשִׁים וְעַקְרַבִּים – חַיָּיב. אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: הַאי מַאן דְּבָעֵי לְמִיצְמַד זִיבּוּרָא וְעַקְרַבָּא – אָסוּר, וְאִי קָאָתוּ בָּתְרֵיהּ – שְׁרֵי.
> **Translation:** Rava **said to** Abaye: **As it is taught** in a *baraita* with regard to the verse: **“And a *ḥover ḥaver*”** (Deuteronomy 18:11), this is referring to both **one** who uses sorcery and thereby **gathers large** beasts into one place, **and** also to **one** who **gathers small** beasts into one place, **and even** to one who **gathers** into one place **snakes and scorpions;** all of these individuals are **liable.** With regard to this prohibition **Abaye says:** In the case of **this one, who** through sorcery **wants to gather** together **a hornet and a scorpion,** this is **prohibited. But if they are pursuing him** and he merely seeks to defend himself, it is **permitted.**

*Abaye* presses: if burning incense to invoke a spirit is not *avodah zarah*, then it is *chover chaver* — invoking spirits — which is a separate prohibition, a *lav*, not a capital offense. *Abaye* asks why the *Gemara* treats it as producing *chiyuv misa*. The response is: the Torah treats this particular form of *ba'al ov* — invoking through incense — as carrying *sekila*, not merely a *lav*. The general category of *chover chaver*, which encompasses invoking small spirits, even gathering snakes and scorpions through incantation, carries only a *lav*. But the specific act of the *ba'al ov* in this form is treated more severely.

*Abaye* further elaborates: *hai man de-ba'ei le-mitzmed diburei ve-akraba* — one who seeks to gather bees or scorpions through invocation is forbidden (*chover chaver*). However, if they are coming toward one unprovoked and one uses such means to protect oneself, that may be permitted — either because it falls under *pikuach nefesh* permitting a *lav*, or because the Torah prohibited *chover chaver* in the context of active invocation for one's benefit, not passive self-defense, and the purpose defines the act.

## What Is Action (II)



### Megadef

Returning to *Rabbi Yochanan's* position, the *Gemara* presses: if *Rabbi Yochanan* holds that bowing down is an act that everyone agrees to, why is moving one's lips not equally an act for everyone? Both are purely physical movements without interaction with the external world.

*Rava* answers: *shani megadef ho'il ve-yeshno ba-lev* — *megadef* is fundamentally different because it is in the heart. The curse is not essentially about the physical movement of the lips; it is about the expression of a thought, an attitude toward God. As *Rashi* notes in *Sanhedrin*, if one happens to utter the words of *giduf* without intending them — without understanding what one is saying — it would not constitute an act of *megadef*. The physical utterance is merely the vehicle for an internal expression. Bowing down, by contrast, is inherently about the physical act of prostration; the act itself is what it is, regardless of what passes through one's mind while doing it. The contrast between *megadef* and *hishtachavayah* is therefore not one of magnitude of physical movement, but of what is fundamentally constitutive of each act.

### Eidim Zomemim

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן, דְּאָמַר כְּפִיפַת קוֹמָתוֹ הָוֵי מַעֲשֵׂה, עֲקִימַת
> **Translation:** The Gemara objects: **According to** the opinion of **Rabbi Yoḥanan, who says the bending of one’s height is** considered **an action, the twisting of**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שְׂפָתָיו נֶהֱוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה גַּבֵּי מְגַדֵּף? אָמַר רָבָא: שָׁאנֵי מְגַדֵּף, הוֹאִיל וְיֶשְׁנוֹ בַּלֵּב, אֲבָל בְּעָלְמָא עֲקִימַת שְׂפָתָיו הָוֵי מַעֲשֶׂה.
> **Translation:** **his lips should be** considered **an action in** the case of one who **blasphemes. Rava said:** The case of one who **blasphemes is different, since it is** primarily **in the heart.** In other words, the transgression of blasphemy is not the actual speech but the verbalizing of a sentiment. One is not liable to bring a sin offering for such an action, as it is essentially a matter of the heart. **But in general, the twisting of one’s lips is** considered **an action.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵתִיב רַבִּי זֵירָא: יָצְאוּ עֵדִים זוֹמְמִין שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ מַעֲשֶׂה, וְאַמַּאי? הָא ״עַל פִּי״ כְּתִיב בָּהֶן! אָמַר רָבָא: שָׁאנֵי עֵדִים זוֹמְמִין, הוֹאִיל וְיֶשְׁנָן בִּרְאִיָּיה.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira raises an objection** from that which is taught in a different context. It is stated in a *baraita* that all who unwittingly transgress prohibitions punishable by death are liable to bring sin offerings, **except for conspiring witnesses,** who are not obligated to bring sin offerings, **as** their transgression **does not involve an action. But why** is that so? **It is written with regard to** such witnesses: **“At the mouth** of two witnesses” (Deuteronomy 17:6). They acted through speech, and the twisting of their lips should be considered an action, as they are liable for what they actually said, not for what was in their hearts. **Rava said:** The case of **conspiring witnesses is different, since their** transgression **is** primarily **through sight,** i.e., the important part of their testimony is what they saw, which is not an action.

The *Gemara* cites a further challenge: we find elsewhere that *eidim zomemim* — false witnesses — are described as an act *she-ein bahem ma'aseh*, lacking an act. Yet they speak. If *Rabbi Yochanan* says moving one's lips is an act, why is testifying falsely not considered an act?

*Rava* answers: *shani eidim zomemim ho'il ve-yeshnam bi-re'iyah* — false testimony is fundamentally about what one saw, about an act of *re'iyah*, of seeing and bearing witness. It is the attestation of an observed fact that constitutes the substance of testimony, not the physical act of speaking. What makes this answer striking is that the *eidim zomemim* in question are, by definition, witnesses who did not actually see anything — they are fabricating. And yet *Rava* characterizes their act as being essentially about *re'iyah*. This implies that testimony is defined by what it purports to express — a claim about what was perceived — rather than by the physical utterance through which it is delivered. In that sense, false testimony runs parallel to *megadef*: in both cases, the physical movement of the lips is merely the vehicle for an underlying content, an idea or claim being projected into the world, and it is that underlying content, not the bodily movement, that is legally constitutive of the act.

The *Gemara* adds that *Rav Hai Gaon*, according to one reading, understood this line differently, with the *girsa* reading *bi-i'ah* rather than *bi-re'iyah* — and his interpretation was that one witness can fulfill the act of false testimony by merely nodding assent while the other speaks, making no movement of the lips at all. Since testimony can be completed without even moving one's lips, it is categorized as *ein bo ma'aseh*.

The discussion across these sugyas thus coheres around a single conceptual axis: the question of what constitutes an act for the purpose of *chiyuv chatas*, how minimal that act can be, and when an apparently physical movement is rendered legally non-corporeal because the essence of the transgression lies in thought, speech, or attestation rather than in a deed in the world.

## Ochel Chelev



### Multiple Lashes

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָאוֹכֵל חֵלֶב. תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ״ – לְחַיֵּיב עַל כׇּל אַחַת וְאַחַת, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: אֵינוֹ חַיָּיב אֶלָּא אַחַת.
> **Translation:** § The mishna included in its list of those liable to receive *karet* **one who eats** forbidden **fat.** With regard to this, **the Sages taught** a *baraita* which deals with the verse: **“You shall eat no fat of ox, or sheep, or goat”** (Leviticus 7:23). This verse serves **to render one liable** to receive lashes **for each and every one,** i.e., one who eats the fat of an ox, and a sheep, and a goat is liable to receive three sets of lashes. This is **the statement of Rabbi Yishmael. And the Rabbis say he is liable** to receive **only one** set of lashes.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נֵימָא בְּהָא קָמִיפַּלְגִי, דְּרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל סָבַר: לוֹקִין עַל לָאו שֶׁבִּכְלָלוֹת, וְרַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: אֵין לוֹקִין עַל לָאו שֶׁבִּכְלָלוֹת?
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Let us say** that Rabbi Yishmael and the Rabbis **disagree about this** matter, **that Rabbi Yishmael maintains one is flogged for** violating **a general prohibition and the Rabbis maintain one is not flogged for** violating **a general prohibition.** This is referring to the violation of a prohibition that includes several different actions, such as this one, which pertains to eating the fat of an ox, sheep, and goat. The Rabbis contend that one does not receive multiple sets of lashes for transgressing each element of such a prohibition.

A *beraisa* is cited: *kol chelev shor ve-chesev ve-ez lo tocheilu* — do not eat any fat of ox, sheep, or goat. The question is whether this verse imposes a single prohibition on eating forbidden fat generically, or whether the separate mention of each animal creates distinct prohibitions. *Rabbi Yishmael* holds: *le-chayev al kol achas ve-achas* — one is liable separately for each animal, producing separate punishments. *Ve-chachamim omrim eino chayav ela achas* — the Sages say there is only one liability.

One might assume the debate turns on the broader principle of *lav she-bichlalos* — whether a prohibition covering multiple items generates separate *malkus* for each. But the *Gemara* clarifies that this is in fact a *malkus* question, not a *chatas* question, and the dispute is more specific. The scenario is one in which a person was warned separately about each animal — this fat is *chelev shor*, this is *chelev chesev*, this is *chelev ez* — and then ate all three. The question is not whether a *lav* that is too generic to be actionable produces *malkus* at all, which is the usual framing of *lav she-bichlalos*, but rather whether a *lav* that explicitly enumerates distinct items produces a separate *malkus* for each. *Neima beha kamifligi* — perhaps this is the axis of their dispute: *Rabbi Yishmael savar lokin al lav shebichlalos*, that one does receive *malkus* for each item within a collective prohibition, while *Rabbanan savri ein lokin al lav shebichlalos*, that one does not. The *Gemara*, however, pushes back on this framing, and the resolution of exactly what *Rabbi Yishmael* holds is taken up immediately after.

### Yishmael Argument

*Rabbi Yishmael's* reasoning: had the Torah intended merely to prohibit eating fat with no special significance to the list of animals, it could have written simply *kol chelev lo tocheilu*. Why specify ox, sheep, and goat? *Shema mina le-challek* — the specification must be to tell us that each constitutes a distinct prohibition, and one who eats all three in one state of forgetfulness receives separate punishments for each.

### Sages Argument

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְעוֹלָם סָבַר רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל: אֵין לוֹקִין עַל לָאו שֶׁבִּכְלָלוֹת, וְשָׁאנֵי הָכָא, דִּמְיַיתְּרִי לֵיהּ קְרָאֵי – נִכְתּוֹב קְרָא ״כׇּל חֵלֶב לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ״, ״שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״ לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לְחַלֵּק.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Actually, Rabbi Yishmael** also **maintains** that **one is not flogged for** violating **a general prohibition, but here it is different, as** elements of **the verse are superfluous.** The Gemara explains: **Let the verse write** only: **You shall eat no fat,** and all the individual types of fat would be included. **Why do I** need the additional terms: **“Ox, or sheep, or goat”? Learn from it** that the verse serves **to separate** between them and render one liable to receive a separate set of lashes for eating each type of forbidden fat.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן? אִי לָא כְּתִיב: ״שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״ הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: אֲפִילּוּ חֵלֶב חַיָּה בַּמַּשְׁמָע, לְהָכִי כְּתַב: ״שׁוֹר כֶּשֶׂב וָעֵז״, לְמֵימְרָא: דְּחֵלֶב שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז הוּא דְּאָסוּר, אֲבָל דְּחַיָּה שְׁרֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** as for **the Rabbis,** how do they respond to this reading of the verse? The Gemara answers: The Rabbis would say that no element of the verse is superfluous, since **if** it had **not written: “Ox, or sheep, or goat,” I would say** that **even** the **fat of an undomesticated animal is included** in the prohibition. **For this** reason the verse **writes: “Ox, or sheep, or goat,” to say that it is** the **fat of an ox, or a sheep, or a goat that is forbidden, but** all the fats **of an undomesticated animal are permitted.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שַׁפִּיר קָאָמְרִי לֵיהּ! אֶלָּא הַיְינוּ טַעְמָא, דְּרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל דְּקָסָבַר: אִם כֵּן, לִכְתּוֹב ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר לֹא תֹאכְלוּ״, ״כֶּשֶׂב וָעֵז״ לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לְחַלֵּק.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty against this interpretation of the dispute: The Rabbis **spoke well to** Rabbi Yishmael, i.e., their response is persuasive. The Gemara suggests an alternative explanation: **Rather, this is the reasoning of Rabbi Yishmael, as he holds: If so,** that the verse serves to exclude the fat of only an undomesticated animal from the prohibition, then **let it write: You shall eat no fat of an ox,** which would be understood as a paradigm representing every type of domesticated animal. **Why do I** need the phrase: **“Sheep or goat”? Learn from it** that the verse serves **to separate** between them and render one liable to receive a separate set of lashes for eating each type of forbidden fat.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: אִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר״, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: נֵילַף ״שׁוֹר״ ״שׁוֹר״ מִסִּינַי,
> **Translation:** **And the Rabbis maintain** that **if the Merciful One had written** only: You shall eat no **fat of an ox, I would say: Let us derive** a verbal analogy **from** the word **“ox”** stated here as a paradigm representing every type of domesticated animal [*behema*] and the word **“ox,”** i.e., *behema*, stated with regard to a mitzva given in preparation for the revelation at **Sinai:** “Whether it be animal [*behema*] or man, it shall not live” (Exodus 19:13).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה גַּבֵּי סִינַי – חַיָּה וָעוֹף כַּיּוֹצֵא בָּהֶן, אַף גַּבֵּי אֲכִילָה – חַיָּה וָעוֹף כַּיּוֹצֵא בָּהֶן, לְהָכִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״, לְמֵימְרָא דְּהָנֵי דְּאָסוּר, אֲבָל חַיָּה וָעוֹף שְׁרֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the meaning of this hypothetical verbal analogy: **Just as with regard to** the command at **Sinai, undomesticated animals and birds are** subject to the same prohibition **as** domesticated animals despite the use of the term *behema*, **so too, with regard to eating** their fat, **undomesticated animals and birds are** subject to the same prohibition **as** domesticated animals despite the verse employing the example of an ox. **For this** reason **the Merciful One writes: “Ox, or sheep, or goat,” to say that these are forbidden, but** all the fats of **an undomesticated animal and birds are permitted.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שַׁפִּיר קָא אָמְרִי לֵיהּ! אֶלָּא הַיְינוּ טַעְמָא, דְּקָסָבַר: נִכְתּוֹב ״כׇּל חֵלֶב כֶּשֶׂב לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ״, אִי נָמֵי: ״כׇּל חֵלֶב עֵז לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ״, ״שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״ לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לְחַלֵּק.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty against this interpretation of the dispute: The Rabbis **spoke well to** Rabbi Yishmael, i.e., their response is persuasive. How could he say the terms in the verse are superfluous? The Gemara suggests an alternative explanation: **Rather, this is the reasoning of Rabbi Yishmael, as he holds: Let** the verse **write: You shall eat no fat of sheep; alternatively,** let it state: **You shall eat no fat of goat. Why do I** need the verse to state all three: **“Ox, or sheep, or goat”? Learn from it** that the verse serves **to separate** between them and render one liable to receive a separate set of lashes for eating each type of forbidden fat.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: אִי כְּתַב ״כָּל חֵלֶב כֶּשֶׂב״, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: חֵלֶב כֶּשֶׂב אָסוּר, וְשׁוֹר וָעֵז שְׁרֵי. וְכִי תֵּימָא, מַאי אוּלְמֵיהּ דְּכֶשֶׂב – מִשּׁוּם דְּנִתְרַבָּה בְּאַלְיָה,
> **Translation:** **And the Rabbis maintain** that **if** the Torah **had written** merely: **You shall eat no fat of sheep, I would say** it is only **the fat of sheep** that is **forbidden, but** the fat of **an ox or a goat** is **permitted. And if you would say:** In **what** way **is** the category **of sheep stronger,** i.e., more fitting to have its fats forbidden, than the categories of ox and goats, that one would think the prohibition applies only to sheep? The answer is that one might have thought so **due to** the fact **that** there is **an increased** obligation **with regard to** a sheep’s **tail,** as it is sacrificed upon the altar, which is not the case with an ox or a goat.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִדְתָנָא רַבִּי חֲנַנְיָא: לָמָּה מָנָה הַכָּתוּב אֵימוּרִין בְּשׁוֹר וְאֵימוּרִים בְּכֶשֶׂב, וְאֵימוּרִים, בְּעֵז, דִּכְתִיב: ״אַךְ בְּכוֹר שׁוֹר וְגוֹ׳?
> **Translation:** **And** this is **as Rabbi Ḥanina taught: Why does the verse list** the obligation to burn the **sacrificial portions** on the altar **with regard to** a firstborn **ox, and** the obligation to burn the **sacrificial portions [*ve’eimurim*] with regard to** a firstborn **sheep, and** the obligation to burn the **sacrificial portions with regard to** a firstborn **goat? As it is written: “But the firstborn of a bull,** or the firstborn of a sheep, or the firstborn of a goat, you shall not redeem; they are sacred. You shall dash their blood against the altar, and shall make their fat smoke for an offering made by fire” (Numbers 18:17). The “fat” mentioned in this verse is their portions to be burned on the altar.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** צְרִיכִי, דְּאִי כְּתַב שׁוֹר, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: כֶּשֶׂב וָעֵז לָא יָלְפִינַן מִינֵּיהּ, דְּאִיכָּא לְמִיפְרַךְ: מָה לְשׁוֹר, שֶׁכֵּן נִתְרַבָּה בִּנְסָכִים.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Ḥanina explains: These repetitions **are necessary, as, if** the Torah **had written** this obligation only with regard to a firstborn **ox I would say** that **we do not derive** the *halakha* of **a sheep and a goat from it, as** that derivation **can be refuted: What** is notable **about an ox?** It is notable **in that** an ox **has an increased** number **of** wine **libations,** more than those poured with sheep and goat offerings. Therefore, perhaps the additional obligation to burn the sacrificial portions applies only with regard to an ox.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נִכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא בְּכֶשֶׂב וְנֵילַף שׁוֹר וָעֵז מִכֶּשֶׂב – אִיכָּא לְמִיפְרַךְ: מָה לְכֶשֶׂב, שֶׁכֵּן נִתְרַבָּה בְּאַלְיָה.
> **Translation:** And likewise, if you were to suggest: **Let the Merciful One write** the obligation to burn the sacrificial portions only **with regard to a sheep and we will derive** the *halakha* of **an ox and a goat from** the *halakha* of **a sheep,** that derivation **can be refuted: What** is notable **about a sheep?** It is notable **in that** there is **an increased** obligation **with regard to** a sheep’s **tail,** as explained earlier.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נִכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא עֵז וְנֵילַף שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב מִינֵּיהּ – אִיכָּא לְמִיפְרַךְ: מָה לְעֵז, שֶׁכֵּן נִתְרַבָּה אֵצֶל עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה.
> **Translation:** And similarly, if you suggest: **Let the Merciful One write** the obligation to burn the sacrificial portions only with regard to **a goat, and we will derive** the *halakha* of **an ox and a sheep from** the *halakha* of a goat, this too **can be refuted: What** is notable **about a goat?** It is notable **in that** a goat **has an increased** applicability **with regard to idol worship,** as one who sins unwittingly in the case of idol worship is liable to bring a goat as a sin offering (see Numbers 15:27), unlike one who transgresses other prohibitions unwittingly, for which they are liable to bring a sheep.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִן חַד לָא יָלְפִינַן, נִכְתּוֹב תַּרְתֵּי וְנֵילַף חֲדָא מִתַּרְתֵּי! הֵי דֵין? נֵילַף שׁוֹר מִכֶּשֶׂב וָעֵז – אִית לְהוֹן פִּירְכָא: מָה לְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז, שֶׁכֵּן נִתְרַבּוּ אֵצֶל הַפֶּסַח!
> **Translation:** Rabbi Ḥanina continues: **We cannot derive** these *halakhot* **from** the *halakha* of any **one** of the others, but **let** the Torah **write two** of them **and we will derive** the *halakha* of **one** of them **from** the *halakha* of the other **two. Which** is **this** animal that should not be written? If one suggests **we will derive** the obligation to burn the sacrificial portions of a firstborn **ox from** the obligation to burn the sacrificial portions of **a sheep and a goat,** this derivation **has a refutation: What** is notable **about a sheep and a goat?** They are notable **in that** that **they have increased** applicability, as they are suitable **for the Paschal offering,** whereas an ox is not suitable for this purpose.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא נִכְתּוֹב כֶּשֶׂב וְנֵילַף מִשּׁוֹר וָעֵז – אִית לְהוֹן פִּירְכָא: מָה לְשׁוֹר וָעֵז, שֶׁכֵּן נִתְרַבָּה אֵצֶל עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה!
> **Translation:** If one suggests the verse should **not write** the obligation to burn the sacrificial portions of **a sheep, and we will derive** its *halakha* **from** the *halakha* of **an ox and a goat,** this derivation **has a refutation: What** is notable **about an ox and a goat?** They are notable **in that they have increased** applicability as suitable offerings to atone **for** an unwitting transgression of **idol worship,** as when a community unwittingly sins with regard to idol worship they bring an ox as a burnt offering and a goat as a sin offering (see Numbers 15:24), while an individual brings a goat but not a sheep.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא נִכְתּוֹב עֵז, וְנֵילַף מִשּׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב – אִית לְהוֹן פִּירְכָא: מָה לְשׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב, שֶׁכֵּן יֵשׁ בּוֹ צַד רִיבּוּי! הִלְכָּךְ לָא יָלְפִי מֵהֲדָדֵי.
> **Translation:** If one suggests that the verse should **not write** the obligation to burn the sacrificial portions of **a goat, and we will derive** its *halakha* **from** the *halakha* of **an ox and a sheep,** this derivation **has a refutation: What** is notable **about an ox and a sheep?** They are notable **in that each** of them **has an increased aspect** of applicability with regard to the altar, as the libations for an ox are greater than those for a goat, and the tail of a sheep, but not that of a goat, is burned on the altar. Rabbi Ḥanina concludes: **Therefore,** all three cases are necessary, as **they cannot be derived from each other.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שַׁפִּיר קָא אָמְרִי לֵיהּ! אֶלָּא לְעוֹלָם טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל כִּדְאָמְרִינַן מֵעִיקָּרָא, דְּאִם כֵּן נִכְתּוֹב ״כׇּל חֵלֶב״ וְלִישְׁתּוֹק. מַאי אָמְרַתְּ, הַאי דִּכְתַב ״שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״ לְמִשְׁרֵי חֵלֶב חַיָּה?
> **Translation:** The Rabbis have explained why all three mentions of an ox, a sheep, and a goat are necessary. Consequently, the Gemara again states: The Rabbis **spoke well to** Rabbi Yishmael, i.e., their response is persuasive. The Gemara suggests: **Rather, the reason of Rabbi Yishmael** is **actually as we said at the outset, that if so,** that one who eats the forbidden fats of all three animals is liable to receive only one set of lashes, **let** the verse **write:** You shall eat no **fat, and be silent. What did you say** in response? That **this** fact **that** the verse **writes: “Ox, or sheep, or goat,”** serves **to permit** the **fat of an undomesticated animal?** This is not a valid objection.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא כִּי כְּתַב קְרָא – בְּעִנְיָינָא דְקָדָשִׁים, וְדָבָר לָמֵד מֵעִנְיָינוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: **When the verse** concerning forbidden fat **is written** it is **in the context of sacrificial** animals, since the subsequent verse concerning the prohibition against eating forbidden fat states: “For whoever eats the fat of the animal of which men present an offering of fire to the Lord, even the soul that eats it shall be cut off from his people” (Leviticus 7:25). The verse categorizes an animal whose fat one may not eat as an “animal of which men present an offering of fire to the Lord,” i.e., offerings. **And** one of the thirteen hermeneutical principles is: **A matter** is **derived from its context,** i.e., one interprets a verse according to the context in which it is written. Since offerings may be brought only from domesticated, not undomesticated, animals, the prohibition against eating forbidden fat applies only to such animals.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִכְּלָל דְּרַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: לָא יָלְפִינַן דָּבָר לָמֵד מֵעִנְיָינוֹ? לָא, דְּכוּלֵּי עָלְמָא יָלְפִינַן דָּבָר הַלָּמֵד מֵעִנְיָינוֹ, וְהָכָא בְּהָא פְּלִיגִי, רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל סָבַר: לְמֵידִין לָאו מִלָּאו בֵּין מִלָּאו וּבֵין מִלָּאו דְּכָרֵת,
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: Can one conclude **by inference that the Rabbis maintain we do not derive** *halakhot* by employing the hermeneutical principle of: **A matter** is **derived from its context?** This cannot be correct, as it is an accepted basic principle of exegesis. The Gemara answers: **No, everyone** agrees that **we do derive** *halakhot* by employing the hermeneutical principle of: **A matter** is **derived from its context, and here** it is **about this** issue **that they disagree: Rabbi Yishmael maintains** that **we derive** the *halakhot* of one **prohibition from** the *halakhot* of another **prohibition,** and this applies **whether** it is **from** a standard **prohibition,** whose transgression results in the punishment of lashes, **or whether from a prohibition** whose transgression results in the punishment **of *karet*.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּ״כׇל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״, מִלָּאו דְּ״חֻקַּת עוֹלָם לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם בְּכֹל מוֹשְׁבֹתֵיכֶם כׇּל חֵלֶב וְכׇל דָּם לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ״, דִּכְתִיב בְּעִנְיָינָא דְקָדָשִׁים, וּבְקָדָשִׁים לָא אִית בְּהוֹן חַיָּה, אַף ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר״, כִּי כְּתִיב לֵיהּ סְתָמָא לֵיכָּא לְסַפּוֹקֵי בְּחַיָּה, הִלְכָּךְ ״שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״ לְחַלֵּק הוּא דַּאֲתָא, לְחַיֵּיב עַל כׇּל אַחַת וְאַחַת.
> **Translation:** Accordingly, the prohibition **of:** “You shall eat **no fat of ox, or sheep, or goat,”** can be derived **from the prohibition of: “It shall be a perpetual statute throughout your generations in all your dwellings, that you shall eat neither fat nor blood”** (Leviticus 3:17), **which is written with regard to the matter of sacrificial** animals. **And there are no undomesticated animals in** the category of **sacrificial** animals. **So too,** with regard to the prohibition of: “You shall eat **no fat of ox,** or sheep, or goat,” **when** the verse **writes it without** further **specification, there is no** reason **to be uncertain** whether or not it is referring also **to undomesticated animals. Therefore,** the phrase **“ox, or sheep, or goat” comes to separate,** i.e., **to render one liable for** eating the forbidden fat of **each and every one** of them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְיָלְפִינַן לָאו דְּ״כׇל חֵלֶב״ וְלָאו דְּ״חֻקַּת עוֹלָם״ מִכָּרֵת דְּ״כִי כׇּל אוֹכֵל חֵלֶב מִן הַבְּהֵמָה אֲשֶׁר יַקְרִיבוּ מִמֶּנָּה״, מָה הָהוּא לְחַלֵּק – אַף הַדִּין לְחַלֵּק.
> **Translation:** All this teaches only that a separate prohibition applies to each type of animal. **And** with regard to the liability to receive lashes for eating each one **we derive the prohibition of:** “You shall eat **no fat,” and the prohibition of: “It shall be a perpetual statute,” from the** example of ***karet*** stated in the verse: **“For anyone who eats the fat of the domesticated animal, of which people present** an offering of fire to the Lord, the soul that eats it shall be cut off from its people” (Leviticus 7:25). **Just as that** verse serves **to separate** and teach that if one unwittingly ate the fat of an ox, a goat, and a sheep he is liable to bring a sin offering for each one, **so too, this** verse serves **to separate** and teach that one is liable to receive lashes for eating the fat of each one.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן, לָאו מִלָּאו יָלְפִינַן, לָאו מִכָּרֵת לָא יָלְפִינַן.
> **Translation:** **And the Rabbis** maintain **we derive** the *halakhot* of one standard **prohibition from** the *halakhot* of another standard **prohibition,** but **we do not derive** the *halakhot* of a standard **prohibition from** the *halakhot* of a prohibition whose transgression results in the punishment of ***karet*.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: הַיְינוּ טַעְמַיְיהוּ דְּרַבָּנַן, כְּדַאֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב מָרִי לְרַב זְבִיד: ״אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה, אַלְיָה דְחוּלִּין תִּיתְּסַר אֲמַר לֵיהּ״ – ״עָלֶיךָ אָמַר קְרָא: ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״, דָּבָר הַשָּׁוֶה בִּשְׁלׇשְׁתָּן בָּעִינַן, וְלֵיכָּא״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **And if you wish, say** instead that **this is the reason of the Rabbis: As Rav Mari said to Rav Zevid** when asked about a different issue: **If that is so,** that a sheep tail is categorized as fat by the verses and is included in the portions of an offering for which one is liable for the misuse of consecrated property, then the **tail of a non-sacred** animal **should be prohibited** for consumption as forbidden fat. Rav Zevid **said to** Rav Mari: **With regard to your** claim, **the verse states** concerning forbidden fat: “You shall eat **no fat of ox, or sheep, or goat”** (Leviticus 7:23). This teaches that for the prohibition against eating forbidden fat to take effect **we require an item that is** found **equally in all three** types of animals, an ox, and a sheep, and a goat, **and that is not** the case here. Since an ox and goat do not have tails, the tail is not prohibited even in the case of a sheep.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הִלְכָּךְ: כִּי אֲתָא ״שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״ – לְמִישְׁרֵי אַלְיָה דְחוּלִּין הוּא דַּאֲתָא. וְרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל, אָמַר לָךְ: אִם כֵּן, לֵימָא קְרָא ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב״, עֵז לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לְחַלֵּק.
> **Translation:** **Therefore,** the same applies with regard to the issue at hand: **When** the verse **“ox, or sheep, or goat” comes, it comes to permit** consumption of the **tail of a non-sacred** animal, and one cannot learn from here that a separate prohibition applies to each type of forbidden fat. **And Rabbi Yishmael** could **say to you** in response: **If so, let** the verse **state:** You shall eat **no fat of ox or sheep; why do I** need the mention of **a goat? Learn from it** that the verse serves **to separate** between them and render one liable to receive lashes for eating each type of forbidden fat.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: מוֹדֶה רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל לְעִנְיַן קׇרְבָּן שֶׁאֵין מֵבִיא אֶלָּא חַטָּאת אַחַת, מַאי טַעְמָא? דְּלָא דָּמֵי הָדֵין לָאו לְלָאו דַּעֲרָיוֹת.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Ḥanina says:** Although **Rabbi Yishmael** maintains that one who unwittingly eats the fat of an ox, a sheep, and a goat is liable to receive a separate set of lashes for each one, he **concedes with regard to** bringing **an offering that** if he ate those types of forbidden fat in a single lapse of awareness he **brings only one sin offering. What is the reason?** The reason is **that this prohibition is not similar to the prohibition of those with whom relations are forbidden.** As derived in the Gemara on 2b, one who unwittingly engages in intercourse with many women to whom he is forbidden is liable to bring a sin offering for each act. In that context the verse states a separate prohibition for each forbidden relative, whereas in this case there is a single prohibition that applies to the forbidden fat of all domesticated animals.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״וְעָשָׂה אַחַת״ ״וְעָשָׂה הֵנָּה״ – לְחַיֵּיב עַל כׇּל אַחַת וְאַחַת,
> **Translation:** § With regard to the sin offering required of one who transgresses the prohibition against eating forbidden fat, **the Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states: “Speak to the children of Israel, saying: A soul that sins in error, from all the commandments of God that may not be performed, and from one of them” (Leviticus 4:2). This phrase should be interpreted as though it states: **And performs one,** and again states: **And performs them,** i.e., it serves **to render one liable** to bring a sin offering **for each and every** transgression.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁאִם אָכַל חֵלֶב וְחֵלֶב, שֵׁם אֶחָד בִּשְׁנֵי הֶעְלֵמוֹת – חַיָּיב שְׁתַּיִם, שְׁתֵּי שֵׁמוֹת בְּהֶעְלֵם אַחַת – חַיָּיב שְׁתַּיִם.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: This teaches **that if one ate** forbidden **fat and** again ate forbidden **fat,** if it was from **one category,** i.e., the same type of forbidden fat, and he ate it **in two lapses of awareness,** that is, he was made aware of his sin after the first instance of consumption and then again ate unwittingly, he is **liable** to bring **two** sin offerings. If he ate forbidden fat from **two categories,** as the Gemara will soon explain, **in one lapse of awareness,** he is likewise **liable** to bring **two** sin offerings.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רָמֵי בַּר חָמָא לְרַב חִסְדָּא: בִּשְׁלָמָא שֵׁם אֶחָד בִּשְׁנֵי הֶעְלֵמוֹת חַיָּיב שְׁתַּיִם, מִשּׁוּם דְּהֶעְלֵמוֹת מְחַלְּקִין. אֶלָּא שְׁנֵי שֵׁמוֹת בְּהֶעְלֵם אַחַת, אַמַּאי חַיָּיב שְׁתַּיִם? הָא בָּעִינַן הֶעְלֵמוֹת מְוחַלְּקִין, וְלֵיכָּא!
> **Translation:** **Rami bar Ḥama said to Rav Ḥisda: Granted,** one who ate forbidden fat from **one category in two lapses of awareness** is **liable** to bring **two** sin offerings, as this is **due to** the fact **that the lapses of awareness separate** between his transgressions; each time he becomes aware of his transgression he is liable to bring another sin offering. **But** in a case where he ate forbidden fat from **two categories in one lapse of awareness, why** is he **liable** to bring **two** sin offerings? **We require separate lapses of awareness and that is not** the case here.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן – כְּגוֹן דַּאֲכַל חֵלֶב דְּנוֹתָר, דְּמִחַיַּיב מִשּׁוּם נוֹתָר וּמִשּׁוּם חֵלֶב. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אִם כֵּן, נִיחַיַּיב נָמֵי מִשּׁוּם קוֹדֶשׁ!
> **Translation:** Rav Ḥisda **said to him: Here we are dealing with** a case **where he ate** forbidden **fat left over from an offering after the time allotted for its consumption [*notar*], as he is liable due to** eating ***notar* and** is also liable **due to** eating forbidden **fat.** These are the two categories mentioned in the *baraita*. Rami bar Ḥama **said to him: If so,** that the case involves more than one prohibition, **let him be liable due to** eating **sacrificial** food **as well,** and he should be liable to bring a guilt offering for the unwitting misuse of consecrated property (see Leviticus 5:14–16).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: כְּגוֹן דַּאֲכַל חֵלֶב דְּהֶקְדֵּשׁ, וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה הִיא. דְּתַנְיָא: אָכַל חֵלֶב נְבֵלָה, אָכַל חֵלֶב מוּקְדָּשִׁין – חַיָּיב שְׁתַּיִם. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: חֵלֶב מוּקְדָּשִׁין – לוֹקֶה שָׁלֹשׁ.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rav Sheshet said:** Here we are dealing with a case **where he ate** forbidden **fat of sacrificial** animals, **and** this ruling **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda,** who renders one liable to bring two sin offerings for eating forbidden fat of sacrificial food. **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: If one **ate** forbidden **fat of an animal carcass,** or if he **ate** forbidden **fat of sacrificial** animals, he is **liable** to receive **two** sets of lashes, either for transgressing the prohibitions of eating forbidden fat and eating an animal carcass, or for both eating forbidden fat and transgressing the prohibition forbidding a non-priest to partake of sacrificial animals. **Rabbi Yehuda says:** If one **ate** forbidden **fat of sacrificial** animals he **is flogged** with **three** sets of lashes, because there are two separate prohibitions of forbidden fat in the case of sacrificial animals, as the Gemara will soon explain, in addition to the prohibition forbidding a non-priest to partake of sacrificial animals.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מְחַיְּיכוּ עֲלַהּ בְּמַעְרְבָא: וְנוֹקְמַהּ כְּגוֹן חֵלֶב דְּשׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז, וְרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל הִיא, דְּאָמַר: לוֹקֶה שְׁלֹשׁ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: **They laughed at this** suggestion **in the West,** i.e., Eretz Yisrael: **And let us interpret** this *baraita* as referring to a case **where** he ate the forbidden **fat of an ox, and** of **a sheep, and** of **a goat, and** explain that **it is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yishmael, who says** that he **is flogged** with **three** sets of lashes for the different types of fat, if he did so intentionally and was forewarned.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַלְמָא לָא אוֹקְמַהּ כְּרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל, דְּאָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: מוֹדֶה הָיָה רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל לְעִנְיַן קׇרְבָּן שֶׁאֵינוֹ חַיָּיב אֶלָּא חַטָּאת אַחַת, הַשְׁתָּא נָמֵי כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה לָא מִתּוֹקְמָא, דְּהָא אָמַר רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר: מוֹדֶה הָיָה רַבִּי יְהוּדָה לְעִנְיַן קׇרְבָּן שֶׁאֵין מֵבִיא אֶלָּא חַטָּאת אַחַת!
> **Translation:** **Why** does one **not interpret** the *baraita* **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yishmael?** The reason is **as Rabbi Ḥanina says:** Although **Rabbi Yishmael** maintains that one who eats the fat of an ox, a sheep, and a goat is liable to receive a separate set of lashes for each one, he **would concede with regard to** bringing **an offering that** if he ate those types of forbidden fat in a single lapse of awareness **he is liable** to bring **only one sin offering. Now too,** the *baraita* **should not be interpreted in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda, as doesn’t Rabbi Eliezer say: Rabbi Yehuda would concede with regard to** bringing **an offering that** if one ate forbidden fat of sacrificial animals he **brings only one sin offering?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ מִשּׁוּם בַּר תּאוּטֵנִי: הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן – כְּגוֹן שֶׁאֲכָלָן בִּשְׁנֵי תַמְחוּיִין, וְאַלִּיבָּא דְּרַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ, דְּאָמַר: תַּמְחוּיִין מְחַלְּקִים.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Reish Lakish said in the name of** the Sage **bar Ta’utni: What are we dealing with here,** where one is liable to bring two sin offerings for eating forbidden fat of two categories? We are dealing with a case **in which he ate** the forbidden fat **from two dishes,** i.e., where each part was prepared in a different manner, **and** this is **in accordance with** the opinion **of Rabbi Yehoshua, who says** that **dishes** serve to **separate** the acts of eating. One who eats forbidden food from two different dishes in the course of a single lapse of awareness is considered to have transgressed two prohibitions and is liable to bring a sin offering for each one.

The Sages respond: had the Torah written only *kol chelev lo tocheilu*, one might have included the fat of a *chaya* — a non-domesticated animal like a deer — which is in fact permitted. The Torah had to specify *shor, chesev, va-ez* in order to limit the prohibition to these domesticated animals and exclude *chaya* and fowl. The specification is thus necessitated by the need to exclude, not by the desire to multiply. The *Gemara* declares: *shapir ka'amrei* — that is a compelling response.

But Rabbi Yishmael's position is not yet defeated. The *Gemara* asks: why not write simply *kol chelev shor lo tocheilu*? Had the Torah written only ox, one would not have applied the prohibition to *chaya* — there is no analogical extension from *shor* to *chaya* in the context of eating that would yield such a reading. In that case, *kesev va-ez lama li* — why was it necessary to add sheep and goat? This, says Rabbi Yishmael, is the proof: *shema mina la-challek* — the only remaining reason is to establish that each constitutes a distinct prohibition. The Sages, however, counter that one actually would have over-generalized from *shor* alone, since the Torah elsewhere uses *shor* in a way that extends to all animals — as in the Shabbat verse *lema'an yanuach shorecha*, where *shor* is understood to include every animal. Had the prohibition been written using *shor* alone, one might have read it to encompass even *chaya* and fowl by that same extension. The Torah therefore had to specify all three precisely to signal that only these three are forbidden, and the broader category of *chaya* and fowl is permitted. The *Gemara* again declares *shapir ka'amrei*.

Rabbi Yishmael presses further: then let the Torah have written *kol chelev kesev lo tocheilu*, or alternatively *kol chelev ez lo tocheilu*. Sheep, unlike ox, is never used as a paradigmatic term that would invite that kind of over-generalization; had the Torah said sheep, one would have understood it to mean *behema* — domesticated animals — without including *chaya*. If so, *shor va-chesev va-ez lama li* — why list all three? The addition is inexplicable unless it is to divide the prohibition and create separate liabilities. The Sages respond that had the Torah written only sheep, one might have concluded the reverse error: not over-generalization but under-specification. One would have said that only sheep is forbidden, reasoning that sheep must have been singled out on account of something distinctive to it in the realm of fat — namely the fat tail, the *alya*, which is a unique offering on the *mizbe'ach* brought exclusively from a sheep. The Torah would thus appear to be treating sheep's fat as categorically special rather than representative, and a reader would conclude that ox and goat are in fact permitted. The listing of all three, then, serves the necessary function of establishing the full scope of the prohibition without leaving room for either error — a point the *Gemara* elaborates further in terms of what is uniquely distinctive about each of the three animals.

### Animal Specialties

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוּפָא: אָכַל חֵלֶב נְבֵילָה – לוֹקֶה שְׁתַּיִם, חֵלֶב מוּקְדָּשִׁין – לוֹקֶה שְׁתַּיִם, רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: חֵלֶב מוּקְדָּשִׁין – לוֹקֶה שָׁלֹשׁ.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara returns to discuss **the** matter **itself:** If one **ate** forbidden **fat of an animal carcass** he **is flogged** with **two** sets of lashes for transgressing the prohibitions of eating forbidden fat and consuming an animal carcass. If he **ate** forbidden **fat of sacrificial** animals, he **is flogged** with **two** sets of lashes, for both eating forbidden fat and transgressing the prohibition forbidding a non-priest to partake of sacrificial animals. **Rabbi Yehuda says:** If one **ate** forbidden **fat of sacrificial** animals, he **is flogged** with **three** sets of lashes.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב שֵׁיזְבִי לְרָבָא: בִּשְׁלָמָא לְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, הַיְינוּ דִּכְתִיבִי קְרָאֵי: ״חֻקַּת עוֹלָם״. ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ״, ״וְכׇל זָר לֹא יֹאכַל קֹדֶשׁ״ – הָא תְּלָתָא לָאוִין. אֶלָּא לְרַבָּנַן, מַאי טַעְמַיְיהוּ?
> **Translation:** **Rav Sheizevi said to Rava: Granted, according to** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda, this is** the meaning of the three **verses that are written: “It shall be a perpetual statute** throughout your generations in all your dwellings, that you shall eat neither fat nor blood” (Leviticus 3:17); **“You shall eat no fat, of ox, or sheep, or goat”** (Leviticus 7:23); **“And no non-priest shall partake of the sacred food”** (Leviticus 22:10), as **these** are **three prohibitions,** two for eating forbidden fat of sacrificial animals, as the two verses that mention fat are discussing sacrificial animals, and one for the prohibition forbidding a non-priest to partake of consecrated food. **But according to** the opinion of **the Rabbis, what is their reasoning?** Why is one liable to receive only two sets of lashes?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָסָבְרִי: לָאו דְּ״חֻקַּת עוֹלָם״ בְּקָדָשִׁים, וְלָאו דְּ״חֵלֶב שׁוֹר״ בְּחוּלִּין. וּצְרִיכִי,
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: The Rabbis **maintain** that the **prohibition of: “It shall be a perpetual statute,”** was stated **with regard to sacrificial** animals, **but the prohibition of:** “You shall eat no **fat of ox,”** was stated **with regard to non-sacred** animals. Therefore, if one ate forbidden fat of sacrificial animals he is liable to receive only two sets of lashes. **And** both verses **are necessary,** as one could not have been derived from the other.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּאִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא בְּקָדָשִׁים, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: קָדָשִׁים הוּא דַּחֲמִירִי, דְּאָסוּר חֶלְבּוֹ, אֲבָל חוּלִּין – אֵימָא לָא, מִשּׁוּם הָכִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא: ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר״.
> **Translation:** **As, had the Merciful One written** the prohibition against eating forbidden fat only **with regard to sacrificial** animals **I would say: It is** only in the case of **sacrificial** animals, **whose** prohibitions are generally **stringent, that** their **fat is forbidden. But** with regard to **non-sacred** animals one might **say** their fat is **not** forbidden. It is **due to that** reason that **the Merciful One writes:** “You shall eat **no fat of ox.”**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי כְּתַב: ״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר״, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: חֵלֶב דְחוּלִּין הוּא דְּאָסוּר מִשּׁוּם דְּלֹא הוּתַּר מִכְּלָלוֹ, אֲבָל חֵלֶב מוּקְדָּשִׁין דְּהוּתַּר מִכְּלָלוֹ, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: מִדְּהוּתַּר בְּשָׂרָן אִישְׁתְּרִי נָמֵי חֶלְבָּן, צְרִיכִי.
> **Translation:** **And** by contrast, **had the Merciful One written** only: “You shall eat **no fat of ox,” I would say: It is** only the **fat** of **non-sacred** animals **that is forbidden, since** there are **no** circumstances in **which its general prohibition was permitted,** i.e., those fats of domesticated animals are always forbidden. **But** with regard to **sacrificial** animals, **whose general prohibition was permitted** in certain circumstances, i.e., with regard to the rest of its meat after the blood has been sprinkled, **I would say** that **from** the fact **that their meat was permitted, their fat was permitted as well.** Therefore, both verses **are necessary,** in order to teach that the prohibition against eating forbidden fat applies equally to sacrificial animals and non-sacred animals.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה סָבַר: כִּי כְּתִיב ״חֵלֶב שׁוֹר״, בָּעִנְיָינָא דְקָדָשִׁים כְּתִיב.
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Yehuda maintains** that **when it is written:** “You shall eat no **fat of ox,” it is** also **written with regard to the matter of sacrificial** animals, as it is stated after the prohibition against eating forbidden fat: “For whoever eats the fat of the animal, of which men present an offering of fire to the Lord, even the soul that eats it shall be cut off from his people” (Leviticus 7:25), and this is derived by employing the hermeneutical principle: A matter is derived from its context.

The discussion then deepens into why no single animal from the three could have served as the representative for all others, which would have made specifying all three redundant. A *beraisa* cited by *Rabbi Chananya* establishes this point in the context of the obligation to offer *imurin* — the internal fats and organs — from a *bechor*: *ach bechor shor o bechor chesev o bechor ez lo sifdeh, es damam tizrok ve-es chelbam taktir*.

Had the Torah written only ox, one might have thought that sheep and goat need not have their *imurin* offered — since ox carries a larger measure of *nesachim*, one might say that is what distinguishes it, and its fat is treated with special sanctity. Had it written only sheep, one might say that sheep alone requires this, given that it alone has the fat tail (*alya*), a distinct element offered on the *mizbeach*. Had it written only goat, one might infer from ox and sheep — but then again, ox has the distinction of larger *nesachim* and sheep has the *alya*, and neither possesses what goat possesses, namely that goat is the only *behemah* brought as a *korban* in the context of *avodah zarah*. Each animal is therefore genuinely distinct and irreplaceable in the context of *kodshim*, and the Torah had no choice but to list all three.

The *gemara* then turns this same analytic pressure back on the *lav* verse. One might attempt a two-animal inference: could one not simply write sheep and goat and derive ox from them? No — *mah l'chesev ve-ez*, sheep and goat share the distinguishing feature of being brought as a *korban Pesach*, which ox is not, so no inference to ox can stand. Could one write ox and goat and derive sheep? No — *mah l'shor ve-ez*, ox and goat are each brought in the context of *avodah zarah*, which sheep is not. Could one write ox and sheep and derive goat? No — *mah l'shor ve-chesev*, both ox and sheep have an element of *ribui* on the *mizbeach* — the ox with its larger *nesachim* and the sheep with its *alya* — which goat does not. No two animals in any combination can yield the third without a valid *pircha* blocking the inference. *Heicha lo yalfi me-hadadi* — there is simply no way to derive any one of the three from the others.

This very same reasoning applies to the prohibition of *chelev*: had only one animal been named, one might either over-generalize or under-specify. The Torah listed all three of necessity.

## Lav from Karet



### Context Mention

The *Gemara* returns to the underlying methodological dispute. The verse containing the *lav* — *kol chelev shor ve-chesev ve-ez lo tocheilu* — appears in proximity to the verse containing the *karet*: *ki kol ochel chelev min ha-behemah asher takrivu mimena korban la-Hashem*. The *karet* verse specifies the prohibition in terms of *behemah asher takrivu* — an animal brought as a *korban* — and makes clear it applies to ox, sheep, and goat. Since the *lav* verse appears in the same context, could one not read the animals listed in the *lav* as already implied by the *karet* verse, making the explicit listing superfluous and therefore interpretively significant?

### Yishmael Learns

*Rabbi Yishmael* applies this logic: *davar ha-lamed me-inyano* — a teaching derived from its context. Since by *karet* the limitation to *shor, chesev, va-ez* is clear, one could have inferred the same for the *lav*. The explicit mention of all three in the *lav* verse is thus not needed to define the scope of the prohibition; it therefore carries additional meaning and signals distinct prohibitions for each animal.

### Sages Restriction

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מִכְּלָל דְּרַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: לָא יָלְפִינַן דָּבָר הַלָּמֵד מֵעִנְיָינוֹ?
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **But** can one conclude from here **by inference that the Rabbis maintain we do not derive** *halakhot* by employing the hermeneutical principle of: **A matter** is **derived from its context?** This cannot be correct, as it is an accepted, basic principle of exegesis.

The Sages respond: *lo yalfinan lav mi-karet* — one may not derive the content of a *lav* from what is stated by a *karet*. What appears in the context of *karet* applies to *karet*; one cannot carry it over to establish the scope of a separate *lav*. Therefore, the listing of all three animals in the *lav* verse was necessary to define the prohibition's scope, and it carries no additional meaning. There is accordingly only one *lav* and only one liability for *malkus*.

The debate thus ultimately hinges on whether *Rabbi Yishmael* is correct that one may derive a *lav* from a *karet* — learning the content of a prohibition from the context of a penalty — or whether, as the Sages hold, such cross-derivation is inadmissible.
