# Menachot 76 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Rubbings & Poundings

### 300 & 500 Requirement

The daf opens with the Mishnah's statement: *kol hamenachot te'unot shalosh me'ot shifa vachamesh me'ot be'ita* — all of the menachot require 300 rubbings and 500 poundings. These are striking numbers. The rubbings are presumably performed on the wheat kernels in order to loosen the shells and bran, while the poundings — Rashi understands the term *be'ita* as striking with the fist — are performed as part of the physical preparation of the offering. The word *be'ita* is related to the word for kicking with a leg, but Rashi, based on context, applies it to pounding with the fist.

### Tanna Kamma vs. R. Yosi

The Mishnah continues: *shifa u've'ita bechitim* — the rubbing and the pounding were both performed with the wheat. It is not entirely clear how pounding the wheat kernels differs from grinding them; one might have expected the process to involve a millstone rather than a mortar-and-pestle style pounding. The suggestion is that the pounding loosens the grain and prepares it for subsequent processing. Whatever the precise physical mechanics, the Tanna Kamma locates both actions at the stage of the wheat.

What is particularly striking about this tannaitic dispute is its underlying structure. Neither the Tanna Kamma nor Rabbi Yosi appears to be basing himself on a biblical verse or on any independent recollection of what was actually done in the Temple. Rather, both tannaim are the inheritors of an earlier, more apodictic mishnaic tradition — the bare statement that all menachot require 300 rubbings and 500 poundings — and what they are doing is reconstructing, or attempting to reconstruct, the context in which those numbers applied. Was the tradition speaking of the wheat kernels, or of the dough? This is, in fact, a broader pattern in the structure of the Mishnah: scholars have long noted that the later tannaim frequently inherited terse, *stam* formulations from an earlier Mishnah, and their "disputes" are often better understood as competing interpretations of that received tradition rather than as independent positions stated from scratch. A clear parallel is the first Mishnah in Gittin, where the core rule about a *get* brought from outside the Land requiring *bifanai nechtav* is followed by a series of tannaim each explaining what counts as *chutz la'aretz* — they are glossing the original line, not debating an issue the redactor newly framed. The same dynamic is almost certainly at work here: *kol hamenachot te'unot shalosh me'ot shifa vachamesh me'ot be'ita* was the original tradition, and the Tanna Kamma and Rabbi Yosi represent two subsequent attempts to situate it within the actual process of preparing the offering.

### Ten vs. Twelve Loaves

Rabbi Yosi disagrees: *babatze'ik* — it was with the dough. For Rabbi Yosi, the pounding in particular makes considerably more sense as an action performed on dough, since one rolls and pounds dough as a natural part of its preparation. The rubbing of dough is less intuitive but still plausible.

The Mishnah then turns to a separate but related question about the loaves themselves. *Kol haminachos ba'os eser eser chutz milechem hapanim* — all of the menachos come in groups of ten, except for the lechem hapanim. The practical significance of this rule is considerable: the wheat or flour would be divided into ten loaves, whether those loaves took the form of ordinary loaves, wafers, or matzos. The lechem hapanim is the obvious exception, since it consisted of two columns of six loaves each on the shulchan — twelve loaves by explicit scriptural design.

To this, the Mishnah adds *vachavittei Kohen Gadol* — and the chavittim of the Kohen Gadol, meaning the daily mincha offering he brought in two halves, morning and afternoon — *sheba'os shtem esrei, divrei Rabbi Yehuda* — that these came in twelve, in the words of Rabbi Yehuda. The Gemara will derive this from a *gezeirah shavah* connecting the chavittim to the lechem hapanim, which is explicitly twelve.

Rabbi Meir takes the opposite position: *kulam ba'os shtem esrei* — all of them come in twelve loaves, *chutz mechallos Toda* — except for the challos Toda, and the nazir's offering, which everyone agrees come in ten. The dispute between the Tanna Kamma and Rabbi Meir is therefore a disagreement about which offering serves as the paradigm and which is the exception. The Tanna Kamma treats the lachmei Toda as the rule — ten — and the lechem hapanim along with the chavittim as the exceptions at twelve. Rabbi Meir inverts this: twelve is the rule, derived from the lechem hapanim, and the lachmei Toda together with what accompanies it are the exceptions at ten. How we know the Toda loaves came in ten is a question the Gemara will address shortly.

### Alternating Pattern

What is especially striking about this dispute is its epistemological character. Neither the Tanna Kamma nor Rabbi Yosi appears to be basing himself on a *pasuk* or on a first-hand memory of how the procedure was actually performed. They had inherited a tradition — the earlier, apodictic Mishnah statement that all menachot require 300 rubbings and 500 poundings — and they are attempting to reconstruct its context. Was that tradition speaking about the wheat or the dough? That is the question they are debating.

This reflects a broader phenomenon well attested in Mishnaic literature. Scholars have observed that the later Tannaim were frequently the inheritors of earlier, terse Mishnah traditions and that what appears in a Mishnah as a tannaitic dispute is often, in reality, commentary and interpretation of an older, undisputed statement. A clear parallel is the first Mishnah in Gittin, where the global statement about bringing a *get* from *chutz la'aretz* to *eretz yisrael* is followed by several Tannaim each explaining what *chutz* means — each interpreting the original line rather than independently framing a new debate. The same structure is present here: the apodictic statement — 300 rubbings and 500 poundings — was the tradition, and the Tanna Kamma and Rabbi Yosi are each explaining what context that tradition referred to.

### Back & Forth Count

A Braita then explains how one practically maintains the three-to-five ratio of rubbings to poundings. Rather than performing all 300 rubbings followed by all 500 poundings, the practitioner alternates: *shaf achat bo'et shtayim* — one rubbing followed by two poundings; then *shaf shtayim bo'et shalosh* — two rubbings followed by three poundings. In this way, the ratio of three to five is preserved throughout the process. According to the Tanna Kamma, this alternating sequence is how one would loosen the shell and bran from the wheat kernels.

### R. Yosi on Dough

Rabbi Yirmiya raises a question about how to count the rubbings. When one performs a rubbing, the natural motion is back and forth. Does that back-and-forth movement count as one rubbing or as two? — *amtuyei ve'atuyei chad*, back and forth is one, *o dilma amtuyei ve'atuyei trei*, back and forth is two? The Gemara leaves this unresolved: *teiku*. This question recurs in other contexts in the Gemara where actions performed back and forth must be counted a fixed number of times.

### Mixed Approach

The Gemara also raises the question of whether Rabbi Yosi intends to say that the rubbing and pounding were performed *only* with the dough and not at all with the wheat, or whether he agrees that they were also performed with the wheat and is merely adding that they were performed with the dough as well. A Braita resolves this: Rabbi Yosi's repetition of the verbs *shifa u've'ita* in his statement — rather than simply saying "also with the dough" — implies that the rubbing and pounding belonged to the dough alone, not to the wheat.

However, the left-hand column of the standard printed edition notes a different *girsa*: *shifa bechitim u've'ita babatze'ik* — the rubbing is with the wheat, and the pounding is with the dough. A note indicates *vechein mashma baRambam* — this is also the implication of the Rambam. This third reading is arguably the most logically intuitive of all: rubbing is a natural action to perform on wheat kernels to separate the shell, while pounding is a natural action to perform on dough during its preparation. Yet notably, no one derives their position from a *pasuk* or from any remembered practice; all positions are reconstructions of a shared older tradition.

## Model Selection Debate

### Paradigm Question

The Mishnah continues: *kol hamenachot ba'ot eser eser chutz milechem hapanim* — all of the menachot come in ten loaves, except for the lechem hapanim, which explicitly comprised twelve loaves arranged in two columns of six on the shulchan, and the chavitei Kohen Gadol — the daily minchat chavitin that the Kohen Gadol brought half in the morning and half in the afternoon — which also come in twelve. This is the position of Rabbi Yehuda.

### R. Yehuda: Ten

Rabbi Meir takes the opposite position: *kulam ba'ot shtem esrei* — all of the menachot come in twelve loaves, except for the chalot Toda and the breads accompanying the Nazir offering, which come in ten. The dispute is, in essence, about which is the general rule and which is the exception: is the paradigm ten loaves with twelve as the anomaly, or is the paradigm twelve with ten as the anomaly?

The Gemara addresses this directly. We have two potential models: the lechem hapanim, which comes in twelve, and the lachmei Todah, which comes in ten. Rabbi Yehuda chooses to derive the general rule from the lachmei Todah — *gamar mi-lachmei Todah*, we learn it from the breads of the Todah offering — and the reasoning behind that choice requires some unpacking. The number ten by the Todah is itself not stated explicitly in the Torah. Rather, the Torah instructs that one loaf of each type be given to the kohen, describing that loaf with the word *terumah*. Now, *terumah* can mean different things in different contexts: the standard terumah separated by a Yisrael is roughly one fortieth to one sixtieth of his produce, a far smaller fraction. But the Sages understood the *terumah* here in light of another context — *terumas ma'aser*, in which the Levites separate a tenth of their ma'aser for the kohen, and the Torah there explicitly calls that portion *ma'aser min ha-ma'aser*, one tenth. Since the Sages understood that the Todah loaves were not being baked in quantities of fifty or sixty, they read the *terumah* of the Todah as meaning one tenth — establishing that there were ten loaves of each type. Once ten is established for the lachmei Todah, it becomes the basis for the rule governing all menachot: *mah le-halan eser af kan eser* — just as there each type came in ten, so too here each type of mincha comes in ten. The Gemara then raises the obvious challenge — *veyelef mi-lechem hapanim?* — why not instead derive the general rule from the lechem hapanim and rule that everything comes in twelve? The Gemara's answer, *mistavra mi-lachmei Todah havah leih lemilaf* — it makes more logical sense to derive from the lachmei Todah — is grounded in the number of points of similarity between an ordinary mincha and the lachmei Todah, a comparison the Gemara now proceeds to develop.

### Mincha & Toda Comparison

The Gemara establishes first the basis for the chavitei Kohen Gadol being twelve. By the lechem hapanim the Torah uses the word *chok*: *kodesh kodashim hu lo me'ishei Hashem chok olam*. By the chavitei Kohen Gadol it says: *chok olam la-Hashem kalil tuktir*. The shared term *chok* constitutes a gezeirah shavah — *atya chukah chukah millechem hapanim* — from which we derive that just as the lechem hapanim came in twelve, so too the chavitei Kohen Gadol come in twelve.

### Lechem Hapanim Model

Having established twelve for the lechem hapanim and, through the gezeirah shavah, for the chavitei Kohen Gadol, the Gemara asks: *kol hamenachot de'ba'ot eser eser minalan* — what is the source for the general rule of ten? *gamar milachmei todah* — we derive it from the breads of the Todah offering. *mah le'halan eser af kan eser* — just as each type of bread there came in ten loaves, so too here each type of bread comes in ten.

The source that lachmei Toda themselves came in ten is derived from the Torah's language. The Torah describes four types of bread accompanying the Todah — three types of matzah and one type of chametz — and says that one of each type is to be given to the Kohen as a *terumah*, a gift. The term *terumah* appears elsewhere in the context of *terumas ma'aser*, where the Levites give a tenth of their tithe to the Kohen, and there the Torah explicitly calls that tenth a *terumah*. From this, Chazal understood that the *terumah* separated from the Todah breads likewise represents one-tenth — meaning each type must have comprised ten loaves in order for one of them to constitute a *terumah*. Once the lachmei Toda are established as coming in ten, that number serves as the paradigm for all other menachot.

### Mincha & Lechem Panim

The Gemara raises an obvious question: we have two available models — the lachmei Toda at ten and the lechem hapanim at twelve. Why does Rabbi Yehuda choose the lachmei Toda as the paradigm and treat twelve as the exception, rather than making the lechem hapanim the rule and treating ten as the exception? *mah le'halan shteim esrei af kan shteim esrei* — why not derive that everything comes in twelve?

The Gemara entertains the possibility of using the lechem hapanim as the model, and in doing so surfaces a counter-argument: there are meaningful ways in which a normal mincha resembles the lechem hapanim that it does not share with lachmei Toda. Two stand out in particular. First, *shekein hekdesh* — both a normal mincha and the lechem hapanim are kodesh kodashim, eaten by the Kohanim inside the Beis HaMikdash. The lachmei Toda, by contrast, occupy a genuinely unusual position: because they accompany a Todah offering, which is a shelamim and therefore kodashim kalim, the bread itself takes on that same status and is eaten outside the Mikdash in Yerushalayim. This is, as the lecturer notes, a striking and easily overlooked phenomenon — the lachmei Toda represent the one case of a bread or mincha brought in the context of the Beis HaMikdash that is nonetheless kodashim kalim, with only one loaf in ten going to the Kohen and the rest eaten by the be'alim. Second, *levonah* — both a normal mincha and the lechem hapanim have levonah, while the lachmei Toda do not. One could add further: *matzah*, in that a normal mincha and lechem hapanim are entirely matzah, whereas ten of the forty loaves of the Todah are chametz. And *ve'etzem* — both a normal mincha and the lechem hapanim are korbanot in their own right, whereas the lachmei Toda are merely an accompaniment to the animal korban, which is the primary offering. On these grounds, one might argue that the lechem hapanim is the more natural model. The Gemara, however, answers that the points of comparison favoring the lachmei Toda are more numerous — *hanehu nefishin*, those are more — a question taken up directly in the section that follows.

### Kohen Gadol Model

The Gemara answers: *mistavra milachmei Todah havei leih lemilaf* — it is more logical to derive from the lachmei Toda. The points of comparison between a normal mincha and the lachmei Toda, and the ways in which they differ from the lechem hapanim, are as follows. *sheken yachid* — both a normal mincha and lachmei Toda come as an individual's korban, whereas the lechem hapanim is a communal offering of the tzibbur. *hamitnadeiv* — both come through a voluntary vow, whereas the lechem hapanim is an obligatory offering. *shemen* — both have oil, whereas the lechem hapanim is baked without oil and is plain bread. *shelo b'shabbat* — neither overrides Shabbat, whereas the lechem hapanim, being a communal and fixed-time offering, does. *shelo b'tumah* — neither is brought in a state of tumah, again reflecting that both are personal and not fixed-time offerings, in contrast to the lechem hapanim.

But the Gemara does not let the matter rest there. The logic driving the preference for lachmei Toda is that it is an individual's offering — but one might try to finesse this by introducing a middle step. The lechem hapanim, after all, teaches us about the chavitin of the Kohen Gadol through a *gezeirah shavah*, and the chavitin of the Kohen Gadol is itself an individual's offering. Could we not, then, be *chozer u-melamed b'binyan av* — return and derive from the Kohen Gadol's mincha as a new paradigm? The chavitin of the Kohen Gadol comes in twelve, and since it is an individual personal korban, it would seem a more apt model for a normal mincha than the communal lechem hapanim. *neileif m'chavitei Kohen Gadol* — let us derive from the chavitin of the Kohen Gadol: *mah halan sheteim esrei af kan sheteim esrei* — just as there the count is twelve, so too here it is twelve.

The Gemara, however, pushes back: *mistavra mi-lachmei Todah havei lei lemilaf* — the lachmei Toda remains the more logical paradigm even over the chavitin of the Kohen Gadol. The argument that the Kohen Gadol's mincha is an individual offering is now neutralized, since the lachmei Toda is equally an individual offering. But the two still diverge in several critical respects. *hedyot* — both a normal mincha and the lachmei Toda are brought by a layperson, whereas the chavitin is brought exclusively by the Kohen Gadol himself. *she'nidva* — both come through a voluntary vow, whereas the Kohen Gadol's mincha is a daily obligation. *chatza'im* — neither is split in halves; a normal mincha and the lachmei Toda are each brought as a single whole unit, whereas the Kohen Gadol's mincha is divided, with half offered in the morning and half in the afternoon. *l'pigul* — both a normal mincha and the lachmei Toda are susceptible to pigul, because each has a distinct *matir*: by the lachmei Toda the pigul thought occurs at the zerikah of the blood of the accompanying animal, and by a normal mincha it occurs at the taking of the kometz. The chavitin of the Kohen Gadol, by contrast, is burned entirely — it has no matir in the technical sense of one act permitting a separate object, and therefore a pigul thought cannot take effect. *shelo b'shabbat v'shelo b'tumah* — both a normal mincha and the lachmei Toda, being personal and non-fixed-time offerings, do not override Shabbat and are not brought in a state of tumah, whereas the Kohen Gadol's daily mincha, like any fixed communal obligation, does override both.

### Kohen Gadol Similarities

The Gemara counters: *adrabah*, on the contrary — *milechem hapanim havei lei lemilaf* — one should derive from the lechem hapanim instead. Here are the ways in which a normal mincha resembles the lechem hapanim and differs from the lachmei Toda. *sheken hekdesh* — both a normal mincha and the lechem hapanim are kodesh kodashim, eaten by the Kohanim within the Beit HaMikdash. The lachmei Toda, by contrast, accompanies a Todah animal which is a shelamim — kodashim kalim — and is therefore itself eaten as kodashim kalim, outside the Mikdash in Yerushalayim. This is the one case in which a bread brought to the Beit HaMikdash has the status of kodashim kalim. *levonah* — both a normal mincha and the lechem hapanim have levonah, whereas the lachmei Toda, accompanying the animal offering, has none. *matzah* — both are entirely matzah, whereas ten of the forty Todah loaves are chametz. *ve'etzem* — both come as independent offerings in their own right, whereas the lachmei Toda is merely an accompaniment to the animal; the animal is the primary korban. Because they come independently, both a normal mincha and the lechem hapanim share additional points of comparison that flow from that status: *hagashah* — both require touching the mincha to the mizbeach, a rite the lachmei Toda does not perform; and *ishim* — a portion of both is actually burned on the fire, again in contrast to the lachmei Toda, which as a mere accompaniment to the animal is not brought to the fire in this way. Given all of these parallels, the Gemara's point is striking: when you count up the similarities between a normal mincha and the chavitei Kohen Gadol versus a normal mincha and the lachmei Toda, the Kohen Gadol model actually yields a longer list — a consideration the Gemara will weigh directly in the following step.

### Chosen Model Rationale

The Gemara resolves the tension: *hanehu nefishin* — the first list has more points of comparison. The lachmei Toda model is supported by five points while the lechem hapanim model has four, and therefore the lachmei Toda serves as the paradigm.

### R. Meir Counters

The Gemara now raises a more sophisticated challenge. The chavitei Kohen Gadol were learned from the lechem hapanim via the gezeirah shavah of *chukah-chukah*. The question is whether *davar halamed bigezeirah shavah chozer umelamed bibinyan av* — can something that was itself derived through a gezeirah shavah then be used as a paradigm to teach other cases through binyan av? If yes, then one need not use the lechem hapanim directly; one can use the chavitei Kohen Gadol — which is an individual's offering rather than a communal one — as the model, and its many points of comparison with a normal mincha would seem to make it a superior paradigm.

But even if one holds that something learned through a gezeirah shavah cannot then turn around and serve as a paradigm for a binyan av — ruling the chavitei Kohen Gadol out as a model — R. Meir would still prefer the lechem hapanim over the lachmei Toda. The reason is *hekdesh me-hekdesh adif lei*: he prefers to learn from one kodshei kodashim offering to another. A normal mincha is kodshei kodashim, and so is the lechem hapanim, whereas the lachmei Toda are only kodashim kalim and may carry an altogether different status. One could add a further consideration along the same lines: the lachmei Toda are an accompaniment to an animal offering and are not brought as an independent korban in their own right, whereas both a normal mincha and the lechem hapanim stand as independent offerings on their own account — and R. Meir finds that structural parallel more compelling than any of the points the lachmei Toda model can offer.

### R. Meir's Exceptions

The Gemara spells out why the chavitei Kohen Gadol might be a better model than the lachmei Toda. *sheken issaron* — both the chavitei Kohen Gadol and a normal mincha consist of a single issaron of flour, whereas a Todah brings a much larger quantity. *kli* — both attain their full kedushah through a kli sharet, a sacred vessel, whereas the lachmei Toda attain their full kedushah only through the slaughtering of the accompanying animal, since the Todah is a shelamim and the slaughter of the animal functions as the act of haktrava for the bread. *hekdesh* — both are kodesh kodashim, whereas the lachmei Toda is kodashim kalim. *levonah* — both have levonah, as they are brought as independent offerings. *matzah* — both are entirely matzah. *ve'atzmam* — both come as independent offerings on their own account. *hagasha* — both require the act of hagasha, touching the mincha to the mizbeach, a requirement that does not apply to lachmei Toda which merely accompanies an animal. *ve'ishim* — portions of both are actually burned on the altar's fire, again unlike the lachmei Toda. This is a considerably longer list than what the lachmei Toda model offered, and the Gemara acknowledges: *vehanach nefishen* — these points are more numerous.

A separate dimension of comparison introduced here concerns *pigul*. Both a normal mincha and lachmei Toda are susceptible to pigul — the mincha through an improper thought during the taking of the kometz, and the lachmei Toda through an improper thought during the sprinkling of the blood of the accompanying animal, which serves as the *matir* for the bread. The chavitei Kohen Gadol, however, is entirely burned on the altar — it has no *matir* in the sense of one act permitting a separate item — and therefore the mechanism of pigul does not apply to it. Similarly, both a normal mincha and lachmei Toda are personal, non-fixed-time offerings and therefore do not override Shabbat or tumah, while the chavitei Kohen Gadol does.

### Shmuel's Leniency

Despite the longer list of comparisons favoring the chavitei Kohen Gadol as the paradigm, the Gemara concludes: *hedyot mehedyot adif leh* — the fact that both the lachmei Toda and a normal mincha are lay offerings, brought by ordinary Israelites rather than by the Kohen Gadol, is a more fundamental and persuasive point of comparison. Even a longer list of technical similarities cannot outweigh the categorical difference between a korban of a *hedyot* and a korban of the Kohen Gadol. It is preferable to derive the rule for a lay offering from another lay offering. This is Rabbi Yehuda's position.

### Teruma Problem

The Gemara now turns to Rabbi Meir, who holds that the general rule is twelve. *mai kasavar* — what does he maintain? Two possibilities are presented. If Rabbi Meir holds *davar halamed bigezeirah shavah chozer umelamed bibinyan av* — that something derived via gezeirah shavah can subsequently be used as a binyan av — then he would derive his rule of twelve from the chavitei Kohen Gadol, using that as his paradigm and relying on the longer list of points of comparison established above. If, however, he holds that something derived via gezeirah shavah cannot be used as a binyan av, then he cannot use the chavitei Kohen Gadol as his model, since it was itself derived from the lechem hapanim through the *chukah-chukah* gezeirah shavah. In that case, Rabbi Meir would derive his rule of twelve directly from the lechem hapanim. His rationale: *hekdesh mehekdesh adif lei* — just as Rabbi Yehuda argues that it is preferable to derive the rule for a lay offering from another lay offering (*hedyot mehedyot*), Rabbi Meir argues the mirror principle: it is preferable to derive the rule for a korban that is kodesh kodashim from another korban that is kodesh kodashim. A normal mincha is kodesh kodashim; the lechem hapanim is kodesh kodashim; this categorical kinship outweighs the advantage the lachmei Toda model offers through the *hedyot* comparison, since the lachmei Toda are kodashim kalim.

### Separation Solution

Having established twelve as the general rule, Rabbi Meir acknowledges the two explicit exceptions. *chutz milachmei toda u'nezirut de'atyan eser eser* — the breads of the Todah and of the Nazir come in ten. For the lachmei Toda, *be'hedya ketiv bei* — it is as if written explicitly, through the derivation from the word *terumah* that we have already seen. The Gemara notes with characteristic irony that calling it *be'hedya* ("explicitly written") is somewhat generous, given that the number ten is derived from the word *terumah* by way of comparison to *terumas ma'aser* — but since this is an established *drasha*, it is treated as though it were explicit. As for nezirut, the Gemara explains: we learn the laws of the Nazir's breads from the lachmei Toda because the Torah uses the superfluous phrase *al zevach todat shelamav* when describing the Todah. The word *shelamim* is redundant — it could have simply said *al zevach hatodah* — and so *lerabot shlamei nazir*, the word *shelamim* is there to include the shelamim of a Nazir. From this inclusion we derive that many of the laws of the Todah apply to the Nazir's offering, including the number of loaves: ten.

## Number Not Meakev

### Baraitot on Meakev

Having established the positions of Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Meir regarding the required number of loaves, the Gemara turns to the question of *bedi'avad* — what is the status after the fact when the prescribed number was not achieved? Rav Tuvi bar Kisna said in the name of Shmuel: *lachmei Toda she'afan arba chalot yatza* — if one baked the breads of the Todah as four loaves — one of each of the four types — rather than the required ten of each type, one has fulfilled the obligation after the fact. The full quantity of flour is used but shaped into one enormous loaf of each variety rather than ten. The number ten is *le'chat'chila*; *bedi'avad* it is not indispensable.

### Toda Number Meakev

A difficulty arises immediately. The Torah requires that one complete loaf from each of the four types be given to the Kohen as *terumah*. If one has only one loaf of each type, how can one give the Kohen a complete loaf while still retaining the remaining loaves for oneself? One cannot give him a broken-off piece, because the Torah's use of the word *echad* — one — is understood to require a complete, unbroken loaf: *shelo yitol paros*. And one cannot give the Kohen the entire loaf of that type, because that would eliminate the character of *terumah*, which by definition involves separating one portion from the rest.

But before addressing that structural problem, the Gemara must first confront a tension in the sources themselves. The baraita cited earlier in the discussion of *me'akev* specifically includes *d'lachmei Todah u'Nezirut* — the breads of the Todah and of the Nazirite — among those cases where the number ten is indispensable. The presumed reason is that for *lachmei Todah* the Torah states the number ten explicitly, and the Nazirite's loaves are derived from that same verse, rather than being inferred by analogy as in the other cases. This stands in apparent tension with Shmuel's ruling that baking four loaves rather than forty suffices after the fact. The Gemara resolves the tension by invoking a debate in the baraita itself: *kol haminachot sheriba bemidat challatan o shemi'eit bemidat challatan k'sherot* — any of the *menachot* where one has more or fewer than ten loaves is valid after the fact — *chutz milechem hapanim vachavitei Kohen Gadol*, except for the *lechem hapanim* and the Kohen Gadol's pan-offering. According to this view, *lachmei Todah* falls on the permissive side of the line. A second opinion, *v'yesh omrim*, goes further and explicitly includes even *lachmei Todah u'Nezirut* among those cases where deviation is acceptable. Shmuel, then, aligns with this more lenient position. The question of whether the number ten is truly *me'akev* by *lachmei Todah* remains a genuine dispute: those who hold it is *me'akev* may reason that the explicit scriptural statement of the number carries special force, though as will emerge from the Gemara's next move, there may be an additional structural reason to treat the Todah's number as categorically binding — a question the gemara will address shortly.

### R. Huna on One Loaf

The resolution offered is: *d'afrishinhu b'lisha* — one separates the Kohen's portion during the kneading of the dough, before baking. When preparing each type, one sets aside a portion of the dough at the kneading stage and bakes it separately. This separate piece then constitutes the complete loaf given to the Kohen, while the main mass of dough is baked as the owner's portion. The result is that one does not in fact end up with exactly four loaves, since one has baked the Kohen's portions separately; one ends up with eight distinct pieces. But the essential point — that the Kohen receives an intact, complete portion — is preserved.

### R. Papa Counters

The Gemara now cites a Braita with a broader ruling: *kol haminachot sheriba bemidat challatan o shemi'eit bemidat challatan kesherot* — any of the menachot for which one made more than the prescribed number of loaves, or fewer, is still valid. The prescribed number is a *mitzvah* — it is the *le'chat'chila* ideal — but it is not *me'akev* for most menachot. The exceptions are the lechem hapanim and the chavitei Kohen Gadol, for which twelve is indispensable. This is presumably because the *chukah* language of the gezeirah shavah implies a strict requirement, and in the case of the lechem hapanim in particular, the physical arrangement of twelve loaves on the shulchan cannot be achieved with any other number.

Rav Papa, however, challenges this framework. His objection centers on the word *matzat* — the unleavened offering — as it appears in the context of the *ma'afeh tanur*, the oven-baked minchah. The word is written deficiently, without a *vav*, which is what allows R. Huna's position that one may bake as few as one loaf. Had it been written *matzot* with a *vav*, one would have read it as demanding the full complement. The difficulty is that by the breads of the Todah, the full spelling *matzot* does appear — and yet Shmuel ruled that if one baked only four loaves of the Todah offering, one has nonetheless fulfilled the obligation *bediavad*. This is an apparent contradiction: the very spelling that R. Huna treats as the source for leniency is absent in the Todah case, and yet leniency applies there too. So Rav Papa's challenge stands not only against R. Huna but, more broadly, against the Tannaitic baraitot that were just cited, which had stated as a general rule that all loaves — with the exception of lechem hapanim, nezirus, and lachmei toda — may be done with fewer than the required number. R. Huna's position, by restricting the leniency to the *ma'afeh tanur* alone, is in tension with those earlier Tannaim. Tosafot resolves this by suggesting that once one derives the leniency from the deficient spelling of *matzat* in the *ma'afeh tanur*, one extends that principle by extrapolation to the other menachot as well — the *ma'afeh tanur* verse serves as the anchor from which the broader permissibility is learned. Whether that reconciliation fully satisfies is a matter the reader may weigh, but in any case the upshot of the sugya to this point is clear: the general rule is that ten loaves is the standard, with certain cases requiring twelve — or, viewed from the other direction, twelve is the standard with certain exceptions of ten — and *bediavad*, if one produces any amount, one has likely fulfilled the obligation, with the possible exceptions of lechem hapanim and perhaps lachmei toda as well. With that, the Gemara moves on to the final ingredient in the recipe — the *omer* — which will carry the discussion into the next perek.

### Omer Ratio

The Braita adds two further exceptions: *ve'yesh omrim af lachmei Toda u'nezirus* — some say that even the breads of the Todah and the Nazir must be ten, and this too is *me'akev*. The basis for treating the ten as *me'akev* in these cases — given that the number is derived from *terumah* rather than from an explicit *chukah* formulation — is a matter of some difficulty. It may be that since the number is derived from an explicit textual comparison rather than purely by analogy, it is treated as though the Torah stated it directly.

### Shtei Halechem Ratio

The Gemara explains Shmuel's ruling — that one is *yotzei* with only four loaves of lachmei Toda — by noting that Shmuel follows a Tanna who does not include the lachmei Toda and nezirut among the *me'akev* exceptions. The Braita in question reads: *kol haminachot sheriba bemidat challatan o shemi'eit bemidat challatan kesherot — chutz milechem hapanim vachavitei Kohen Gadol* — excluding only the lechem hapanim and the chavitei Kohen Gadol, but not the lachmei Toda. Shmuel aligns with this Tanna and therefore rules that four loaves of lachmei Toda are valid after the fact.

### Lechem Hapanim Ratio

Rav Huna offers a more restricted ruling: *mincha ma'afeh she'afah challah achat yatza* — if one baked the oven-baked mincha as a single loaf rather than the prescribed ten, one has nonetheless fulfilled the obligation. His basis is a textual observation: the word in the Torah is *matzat* — spelled without the letter *vav* — which can be read as a singular form, indicating that *bedi'avad* even one *matzah* suffices. Rav Huna limits this ruling to the *mincha ma'afeh* specifically, based on this *pasuk*, and is not prepared to extend it to all menachot on the basis of the broader Braita.

### Ratio Explanation

Rav Pappa challenges this line of reasoning. If the logic is that *matzat* without a *vav* permits a reduced number, then by the breads of the Todah the Torah writes *matzot* — with a *vav* — and yet Shmuel rules that even four loaves are valid there. The presence of the full plural spelling *matzot* would, on Rav Huna's reasoning, imply that the full number is required, not that a reduced number is acceptable. This constitutes a challenge to Shmuel's ruling from Rav Huna's logic: if spelling matters in one direction, it ought to matter in the other. Tosafot reconciles this by suggesting that once Rav Huna derives *bedi'avad* validity from the defective spelling in *mincha ma'afeh*, one may extrapolate that principle broadly to all menachot — and from that extrapolation one could support Shmuel's position as well.

The Mishnah then establishes that the ratio of starting grain to finished sifted flour differs across the three major communal flour-based offerings, and it is worth pausing to understand why the ratios descend as they do rather than remaining uniform. The *Omer* has the highest ratio — ten to one — because, as the gemara explains, *keivan d'michadsha ati umise'orin ati* — it comes from newly harvested grain and it comes from barley. Both factors drive the ratio up: freshly harvested grain has not had time to dry out, so there is proportionally far more bran, hulls, and *psolet* — dross — that must be removed, and barley in any case contains significantly more *psolet* than wheat. The *shtei halechem*, by contrast, require only a five-to-one ratio — two *esronos* from three *se'im* — because, as the gemara notes, *keivan d'michitin atyan* — they come from wheat — which is an intrinsically better grain for milling, even though they too are brought from the new harvest. The two factors thus partially offset each other, and the ratio is halved accordingly. The *lechem hapanim* achieves the lowest ratio of all, roughly three and a third to one, because it has two favorable factors working simultaneously: *keivan d'michitin atu umiyashan atu* — it comes from wheat, and it comes from aged, dried-out grain. Unlike the *Omer* or the *shtei halechem*, the *lechem hapanim* may always be brought from grain of the previous year's harvest, grain that has been drying out over time and has shed much of its extraneous matter naturally. The result is that *isaron muvchar ati mise'ah* — a fine, well-sifted *isaron* can be obtained from a single *se'ah*. One should not be misled by the descending numbers in the Mishnah into thinking the later offerings require less care or effort; the lower ratio simply reflects the superior milling yield of their grain.

## Wheat-to-Flour Ratios

### Quality Clarification

The Mishnah now addresses the quantities of wheat required for the three major communal flour-based offerings. The Omer requires one issaron of flour — approximately a five-pound bag of flour by rough modern equivalence — and the starting wheat for this is three *se'in*. Three *se'in* constitute one *eifah*, and one *eifah* equals ten *issaronim* (the issaron being one-tenth of an eifah, as the Torah states: *asirit ha'eifah*). The ratio of starting wheat to finished flour is therefore ten to one.

One might be tempted to read this high ratio as evidence that the Omer demands a superior quality of flour — that the refinement standard is simply more exacting. That is not the correct inference. The ratio is high not because a stricter grade of whiteness or fineness is imposed, but because the raw wheat itself requires a larger quantity of starting material to yield the same finished product. The quality standard and the yield ratio are separate matters, and conflating them would be a mistake.

It is also worth pausing on what the word *issaron* means in practice across these different offerings, because the same unit functions very differently depending on the context. In an ordinary *mincha*, one *issaron* of flour is divided into ten loaves — the single measure is parceled out among multiple portions. By contrast, for the *shtei halechem* each individual loaf receives its own full *issaron*, and for the *lechem hapanim* two *issaronim* go into each individual loaf. The unit of measurement is identical, but in the case of the *mincha* that one measure is being spread across ten loaves, whereas in these communal offerings the same measure — or even twice that measure — constitutes a single, substantially larger loaf. The *issaron* as a label stays constant; what changes dramatically is how much bread it ultimately represents per piece.

### Sieve Numbers

The shtei halechem requires two *esronim* of flour — one issaron per loaf — also from three *se'in* of wheat. Since three *se'in* yield two *esronim*, the ratio here is five to one, exactly half that of the Omer.

The Mishnah then turns from quantities of starting wheat to the number of sieves used in processing each offering. The *Omer* was sifted through thirteen sieves, the *shtei halechem* through twelve, and the *lechem hapanim* through eleven. The logic of the decreasing sieve count tracks the decreasing ratios of starting wheat: more starting wheat relative to finished flour means more bran to remove, which demands more sifting. What is surprising, however, is how small the difference actually is. Given how dramatically the wheat-to-flour ratios diverge across the three offerings — ten to one, five to one, and roughly three and one-third to one — a difference of only a single sieve between each offering seems almost disproportionately modest. The Gemara does not offer a direct explanation for this, and it is worth flagging as genuinely puzzling. One partial answer the Gemara does provide is that the *lechem hapanim* was made from older grain, which by its nature required less sifting — a point that addresses the gap between the *shtei halechem* and the *lechem hapanim* specifically, though it does not fully account for why the overall spread across all three offerings is so narrow. The sieves themselves, it should be noted, were not simply the same implement used repeatedly: each successive sieve had progressively finer holes, so that each pass removed something the previous one had not, making each additional sieve a genuinely distinct stage of refinement rather than mere repetition.

### R. Shimon Opinion

The lechem hapanim requires 24 *esronim* — two *esronim* per loaf for each of the twelve loaves, as the Torah states explicitly: *shtei esronim yihyeh hachalah ha'achat* — from 24 *se'in* of wheat. Since 24 *se'in* is eight *eifot*, which is 80 *issaronim*, the ratio of starting wheat to finished flour is approximately 80 to 24, or roughly three and one-third to one. The ratio thus decreases progressively: ten to one for the Omer, five to one for the shtei halechem, and approximately three and one-third to one for the lechem hapanim.

### Alternating Sieves

The Gemara explains why the ratios differ. For the Omer: *keivan d'michadsha ati* — it comes from newly harvested grain, *u'mise'orin ati* — and from barley. Two factors combine to require a high ratio. First, newly harvested grain that has not yet dried out retains considerably more bran and chaff, requiring proportionally more starting material to produce a given quantity of fine flour. Second, barley itself has substantially more *psolet* — dross, hulls, and waste — than wheat. For these two reasons, *issaron muvchar lo ati ela mishalosh se'in* — a fine, high-quality issaron of solet cannot be obtained from less than three *se'in*, yielding the ten-to-one ratio.

### Sieve Purposes

The shtei halechem comes from newly harvested grain as well — these are the two loaves of the new wheat crop brought on Shavuot — but it is wheat rather than barley. The two factors therefore partially cancel: newly harvested grain increases the ratio, but wheat rather than barley cuts it in half. *af al gav d'michadsha atyan* — although they come from new grain, *keivan d'michitin atyan* — since they come from wheat, the ratio is halved, and two *esronim* can be obtained from three *se'in*.

The Gemara's discussion of sifting ratios presupposes a particular sieving process worth pausing to understand. The sieves used in this milling procedure come in two types that work in opposite directions. A thin sieve — one with very small holes — is designed to remove fine impurities like sand or dust: the unwanted particles are small enough to fall through while the flour grains themselves are retained above. A thick sieve — one with large holes — works the opposite way: the grain falls through the larger openings while the coarser bran and husks, being too big to pass, remain on top. The key to the alternating process is that neither a single thin sieve nor a single thick sieve is sufficient on its own, because any fixed hole size will catch only impurities within a particular size range. As Rashi explains, the sieves are arranged so that the hole sizes converge progressively — the wide-holed sieves become incrementally narrower and the narrow-holed sieves become incrementally wider — so that at each successive stage something more is removed than was removed at the stage before. The result is a graduated filtration that strips away the full spectrum of *psolet* from the coarsest bran down to the finest particulate, yielding the refined *solet* that all these offerings require.

### Progressive Method

The lechem hapanim benefits from two favorable factors simultaneously. *keivan d'michitin atu* — it comes from wheat, and *umiyashan atu* — it comes from aged, dried grain. The lechem hapanim, unlike the Omer or shtei halechem, need not come from the newly harvested crop; it can be brought from whatever wheat is available at any time of year, and older grain that has dried out yields a much higher proportion of fine flour. With both favorable factors in play, *issaron muvchar ati mise'ah* — a fine issaron can be obtained from a single *se'ah*, yielding the lowest ratio of approximately three and one-third to one.

An important clarification follows: one should not infer from the higher starting ratio of the Omer that the standard of flour quality required for the Omer is higher. The end product — a fine issaron of solet — is the same in all cases. The higher ratio simply reflects the inferior starting material: more wheat must be processed to produce the same quality of finished flour. The quality of the result is identical; what differs is how much raw material is needed to achieve it.

### Buy Flour

A Braita makes this distinction explicit and gives it halachic force: *kol hamenachot sheriba bemidat isronam o shemi'eit bemidat isronam pesulot* — any mincha for which the quantity of finished flour was more or less than the prescribed issaron is invalid. The issaron is *me'akev*. However, *riba bemidat se'im shelahen o shemi'eit bemidat se'im shelahen kesherot* — if one started with more or less wheat than the prescribed three *se'in* but still arrived at the correct issaron of fine flour, the mincha is valid. The ratios of starting wheat to flour are provided as practical guidance for achieving the correct quantity of solet, not as independent requirements. What must be precise is the finished product; the process of arriving there carries latitude.

A second Braita extends this latitude further, deriving from the verse *velakachta solet ve'afita ota* — "and you shall take fine flour and bake it" — that one may actually purchase solet already prepared from the marketplace rather than sifting it oneself. And if that is permitted, a further derivation from *velakachta* — "and you shall take" — establishes that one may even purchase the wheat itself from the marketplace rather than processing it from scratch. This is, as the lecturer notes, a striking ruling, particularly given the general expectation of rigorous, hands-on oversight in matters pertaining to the Mikdash. We encountered something analogous earlier with respect to checking a korban for a mum: one may rely on a middleman who certifies that the animal was inspected for the requisite four days. Here too, the sifting presumably still needs to be done to the proper standard, but one may trust another party to have done it — a degree of reliance on others that might seem surprising in the context of Temple service. The lecturer observes that at least with flour, one can to some extent verify the results by inspection; the finished solet either meets the standard or it does not. The number of sieves used in the sifting process is taken up in the final Mishnah of the daf, discussed in the next section.

## Sifting Process

### Buy Wheat

The final Mishnah of the daf addresses the sifting process. The Omer was sifted through thirteen sieves. The shtei halechem was sifted through twelve. The lechem hapanim through eleven. The numbers decrease as the ratio of starting material to finished flour decreases — which is intuitive, since the inferior starting material of the Omer (new barley) requires more processing to achieve the same quality of solet. What is somewhat striking is that the differences between the three offerings are each only one sieve, even as the differences in the starting wheat ratios are quite dramatic. The Gemara does not offer an explicit explanation of this; the numbers appear to carry a degree of symbolic or conventional character.

One may also note that the Mishnah specifies numbers for these three communal offerings but does not prescribe a fixed number of sieves for an ordinary personal mincha. This reflects a broader pattern: the communal offerings were subject to precise, standardized procedures, whereas the personal mincha was held to a standard of *solet* — fine flour — without the same degree of procedural specification.

### Lechem Exception

Rabbi Shimon disagrees with the fixed numbers entirely: *lo haya lahen kitzva ela solet menufa kol tzorcha haya mevi* — there was no fixed number of sieves; rather, one would bring finely sifted flour sifted as much as necessary. His source is the verse *ve'lakachta solet ve'afita otah* — you shall take solet and you shall bake it. Rabbi Shimon reads a play on *ve'afita* — connecting it to the concept of being well-sifted (*menufa*) — to indicate that what matters is achieving sufficiently fine flour, not the specific number of sieves used. This is arguably the more logically coherent position: if the goal is *solet*, then one sieves until *solet* is achieved, whatever number of passes that requires.

### Expense Principle

A Braita describes the order of sifting: *b'daka b'gasa b'daka b'gasa* — one alternates between a fine-holed sieve and a coarse-holed sieve. The two types of sieves serve opposite purposes. The fine-holed sieve is designed to allow small particles — sand, dust, and fine debris — to pass through while retaining the grain; in this way, the fine *psolet* falls out below. The coarse-holed sieve is designed to allow the grain to pass through while retaining large particles such as bran above. By alternating the two types and progressively adjusting the sizes of the holes — the fine holes becoming progressively finer and the coarse holes becoming progressively coarser — more and more of the waste material is removed at each stage until only pure solet remains.

### Shimon b Elazar Sieves

Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar — a distinct figure from Rabbi Shimon of the Mishnah — describes a different arrangement: *shlosha asar nefot hayu bamikdash, zu lemala mizu vezu lemala mizu* — there were thirteen sieves in the Temple, each one arranged above the other, *elyona koletet subin vetachtona koletet solet* — the topmost sieve catches the bran, and the bottommost catches the solet. On Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar's reading, rather than alternating fine and coarse sieves, the thirteen sieves are arranged in a descending series from largest holes to smallest, so that the grain passes progressively through ever-finer sieving, with the bran caught at the top and the finest solet collected at the bottom. This is a single hierarchical system rather than an alternating back-and-forth process.

### Torah Saves Money

A Braita addresses the question of how the flour was obtained. The verse *ve'lakachta solet ve'afita otah* — you shall take solet and bake it — teaches that *solet* may be purchased directly from the marketplace. One need not produce the flour oneself from scratch; purchasing pre-sifted flour from a vendor is permitted. Furthermore, *u'minayin she'afilu chitin* — how do we know one may even purchase raw wheat rather than flour? *talmud lomar ve'lakachta* — the word *ve'lakachta*, "you shall take," indicates that one may acquire the grain in whatever form it comes, even as unprocessed wheat.

However, *yachol af bish'ar menachot ken* — one might have thought that this latitude to purchase raw wheat applies to all menachot. The Braita implies that for other menachot it does not apply — for them one must purchase flour rather than unprocessed wheat — and the reason offered is *mipnei hachisachon*, because of the financial saving. The Gemara asks: *mai mipnei hachisachon* — what does this mean?

### Torah: Mammon Yisrael

Rabbi Elazar explains: *haTorah chasah al mamonam shel Yisrael* — the Torah is concerned about the financial expenditure of Israel. Specially sifted and processed flour, of the quality required for the Mikdash, carries a significant premium — particularly for the lechem hapanim, which required 24 *esronim* of solet and was brought every week. Purchasing that quantity of premium flour from the marketplace week after week would be enormously expensive. Therefore, the Torah permits purchasing raw wheat for the lechem hapanim so that the processing can be done in-house, reducing the cost. The permission to purchase wheat rather than finished flour is specifically calibrated to cases where the financial burden would otherwise be prohibitive.

The principle *haTorah chasah al mamonam shel Yisrael* — the Torah is solicitous of the financial resources of Israel — is a significant and recurring principle in halacha. Its source, as cited here, is the verse *ve'hishkita et ha'eda ve'et be'iram* — and you shall give the congregation and their animals to drink — where concern for communal resources is explicit in the Divine instruction to Moshe. The principle plays an important practical role across many areas of halacha, frequently supporting more lenient rulings where strict application would impose unnecessary financial hardship.
