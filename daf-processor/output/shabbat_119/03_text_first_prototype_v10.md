## Kavod & Oneg Shabbat



### Previous Kavod Shabbat

We pick up on 119b, six lines down in the wide lines. The previous daf opened a rich discussion centered on *kavod Shabbat* and *oneg Shabbat* — the twin obligations of honoring and enjoying the Shabbat. A central theme was that it is not enough merely to have preparations completed; the *Rishonim* and *Amoraim* emphasize the importance of personal involvement in the act of preparation itself, as one would invest in receiving an important guest. There was also a nuanced discussion about dress during the preparation process — whether one should don fine clothing befitting the day or more workmanlike attire suited to the physical labor of preparing. Even the question of appropriate dress for one's *kavod Shabbat* preparations is treated as a substantive issue rather than a mere aside. This is the broader context within which the following discussion unfolds: the laws of eating and honoring Shabbat.

### Yosef Mokir Shabbat

The famous *aggadata* of *Yosef Mokir Shabbat* — Yosef who honors Shabbat — was treated in detail on the previous daf. The story illustrates not only the reward for honoring Shabbat but the spiritual logic behind personal investment in its observance: when a person throws himself into the preparations with genuine enthusiasm, it reflects an internalized appreciation of Shabbat's sanctity that no mere delegation of tasks can replicate.

## Fire & Sabbath Violation



### Fire & Chillul Shabbat

*Amar Rav Yehuda berei de-Rav Shmuel mishmei de-Rav*: Rav Yehuda the son of Rav Shmuel said in the name of Rav: *ein hadlaka metzuya bemakom she-yesh chillul Shabbat* — fire is only prevalent in a place where Shabbat is desecrated. This statement ties directly into the broader discussion of fire breaking out on Shabbat, which is the formal topic of the *sugya* — namely, what food may be saved when a fire breaks out. Having established great praise for those who honor Shabbat, and having noted that if Israel were to keep two Shabbatot in full observance redemption would come immediately, the *Gemara* now turns to the rhetorical counterpart: the negative consequences of *chillul Shabbat*. The pivot is natural, as the very scenario of a fire on Shabbat invites the question of causation.

### Jeremiah Verse

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב שְׁמוּאֵל מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַב: אֵין הַדְּלֵיקָה מְצוּיָה אֶלָּא בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁיֵּשׁ חִילּוּל שַׁבָּת, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְאִם לֹא תִשְׁמְעוּ אֵלַי לְקַדֵּשׁ אֶת יוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת וּלְבִלְתִּי שְׂאֵת מַשָּׂא וְגוֹ׳ וְהִצַּתִּי אֵשׁ בִּשְׁעָרֶיהָ וְאָכְלָה אַרְמְנוֹת יְרוּשָׁלִַים וְלֹא תִכְבֶּה״. מַאי ״וְלֹא תִכְבֶּה״? אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁאֵין בְּנֵי אָדָם מְצוּיִין לְכַבּוֹתָהּ. אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: לָא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁחִלְּלוּ בָּהּ אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וּמִשַׁבְּתוֹתַי הֶעְלִימוּ עֵינֵיהֶם וָאֵחַל בְּתוֹכָם״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yehuda, son of Rav Shmuel, said in the name of Rav: Fire is only found in a place where there is desecration of Shabbat, as it is stated: “And if you do not heed Me to sanctify the day of Shabbat, and to refrain from carrying burdens** and come to the gates of Jerusalem on the day of Shabbat, **and I will light a fire in its gates and it will consume the palaces of Jerusalem and it will not be extinguished”** (Jeremiah 17:27). The Gemara asks: **What** is the meaning of: **And it will not be extinguished? Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said:** Fire will break out **at a time when people are not found to extinguish it. Abaye said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because people desecrated the Shabbat in it, as it is stated: “And from My *Shabbatot* they averted their eyes, and I was profaned among them”** (Ezekiel 22:26). Several punishments were decreed to befall Jerusalem as punishment for this transgression.

The source for Rav Yehuda's statement is the verse in Yirmiyahu: *ve-im lo tishme'u elai lekadesh es yom ha-Shabbat ve-lavitasei masa* — if you do not listen to Me to sanctify the Shabbat and to refrain from carrying burdens — *ve-hitzati esh be-sha'areha ve-achla armonot Yerushalayim velo tichbe* — then I will kindle a fire in its gates, and it will consume the towers of Jerusalem, and it will not be extinguished.

### Nachman: Fire Not Put Out

The *Gemara* asks: *mai velo tichbe* — what is the meaning of the phrase "it will not be extinguished"? *Amar Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak*: *be-sha'ah she-ein bnei adam metzuyin le-khabotah* — it will occur at a time when people are not around to put it out, so that it continues to burn. Presumably that time is Shabbat itself, when people are in *shul* and not positioned to fight a fire.

### Midda Kneged Midda

This constitutes a measure of *midda keneged midda* — measure for measure — for not keeping Shabbat. One might ask whether statements of this kind are meant literally as divine retribution, or metaphorically as rhetorical exhortation to greater observance. They are clearly punitive in nature, not descriptive. It is important to note the danger in applying this kind of rhetoric retroactively as a form of *onaat devarim* — causing pain through words — by blaming individuals or communities for tragedies that befall them. The *Gemara*'s intent is plainly to encourage observance, not to provide a framework for finger-pointing at those who suffer. The arrogance of claiming to know precisely how divine providence operates, combined with the cruelty of compounding someone's tragedy with blame, makes such after-the-fact attribution deeply problematic — even if the *Gemara* itself speaks in these terms as a hortatory device.

## Jerusalem's Destruction



### Abbaye: Shabbat Violation

*Amar Abbaye*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ella bishvil she-chillalu bah es ha-Shabbat* — Jerusalem was only destroyed because Shabbat was desecrated within it. The verse cited is: *umishabbotosai he'elimu eineihem* — from My Shabbatot they averted their eyes — *ve-achel betocham* — and I began destruction in their midst. The language of *ve-achel* here connects to the root of *le-hatchil*, beginning, the commencement of devastation.

### Rabbi Abbahu: Shema

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אֲבָהוּ: לֹא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁבִּיטְּלוּ קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע שַׁחֲרִית וְעַרְבִית, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״הוֹי מַשְׁכִּימֵי בַבֹּקֶר שֵׁכָר יִרְדֹּפוּ וְגוֹ׳״, וּכְתִיב: ״וְהָיָה כִנּוֹר וָנֶבֶל תּוֹף וְחָלִיל וָיַיִן מִשְׁתֵּיהֶם וְאֵת פּוֹעַל ה׳ לֹא יַבִּיטוּ״, וּכְתִיב: ״לָכֵן גָּלָה עַמִּי מִבְּלִי דָעַת״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests additional reasons for the destruction of Jerusalem.  **Rabbi Abbahu said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because** its citizens intentionally **omitted recitation of *Shema* morning and evening, as it is stated: “Woe to those who rise early in the morning and pursue the drink** and are aflame from wine until late in the evening” (Isaiah 5:11). **And it is written** in the continuation of that passage: **“And their drinking parties have lyre and lute, drum and flute and wine, and they do not look upon the actions of God,** and they do not see His hands’ creations” (Isaiah 5:12). This means that in the morning and evening, when the Jews should have been reciting *Shema*, they were drinking wine and liquor. **And it is written** in that passage: **“Therefore My nation is being exiled for its ignorance;** its honor will die of hunger and its multitudes will be parched with thirst” (Isaiah 5:13).

*Amar Rabbi Abbahu*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ella bishvil she-bitlu keri'at shema shachrit va-arvit* — Jerusalem was destroyed only because they ceased to recite the *shema* in the morning and in the evenings. The verse cited is: *hoi mashkimei vavoker shechar yirdofu* — woe to those who rise early in the morning to pursue intoxicating drink. The continuation of the passage reads: *ve-haya kinnor va-nevel ve-tof ve-chalil ve-yayin mishtehem* — and there was harp, lute, drum, flute, and wine at their feasts — *ve-et po'al Hashem lo yabitu* — but they do not look at the work of God. And then: *lachen gala ami mi-bli da'at* — therefore My people was exiled for lack of knowledge.

Rashi explains this last phrase with reference to the declaration of God's unity — *meyached shemo*, the proclamation of *Hashem Echad*. The broader reading, however, encompasses something more: a failure to perceive God's presence in the world, which is precisely what the *berakhot* surrounding *shema* in *shacharit* are about — *yotzer ha-me'orot*, the creation of light, *ma'aseh bereishit*, the ongoing creation. Instead of cultivating this awareness, the people pursued pleasure.

What is striking about this shift in the *Gemara* is the move to what might seem like a relatively minor infraction — neglecting *keriat shema* — in the context of explaining a national catastrophe. The point may be precisely that before one focuses on gross violations, it is worth identifying the smaller failures that are foundational to the entire religious orientation. A lack of that bedrock awareness of God in the world — the very thing *shema* is designed to cultivate morning and evening — is what ultimately opens the door to the larger spiritual and moral collapse that leads to destruction.

### Rav Hamnuna: Education

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב הַמְנוּנָא: לֹא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁבִּיטְּלוּ בָּהּ תִּינוֹקוֹת שֶׁל בֵּית רַבָּן, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״שְׁפוֹךְ עַל עוֹלָל בַּחוּץ וְגוֹ׳״. מַה טַּעַם ״שְׁפוֹךְ״ — מִשּׁוּם דְּ״עוֹלָל בַּחוּץ״. אָמַר עוּלָּא: לֹא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא מִפְּנֵי שֶׁלֹּא הָיָה לָהֶם בּוֹשֶׁת פָּנִים זֶה מִזֶּה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״הוֹבִישׁוּ כִּי תוֹעֵבָה עָשׂוּ גַּם בּוֹשׁ לֹא יֵבוֹשׁוּ וְגוֹ׳״. אָמַר רַבִּי יִצְחָק: לֹא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁהוּשְׁווּ קָטָן וְגָדוֹל, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְהָיָה כָעָם כַּכֹּהֵן״, וּכְתִיב בָּתְרֵיהּ: ״הִבּוֹק תִּבּוֹק הָאָרֶץ״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Hamnuna said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because schoolchildren there were interrupted** from studying Torah, **as it is stated:** “And I am filled with the wrath of God, I cannot contain it, **pour it onto the infants in the street** and onto the gathering of youths together, for men and women alike will be captured, the elderly along with those of advanced years” (Jeremiah 6:11). Rav Hamnuna explains: **What is the reason that** the wrath is **poured?** It is **because infants are outside** in the streets and are not studying Torah.  **Ulla said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because people had no shame before each other, as it is stated: “They acted shamefully; they have performed abominations, yet they neither were ashamed** nor did they know humiliation. Therefore, they will fall among the fallen, they will fail at the time that I punish them, said God” (Jeremiah 6:15).  **Rabbi Yitzḥak said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because** its **small and** the **great** citizens **were equated.** They did not properly value the prominent leaders of their generation, **as it is stated: “And the common people were like the priest,** the slave like his master, the maidservant like her mistress, the buyer like the seller, the lender like the borrower, the creditor like the one indebted to him” (Isaiah 24:2). **And it is written afterward: “The land shall be utterly desolate** and completely plundered, for God has said this” (Isaiah 24:3).

*Amar Rav Hamnuna*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ella bishvil she-bitlu bah tinokot shel beit rabban* — Jerusalem was destroyed because the schoolchildren who attended religious instruction were prevented from learning. The verse is: *shefoch al olel bachutz* — pour out wrath upon the suckling in the street. The *Gemara* interprets: *ma ta'am shefoch? Mishum de-olel bachutz* — why is wrath poured out? Because the children are outside rather than in school where they should be.

This too fits the pattern of the preceding statements: perhaps a community is diligent in observance, perhaps it respects authority, but if it does not invest sufficiently in the Jewish education of the next generation, it reveals a fundamental indifference to continuity and to the weight of Torah as the animating force of communal life. That indifference ultimately erodes everything else. It is worth noting that this teaching was eventually translated into practical law: the *Rambam* records in *Hilkhot Talmud Torah* that school children may not be dismissed from learning even for the purpose of building the *Beit HaMikdash* — a legal ruling that reflects how seriously the *Gemara* takes this principle.

### Ulla: Shame

*Amar Ulla*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ela mipnei she-lo haya lahem boshet panim ze mi-ze* — Jerusalem was destroyed because they had no shame before one another for acting improperly. The verse is: *hovishu ki to'evah asu, gam bosh lo yevoshu* — they should have been ashamed, for they committed an abomination, yet they are not ashamed. When a basic sense of shame for misconduct disappears from a society, the practical restraint that shame provides disappears with it. Here again the common denominator with the preceding statements becomes clear: these are not descriptions of some spectacular transgression, but of characterological and attitudinal failures — a way of being in the world that removes the internal and social brakes on sin.

### Rav Yitzchak: Status

*Amar Rav Yitzchak*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ella bishvil she-hushvu katan ve-gadol* — Jerusalem was destroyed because they equated the small with the great, failing to recognize and appropriately honor differences in stature and merit. The verse cited is: *ve-haya kha'am ka-kohen* — the people were like the *kohen* — indicating a total flattening of distinction between laity and religious leadership, a kind of radical equality that left no room even for meritocracy. The continuation — *ve-acharav ha-bok tibok* — signals the inevitable emptying out that follows.

### Rabbi Chanina: Tochacha

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב עַמְרָם בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בַּר אַבָּא, אָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בַּר אַבָּא, אָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: לֹא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁלֹּא הוֹכִיחוּ זֶה אֶת זֶה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״הָיוּ שָׂרֶיהָ כְּאַיָּלִים לֹא מָצְאוּ מִרְעֶה״. מָה אַיִל זֶה, רֹאשׁוֹ שֶׁל זֶה בְּצַד זְנָבוֹ שֶׁל זֶה, אַף יִשְׂרָאֵל שֶׁבְּאוֹתוֹ הַדּוֹר כָּבְשׁוּ פְּנֵיהֶם בַּקַּרְקַע וְלֹא הוֹכִיחוּ זֶה אֶת זֶה. אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: לֹא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁבִּיזּוּ בָּהּ תַּלְמִידֵי חֲכָמִים, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַיִּהְיוּ מַלְעִיבִים בְּמַלְאֲכֵי הָאֱלֹהִים וּבוֹזִים דְּבָרָיו וּמִתַּעְתְּעִים בִּנְבִיאָיו עַד עֲלוֹת חֲמַת ה׳ בְּעַמּוֹ עַד [לְ]אֵין מַרְפֵּא״. מַאי ״עַד לְאֵין מַרְפֵּא״? אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה אָמַר רַב: כׇּל הַמְבַזֶּה תַּלְמִידֵי חֲכָמִים אֵין לוֹ רְפוּאָה לְמַכָּתוֹ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Amram, son** of **Rabbi Shimon bar Abba, said** that **Rabbi Shimon bar Abba said** that **Rabbi Ḥanina said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because the people did not rebuke one another, as it is stated: “Her ministers were like stags that found no pasture,** and they walked without strength before their pursuer” (Lamentations 1:6). **Just as this stag** turns **its head toward the other’s tail** when it grazes, and each one feeds on its own, **so too, the Jewish people in that generation lowered their faces to the ground and did not rebuke one another.**  **Rabbi Yehuda said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because they disparaged the Torah scholars in it, as it is stated: “And they mocked the messengers of God and disdained His words and taunted His prophets, until the wrath of God arose against His people, until it could not be healed”** (II Chronicles 36:16). **What** is the meaning of: **Until it could not be healed? Rav Yehuda said** that **Rav said:** It means that **anyone who disparages Torah scholars cannot be healed from his wound.**

*Amar Rav Amram brei de-Rabbi Shimon bar Abba, amar Rabbi Shimon bar Abba, amar Rabbi Chanina*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ella bishvil she-lo hochichu ze et ze* — Jerusalem was destroyed because people did not give *tochacha* — remonstrance — to one another. By failing to rebuke one another's misconduct, people both lose their own sensitivity to what is wrong and implicitly endorse the behavior of others through silence.

The verse cited is: *hayu sareha ke-ayalim lo matze'u mir'eh* — its officers were like harts that found no pasture. The *Gemara* explains: *ma ayal ze rosho shel ze be-tzad zanavo shel ze* — just as harts walk in a line with each head at the tail of the one before it — *af Yisrael she-be-oto dor kavshu pneihem ba-karka, ve-lo hochichu ze et ze* — so too in that generation, everyone buried their faces in the ground and did not look one another in the eye, and thus did not rebuke one another. This failure of moral courage and mutual accountability then led to compounding sin.

### Rav Yehuda: Respect Torah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה אָמַר רַב: מַאי דִּכְתִיב ״אַל תִּגְּעוּ בִּמְשִׁיחָי וּבִנְבִיאַי אַל תָּרֵעוּ״, ״אַל תִּגְעוּ בִּמְשִׁיחָי״ — אֵלּוּ תִּינוֹקוֹת שֶׁל בֵּית רַבָּן, ״וּבִנְבִיאַי אַל תָּרֵעוּ״ — אֵלּוּ תַּלְמִידֵי חֲכָמִים. אָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יְהוּדָה נְשִׂיאָה: אֵין הָעוֹלָם מִתְקַיֵּים אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל הֶבֶל תִּינוֹקוֹת שֶׁל בֵּית רַבָּן. אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב פָּפָּא לְאַבָּיֵי: דִּידִי וְדִידָךְ מַאי? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אֵינוֹ דּוֹמֶה הֶבֶל שֶׁיֵּשׁ בּוֹ חֵטְא לְהֶבֶל שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ חֵטְא. וְאָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יְהוּדָה נְשִׂיאָה: אֵין מְבַטְּלִין תִּינוֹקוֹת שֶׁל בֵּית רַבָּן אֲפִילּוּ לְבִנְיַן בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ. וְאָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ לְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה נְשִׂיאָה: כָּךְ מְקּוּבְּלַנִי מֵאֲבוֹתַי, וְאָמְרִי לַהּ מֵאֲבוֹתֶיךָ: כׇּל עִיר שֶׁאֵין בָּהּ תִּינוֹקוֹת שֶׁל בֵּית רַבָּן מַחֲרִיבִין אוֹתָהּ, רָבִינָא אָמַר: מַחְרִימִין אוֹתָהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yehuda said** that **Rav said: What** is the meaning of that **which is written: “Do not touch My anointed ones and do My prophets no harm”** (I Chronicles 16:22)? **“Do not touch My anointed ones,” these are the schoolchildren,** who are as precious and important as kings and priests (Maharsha); **“and do not harm My prophets,” these are Torah scholars. Reish Lakish said in the name of Rabbi Yehuda Nesia: The world only exists because of the breath,** i.e., reciting Torah, **of schoolchildren. Rav Pappa said to Abaye: My** Torah study **and yours, what** is its status? Why is the Torah study of adults worth less? He **said to him: The breath** of adults, **which is** tainted by **sin, is not similar to the breath** of children, **which is not** tainted by **sin. And Reish Lakish said in the name of Rabbi Yehuda Nesia: One may not interrupt schoolchildren** from studying Torah, **even in order to build the Temple. And Reish Lakish said to Rabbi Yehuda Nesia: I have received from my ancestors, and some say** that he said to him: I have received **from your ancestors as follows: Any city in which there are no schoolchildren** studying Torah, they **destroy it. Ravina said:** They leave **it desolate.**

*Amar Rav Yehuda*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ella bishvil she-bizu bah talmidei chachamim* — Jerusalem was destroyed because they showed contempt for Torah scholars within it. This is closely related to the earlier point about equating the small and the great: when *talmidei chachamim* are not accorded appropriate respect, it reflects a denigration of Torah itself and of its authority over communal life. The verse is: *va-yihyu mal'ivim be-mal'achei ha-Elohim, u-vozim devaro, u-misa'ta'im bi-nvi'av, ad alot chamat Hashem be-amo, ad le-ein marpe* — they mocked God's emissaries, disdained His word, and scorned His prophets, until the anger of God rose against His people, until there was no remedy.

*Amar Rav Yehuda amar Rav*: *kol ha-mevazeh talmid chacham ein lo refu'ah le-makato* — whoever denigrates a *talmid chacham*, the wound he incurs will have no healing — a direct reading of the phrase *ad le-ein marpe*.

The *Gemara* then draws on an additional verse: *al tigu bi-meshichai u-vi-nvi'ai al tera'u* — do not touch My anointed ones, and do not harm My prophets. *Alu tinokot shel beit rabban* — "My anointed ones" refers to the schoolchildren, who were anointed with oil as part of their initiation into learning, as Rashi notes. *Ve-elu talmidei chachamim* — "My prophets" refers to the Torah sages. Both sets of statements converge: whether one speaks of the youngest children in *cheder* or the most accomplished scholars, the disregard for Torah and its bearers at every level is the thread connecting all of these explanations for Jerusalem's destruction.

## School Children & Torah



### Reish Lakish: Child

*Amar Reish Lakish mishum Rabbi Yehuda Nesi'ah* — Reish Lakish in the name of Rabbi Yehuda Nesi'ah, grandson of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi: *ein olam miskayem ella bishvil hevel tinokot shel beit rabban* — the world only continues to exist by virtue of the breath of schoolchildren, the pure, innocent words of Torah emerging from their mouths.

### Papa & Abaye Dispute

*Amar lei Rav Papa le-Abaye*: *didi ve-didach mai?* — what about our Torah learning, does it not similarly sustain the world? The response: *eino domeh hevel she-yesh bo cheit le-hevel she-ein bo cheit* — breath that is accompanied by sin is not comparable to breath that is without sin. There is a quality of innocence and purity in the learning of children who have not yet sinned that gives their Torah a distinctive power — not merely intellectual achievement, but something about moral innocence that sustains the world and gives it hope for the future.

### School Priority

*Ve-amar Reish Lakish mishum Rabbi Yehuda Nesi'ah*: *ein mevatlin tinokot shel beit rabban afilu le-vinyan Beit HaMikdash* — one does not dismiss schoolchildren from their learning even for the sake of building the *Beit HaMikdash*. While much of this discussion has the character of *derasha* and *aggadata*, the *Rambam* codifies this principle as practical *halakha* in *Hilkhot Talmud Torah* — that children remain in school even if the Temple is being built. So seriously does the *Gemara* take the continuity of Torah education.

### City Without School

*Ve-amar Reish Lakish mishum Rabbi Yehuda Nesi'ah*: *kach mekublani me-avotai* — here is the tradition I received from my forefathers (and some say he attributed it to *Rabbi Yehuda Nesi'ah*'s own forefathers, presumably *Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi*): *kol ir she-ein bah tinokot shel beit rabban, machrivin otah* — any city that does not have schoolchildren — that has not established a *cheder*, a Jewish school — will be destroyed. *Ravina* adds that the word is not merely *machrivin* — destruction — but *machrimin otah*, invoking *cherem*, a total and more severe form of destruction, conveying an even greater gravity.

## Trustworthiness & Ethics



### Rava: Trustworthy People

*Ve-amar Rava*: *lo charva Yerushalayim ella bishvil she-pasku mimena anshei emunah* — Jerusalem was destroyed because trustworthy people, people of integrity, ceased to dwell within it. This shifts the discussion from the realm of Torah and its study to a more general statement about basic moral character and faithfulness.

### Jeremiah: One Honest

The verse cited is from Yirmiyahu: *shotetu be-chutzot Yerushalayim u-re'u na, u-de'u u-vakshu bi-rchovoteha, im timtze'u ish oseh mishpat, mevakesh emunah, ve-eslach lah* — roam through the streets of Jerusalem and look, seek in its public squares — can you find even one person who acts with justice, who seeks faithfulness? If so, I will forgive it. The implication is that no such person could be found, and therefore it was destroyed. This connects back thematically to earlier material in the *sugya* about *shomer emunim* — keeping faith — and the importance of trustworthiness as a foundational civic and spiritual virtue.

### Ketina: Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רָבָא: לֹא חָרְבָה יְרוּשָׁלַיִם אֶלָּא בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁפָּסְקוּ מִמֶּנָּה אַנְשֵׁי אֲמָנָה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״שׁוֹטְטוּ בְּחוּצוֹת יְרוּשָׁלִַים וּרְאוּ נָא [וּדְעוּ וּבַקְשׁוּ בִרְחוֹבוֹתֶיהָ אִם תִּמְצְאוּ אִישׁ] (אִם יֵשׁ אִישׁ) עוֹשֶׂה מִשְׁפָּט מְבַקֵּשׁ אֱמוּנָה וְאֶסְלַח לָהּ״. אִינִי?! וְהָאָמַר רַב קַטִּינָא: אֲפִילּוּ בִּשְׁעַת כִּשְׁלוֹנָהּ שֶׁל יְרוּשָׁלָיִם לֹא פָּסְקוּ מִמֶּנָּה אַנְשֵׁי אֲמָנָה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״כִּי יִתְפֹּשׂ אִישׁ בְּאָחִיו בֵּית אָבִיו לֵאמֹר שִׂמְלָה לְכָה קָצִין תִּהְיֶה לָּנוּ״, דְּבָרִים שֶׁבְּנֵי אָדָם מִתְכַּסִּין בָּהֶן כְּשִׂמְלָה יֶשְׁנָן בְּיָדֶיךָ. ״וְהַמַּכְשֵׁלָה הַזֹּאת תַּחַת יָדֶךָ״,
> **Translation:** **And Rava said: Jerusalem was destroyed only because there were no more trustworthy people there, as it is stated: “Roam about the streets of Jerusalem and see, and search its plazas, if you can find a person, who acts justly, who seeks integrity, that I should forgive it”** (Jeremiah 5:1). The Gemara asks: **Is that so? Didn’t Rav Ketina say: Even at the time of Jerusalem’s failure, trustworthy people did not cease there, as it is stated: “For a man will grab his brother of his father’s house and say: You have a garment. Come be a chief over us** and let this ruin be under your care” (Isaiah 3:6)? **Things that people use to cover up like a garment,** secrets, **are in your hands** and you know about them. Therefore, you should be a leader of the community. And that which is stated: **“And let this ruin be under your care,”** meaning:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּבָרִים שֶׁאֵין בְּנֵי אָדָם עוֹמְדִין עֲלֵיהֶם אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן נִכְשָׁלִים בָּהֶן יֶשְׁנָן תַּחַת יָדֶיךָ, ״קָצִין תִּהְיֶה לָנוּ״. ״יִשָּׂא בַיּוֹם הַהוּא לֵאמֹר לֹא אֶהְיֶה חוֹבֵשׁ וְגוֹ׳״ — אֵין יִשָּׂא אֶלָּא לְשׁוֹן שְׁבוּעָה, וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר ״לֹא תִשָּׂא אֶת שֵׁם ה׳״. ״לֹא אֶהְיֶה חוֹבֵשׁ״ — לֹא אֶהְיֶה מֵחוֹבְשֵׁי עַצְמָן בְּבֵית הַמִּדְרָשׁ. ״וּבְבֵיתִי אֵין לֶחֶם וְאֵין שִׂמְלָה״ — שֶׁאֵין בְּיָדִי לֹא מִקְרָא וְלֹא מִשְׁנָה וְלֹא גְּמָרָא. וּמִמַּאי? דִּילְמָא שָׁאנֵי הָתָם, דְּאִי אֲמַר לְהוּ גְּמִירְנָא, אֲמַרוּ לֵיהּ: אֵימָא לַן. הֲוָה לֵיהּ לְמֵימַר גְּמַר וּשְׁכַח, מַאי ״לֹא אֶהְיֶה חוֹבֵשׁ״? — כְּלָל! לָא קַשְׁיָא: כָּאן בְּדִבְרֵי תוֹרָה, כָּאן בְּמַשָּׂא וּמַתָּן.
> **Translation:** **Matters** of Torah **that people do not ascertain unless they misunderstand them are in your hands;** therefore, **be a chief over us.** It is stated later on in that passage: **“He will rise [*yissa*] on that day, saying, I will not be a ruler** while in my house there is no bread and no garment, you shall not appoint me as chief of the people” (Isaiah 3:7). **The term rise [*yissa*] means nothing other than an oath, as it says: “Do not raise [*tissa*] the name of the Lord** your God in vain” (Exodus 20:17). **I will not be a ruler,** means: **I will not be one of those who close themselves up in the study hall,** as I do not regularly do so, **while in my house there is no bread and no garment, for I have neither Bible nor Mishna nor Gemara in my hands.** According to this explanation, there were people of faith who truthfully admitted that they did not study Torah, even during the time of the destruction of Jerusalem. The Gemara rejects this: **And from what** do you conclude that this admission was due to their faithfulness? **Perhaps it is different** in that case, **as had he told them: I studied** Torah, **they would have said to him: Tell us** what you studied. As he would not know to respond, he would have no choice other than to admit that he did not study. The Gemara rejects this: That is not difficult, as **he could have** at least **said he learned and forgot.** The Gemara asks: Therefore, **what** is: **I will not be a ruler?** It means that he is telling the truth and although he could have lied, he admits that he did **not** study Torah **at all.** The Gemara answers: This is **not difficult. Here,** where it was taught that they were faithful, that was **with regard to matters of Torah;** whereas **here,** where it was taught that there were no more people of faith in Jerusalem, that was **with regard to** matters of **business.**

The *Gemara* challenges: *eini?* — is that really true? *Ve-amar Rav Ketina*: *afilu be-sha'at kishlonah shel Yerushalayim, lo pasku mimena anshei emunah* — even at the time of Jerusalem's stumbling and decline, trustworthy people did not altogether cease. The verse cited is: *ki yitpos ish be-achiv beit aviv, simlah lecha katzin tihyeh lanu* — a man will grab his kinsman from his father's house and say: you have a cloak, be our ruler. The *Gemara* explains the verse's imagery: the "cloak" (*simlah*) refers to *devarim she-bnei adam mitkasim bahem ke-simlah* — matters that people conceal within themselves as a garment conceals the body — meaning, you possess Torah knowledge that you keep hidden. *Yeshnan be-yadecha* — you have this. You should therefore be our leader.

### Rejected Leader Parable

The verse continues: *ve-ha-machshelah hazot tachat yadecha* — and this stumbling is under your hand. The *Gemara* interprets: *devarim she-ein bnei adam omdim aleihem ela im ken nichshalim bahem* — matters so difficult that people cannot truly grasp them without first making mistakes and learning from those errors. The people are saying to this individual: you possess both the concealed knowledge — the fundamentals — and the hard-won understanding that comes only through struggle. Be our leader, our *talmid chacham*.

To this the man responds with an oath: *yisa ba-yom ha-hu le-emor lo eheyeh chovesh* — he will take an oath on that day, saying: I will not be bound. *Ein yisa ela leshon shevuah* — *yisa* here is oath language, as in *lo tisa et shem Hashem Elokecha*. And he explains: *lo eheyeh me-chovshei atzman be-veit ha-midrash* — I am not among those who bind themselves to their seat in the *beit midrash*. You are mistaken about me. *U-ve-veiti ein lechem ve-ein simlah* — in my house there is no bread and no cloak — *she-ein be-yadi lo mikra ve-lo mishnah ve-lo Gemara* — I have neither Scripture nor Mishnah nor Gemara. I am not who you think I am.

The point of the entire *derasha*: this man, when pressured to claim a position of leadership on the basis of Torah scholarship he does not possess, refuses to lie. He openly declares his ignorance. This demonstrates that even in the period of Jerusalem's decline, there existed people of basic honesty.

### Torah vs. Business

The *Gemara* probes: *u-mimai* — but how does this prove trustworthiness? *Dilma shani hasam di-amru lei gmirna, amru lei eima lan* — perhaps he is not refusing to lie out of genuine honesty, but rather out of self-interest: if he claims to be a *talmid chacham*, they will immediately demand that he deliver a *Gemara shiur*, and he cannot. So he is simply avoiding an untenable position.

The *Gemara* responds: *hava lei le-meimar gmar ve-shachach* — if his concern were self-image rather than truth, he could have simply said: "You're right, I was once learned, but I have forgotten." That would have gotten him out of the difficulty without full disclosure. The fact that he admits to never having possessed Torah knowledge at all demonstrates genuine honesty.

The *Gemara* then resolves the apparent contradiction between Rava's statement — that Jerusalem was destroyed because trustworthy people ceased — and the evidence that even in its decline, at least some honest people could be found: *lo kashya, kan be-divrei Torah, kan be-masa u-matan* — there is no difficulty. In matters of Torah, perhaps people retained enough residual respect for Torah that they would not lie about their learning. In business dealings, where the *yetzer ha-ra* is stronger and the temptations greater, they ceased to be trustworthy — and that is what ultimately brought about the destruction.

This is a striking resolution. The *sugya* begins with the centrality of Shabbat observance and the respect for Torah, and ends with the observation that even when other forms of honesty remained, the destruction came from a basic failure of commercial integrity — the loss of faithfulness in everyday economic life. While the earlier causes focused on observance and Torah, the final cause points to fundamental morality and *emunah* in human dealings as the cornerstone without which a community cannot endure.

## Saving Food Rules



### Basket of Bread

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ מַצִּילִין סַל מָלֵא כִּכָּרוֹת, אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁיֵּשׁ בּוֹ מֵאָה סְעוּדוֹת, וְעִיגּוּל שֶׁל דְּבֵילָה, וְחָבִית שֶׁל יַיִן. וְאוֹמֵר לַאֲחֵרִים: בּוֹאוּ וְהַצִּילוּ לָכֶם. וְאִם הָיוּ פִּיקְחִין, עוֹשִׂין עִמּוֹ חֶשְׁבּוֹן אַחַר הַשַּׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **One may rescue a basket full of loaves** and the like from a fire on Shabbat, **even if there is** food for **one hundred meals in it. And** one may rescue **a round** cake **of dried figs,** even though it is very large, **and** one may rescue **a barrel** full **of wine. And one may** even **say to others: Come and rescue for yourselves. And if** the people who rescue with him were **clever, they make a calculation with him after Shabbat** in order to receive payment for the items that they rescued.

The *Gemara* now returns to the practical *sugya* of saving food during a fire on Shabbat, moving from the quantity limitation of three meals to more specific rules. The *Mishnah* states: *matzilim sal malei kikarot af al pi she-yesh bah meah seudot* — one may save a basket full of loaves of bread even if it contains a hundred portions. The basket, as a single object, may be taken out in its entirety — one is not required to remove bread from it in order to limit the quantity. The key is that this is one act of saving a single vessel.

### Single Objects

Similarly: *ve-igul shel dveilah* — a round pressed mass of figs — and *ve-chavis shel yayin* — a barrel of wine — may each be taken out in their entirety as single objects, even if their contents exceed three meals' worth. The principle in each case is that it is a *kli echad*, a single object, which may be carried out as one act regardless of how much food it contains.

### Save for Yourselves

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְהֵיכָן מַצִּילִין אוֹתָן — לֶחָצֵר הַמְעוֹרֶבֶת. בֶּן בְּתִירָה אוֹמֵר: אַף לְשֶׁאֵינָהּ מְעוֹרֶבֶת. וּלְשָׁם מוֹצִיא כׇּל כְּלֵי תַּשְׁמִישׁוֹ, וְלוֹבֵשׁ כׇּל מַה שֶּׁיָּכוֹל לִלְבּוֹשׁ, וְעוֹטֵף כׇּל מַה שֶּׁיָּכוֹל לַעֲטוֹף. רַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹמֵר: שְׁמֹנָה עָשָׂר כֵּלִים. וְחוֹזֵר וְלוֹבֵשׁ וּמוֹצִיא, וְאוֹמֵר לַאֲחֵרִים: בּוֹאוּ וְהַצִּילוּ עִמִּי.
> **Translation:** **To where may one rescue** items moved from the fire? One may do so **to a courtyard** where an ***eiruv*** was established, and it is permitted to carry. **Ben Beteira says:** One may do so **even to** a courtyard **where an eiruv** was **not** established. **And one may carry there all the utensils, and put on all** the garments **that he can wear, and one may wrap all** the cloths **that he can wrap** around himself in order to rescue his property. **Rabbi Yosei says:** One may put on only **eighteen garments** at once, as people sometimes wear that number of garments, but not more. However, **one may again put on** that number of garments **and carry** it **out. And he may say to others: Come and rescue with me.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ וְהָא תְּנָא לֵיהּ רֵישָׁא שָׁלֹשׁ סְעוּדוֹת וְתוּ לָא! אָמַר רַב הוּנָא: לָא קַשְׁיָא, כָּאן — בְּבָא לְהַצִּיל, כָּאן — בְּבָא לְקַפֵּל. וּבָא לְהַצִּיל — מַצִּיל אֶת כּוּלָּן. בָּא לְקַפֵּל — אֵינוֹ מְקַפֵּל אֶלָּא מְזוֹן שָׁלֹשׁ סְעוּדוֹת. רַבִּי אַבָּא בַּר זַבְדָּא אָמַר רַב: אִידֵּי וְאִידֵּי בָּבָא לְקַפֵּל, וְלָא קַשְׁיָא: כָּאן — לְאוֹתָהּ חָצֵר, כָּאן — לְחָצֵר אַחֶרֶת. בָּעֵי רַב הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: פֵּירַשׂ טַלִּיתוֹ, וְקִיפֵּל וְהִנִּיחַ, וְקִיפֵּל וְהִנִּיחַ, מַאי: כְּבָא לְהַצִּיל דָּמֵי, אוֹ כְּבָא לְקַפֵּל דָּמֵי?
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara asks: **Wasn’t it taught in the first clause,** i.e., the preceding mishna, that one may rescue **three meals and no more? Rav Huna said:** This is **not difficult. This** current mishna permitted rescuing a greater amount **when one comes to rescue** a basket or round cake of figs; **that** previous mishna permitted rescuing food for only three meals in a case where **one comes to collect** the food. **When one comes to rescue, he may rescue everything; when one comes to collect, he may collect food for only three meals.** However, **Rabbi Abba bar Zavda said** that **Rav said: Both this and that** are referring to a case **where one comes to collect, and** this is **not difficult. This** current mishna permitted rescuing a greater amount when one moves the objects **to the same courtyard; that,** the previous mishna, permitted saving food for only three meals when one transfers it **to a different courtyard. Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua, raised a dilemma:** If **one spread his cloak** on the ground **and collected and placed** objects in the cloak, **and collected and placed** objects inside it, **what** is the ruling? Is he **considered as one who comes to rescue or** is he **like one who comes to collect?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִדְּאָמַר רָבָא: אַטְעֲיֵהּ רַב שֵׁיזְבִי לְרַב חִסְדָּא, וּדְרַשׁ: וּבִלְבַד שֶׁלֹּא יָבִיא כְּלִי שֶׁהוּא מַחֲזִיק יוֹתֵר מִשָּׁלֹשׁ סְעוּדוֹת, שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ כְּבָא לְהַצִּיל דָּמֵי, וְשַׁפִּיר דָּמֵי. אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק לְרָבָא: מַאי טָעוּתָא? אֲמַר לֵיהּ, דְּקָתָנֵי: וּבִלְבַד שֶׁלֹּא יָבִיא כְּלִי אַחֵר וְיִקְלוֹט, כְּלִי אַחֵר וִיצָרֵף. כְּלִי אַחֵר הוּא דְּלָא, אֲבָל בְּהָהוּא מָנָא — כַּמָּה דְּבָעֵי מַצִּיל.
> **Translation:** The Gemara attempts to resolve the dilemma **from that which Rava said: Rav Sheizvi misled Rav Ḥisda, and** Rav Ḥisda **taught** (Rabbeinu Ḥananel) a *halakha* with regard to a barrel that breaks on a roof, and added: And it is permitted **provided that one does not bring a vessel that holds more than three meals. Conclude from** the fact that Rava said that Rav Ḥisda’s restriction of the amount that can be rescued to food sufficient for three meals is mistaken, that one who takes a large amount all at once is **like one who comes to rescue, and** he may **well** do so. **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said to Rava: What is the error** in Rav Ḥisda’s statement? Perhaps it is actually prohibited. He **said to him, as it is taught: Provided that he does not bring another vessel** and place it on the ground to **catch** the liquid, **another vessel and attach** the vessel next to the roof. Apparently **it is another vessel that one may not** bring, **but in that** same **vessel, one may rescue as much as he wants,** and the Sages did not establish a maximum size for the vessel.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעִיגּוּל שֶׁל דְּבֵילָה כּוּ׳. חֶשְׁבּוֹן מַאי עֲבִידְתֵּיהּ? מֵהֶפְקֵירָא קָזָכוּ? — אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: מִדַּת חֲסִידוּת שָׁנוּ כָּאן. אָמַר רָבָא: חֲסִידֵי אַגְרָא דְשַׁבְּתָא שָׁקְלִי?! אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא: הָכָא בִּירֵא שָׁמַיִם עָסְקִינַן, וְלָא נִיחָא לֵיהּ דְּלִיתְהֲנֵי מֵאֲחֵרִים, וּבְחִנָּם נָמֵי לָא נִיחָא לֵיהּ דְּלִיטְרַח. וְהָכִי קָאָמַר: וְאִם הָיוּ פִּיקְחִין, דְּיָדְעִי דִּכְהַאי גַּוְונָא לָאו שְׂכַר שַׁבָּת הוּא, עוֹשִׂין עִמּוֹ חֶשְׁבּוֹן לְאַחַר הַשַּׁבָּת.
> **Translation:** **And** we learned in the mishna that one is permitted to rescue **a round** cake **of dried figs** from a fire, and one may even say to others: Come and rescue for yourselves. And if the people who rescue with him are clever, they make a calculation with him after Shabbat. The Gemara is surprised at this: **What is** the mention of **a calculation doing** here? **Aren’t they acquiring** food **from ownerless** property? What calculation is necessary here? Whatever they rescue is theirs, and they do not need to return it to the original owner. **Rav Ḥisda said: They taught an attribute of piety here.** These are pious people. They want to return the objects to their owner even though they are not legally obligated to do so, and they were permitted to receive payment for their efforts. **Rava said:** And do **pious** people **take payment** for work they do on **Shabbat? Rather, Rava said: Here, we are dealing with one who is Heaven-fearing** but not completely pious. **And** it is **uncomfortable for him to benefit from** the property of **others, and it is also uncomfortable for him to exert** himself **for free. And this is what** the mishna **is saying: And if they are clever and know that in a situation of this kind it is not** technically **payment for Shabbat** labor, and it is permitted because they are only receiving a small portion of the value of the objects that they rescued, **they** may **make a calculation with him after Shabbat.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְהֵיכָן מַצִּילִין כּוּ׳. מַאי שְׁנָא הָכָא דְּקָתָנֵי ״לָכֶם״, וּמַאי שְׁנָא הָכָא דְּקָתָנֵי ״עִמִּי״? אָמְרִי: גַּבֵּי מְזוֹנוֹת קָתָנֵי ״לָכֶם״ — מִשּׁוּם דְּלָא קָא חֲזֵי אֶלָּא מְזוֹן שָׁלֹשׁ סְעוּדוֹת. אֲבָל גַּבֵּי לְבוּשִׁים קָתָנֵי ״עִמִּי״ — מִשּׁוּם דְּקָחֲזֵי לֵיהּ לְכוּלֵּי יוֹמָא.
> **Translation:** We learned in the mishna that one may say to others: Come and rescue for yourselves. We also learned: **And to where may one rescue** the food? According to the first *tanna*, one may rescue to a courtyard in which an *eiruv* was established. According to ben Beteira, one may rescue even to a courtyard in which an *eiruv* was not established. The Gemara asks: **What is different here,** with regard to rescuing food, **that it is taught** that one may say to others: Come and rescue **for yourselves, and what is different here,** with regard to rescuing clothing, **that it is taught** that one may say to others: Come and rescue **with me?** The Gemara answers: **With regard to food, it taught: For yourselves, because only food for three meals is suited for him,** and only others can benefit from the rest. **However, with regard to** the **garments, it is taught: With me, because it is suited for him** to continue rescuing garments **all day,** since he is permitted to wear other clothes.

In addition to the single-object exception, the *Mishnah* offers another method for saving more than three meals' worth: *ve-omer la-acherim: bo'u ve-hatzilu lachem* — one may say to others: come and save for yourselves. By relinquishing his ownership and inviting others to take the food, each person who comes may save three meals for himself, and the total amount preserved may far exceed what the homeowner could save on his own.

The *Gemara* follows: *im hayu pikchin, osin imahem cheshbon achar Shabbat* — if they were clever, they would settle accounts with the homeowner after Shabbat. The *Gemara* asks: *cheshbon mai avidtei?* — why is a settlement necessary at all? Once the homeowner declared the food ownerless (*hefker*), ownership passed to those who took it. *Amar Rav Chisda*: this is a matter of *midat chasidut* — a measure of piety. Strictly speaking, those who took the food may keep it; but as an act of righteousness, they would return it.

*Amar Rava*: but how can someone be called a *chasid* here if they intend to be compensated? A true *chasid* would return the food without expectation of payment. The *Gemara* responds: *hacha bi-yerei shamayim askinan* — here we are dealing not with a *chasid* but with a *yerei shamayim*, a God-fearing person with a strict moral code. Such a person operates in a middle register: *ve-lo nicha lei de-lihani me-acherim* — he does not want to profit from another's misfortune, but *u-ve-chinam nami lo nicha lei de-litrach* — he also does not want to expend effort without recompense. He neither appropriates the food as a windfall nor donates his effort for nothing. Therefore, *ve-hachi ka-amar* — this is what the *Mishnah* means: if they were *pikchin*, meaning, if they understood the arrangement clearly enough to know that settling accounts afterward does not constitute *sechar Shabbat* — payment for labor on Shabbat — because technically they were taking for themselves and the food was truly theirs, then they could make such an arrangement after Shabbat without violating any prohibition.

### Where to Save

*Le-heichan matzilin?* — Where may one save food? The *Mishnah* is more stringent about food than it was about sacred books. For *sifrei kodesh*, the *Gemara* earlier allowed saving even to a courtyard with some architectural deficiency. For food, the rule is stricter: *le-chatzer me'urevet* — to a courtyard that is both architecturally complete and has an *eruv*. No allowances are made, because the concern is that permitting too much latitude will lead a person to extinguish the fire actively.

### Ben Beteira Lenient

*Ben Beteira omer*: *af le-she-eina me'urevet* — even to a courtyard without an *eruv*, as long as it is architecturally correct, saving food there is permitted. The tension here is instructive: for *sifrei kodesh*, leniency was extended on account of the sanctity of the texts; for food, the *Tanna Kamma* is stricter than even the standard rule of carrying would require, precisely because of the fear that one will come to extinguish the fire. Ben Beteira disagrees and applies a more lenient standard.

## Saving Clothes & Objects



### Vessels for Shabbat

The *Mishnah* continues: *u-le-sham motzi klei tashmisho* — there one may also bring out vessels and utensils needed for Shabbat use. Rashi understands this to mean objects one will actually use on Shabbat — furniture, household items, and the like — though the precise limits of what qualifies as needed for Shabbat remain subject to discussion.

### Wearing Clothes

By clothing, the *Mishnah* takes a different approach than it does with food. Rather than specifying a fixed quantity, it states: *u-lovesh kol mah she-yuchal lilbosh* — one may wear and take out whatever one is able to wear. *Ve-otef kol mah she-yuchal la-atof* — and one may wrap whatever one is able to wrap. Anything that can be carried out on one's body — piled on, layered, draped — may be taken out, even if far more than one would normally wear.

### Back & Forth

The *Tanna Kamma* permits going back and forth repeatedly: *ve-chozer ve-lovesh u-motzi* — returning, dressing again in additional garments, and going out again. This parallels the loophole by food — where others could be invited to take for themselves — except that here the food equivalent permitted others to act, while for clothing the homeowner himself may make multiple trips, each time loading on as many garments as he can wear.

### Rabbi Yose: 18 Garments

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: לוֹבֵשׁ מוֹצִיא וּפוֹשֵׁט, וְחוֹזֵר וְלוֹבֵשׁ וּמוֹצִיא וּפוֹשֵׁט, וַאֲפִילּוּ כׇּל הַיּוֹם כּוּלּוֹ — דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי מֵאִיר. רַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹמֵר: שְׁמֹנָה עָשָׂר כֵּלִים, וְאֵלּוּ הֵם שְׁמֹנָה עָשָׂר כֵּלִים: מִקְטוֹרֶן, אוּנְקְלֵי, וּפוּנְדָּא, קַלְבּוֹס שֶׁל פִּשְׁתָּן, וְחָלוּק, וְאַפִּילְיוֹן, וּמַעְפּוֹרֶת, וּשְׁנֵי סַפְרֵקִין, וּשְׁנֵי מִנְעָלִים, וּשְׁנֵי אַנְפִּילָאוֹת, וּשְׁנֵי פַּרְגּוֹד, וַחֲגוֹר שֶׁבְּמׇתְנָיו, וְכוֹבַע שֶׁבְּרֹאשׁוֹ, וְסוּדָר שֶׁבְּצַוָּארוֹ.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: If one wants to rescue objects from a fire and there are many garments there, he **may wear** them, and **take** them **out** to a safe place, **and remove** them there, **and return** to the fire, **and wear** other clothes, **and take** them **out and remove** them. **And** he may **even** do so **all day long;** this is **the statement of Rabbi Meir. Rabbi Yosei says:** One may wear only **eighteen garments, and these are** the **eighteen garments: A cloak [*miktoren*], a cape [*unkali*],** a broad garment worn on one’s shoulders, **and a** large hollow **belt** worn over the clothes, **a wide linen garment [*kalbus*], and a robe** worn against the skin, **a robe** wrapped above, **and a kerchief** on one’s head, **and two straps,** i.e., belts, **and two shoes, and two socks [*anpilaot*], and two** tall **boots [*pargod*], and a belt around one’s loins** over the robe, **and a hat on one’s head, and a scarf around one’s neck.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן נַנָּס אוֹמֵר: פּוֹרְסִין עוֹר שֶׁל גְּדִי עַל גַּבֵּי שִׁידָּה תֵּיבָה וּמִגְדָּל שֶׁאָחַז בָּהֶן אֶת הָאוּר, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא מְחָרֵךְ. וְעוֹשִׂין מְחִיצָה בְּכׇל הַכֵּלִים, בֵּין מְלֵאִין בֵּין רֵיקָנִים, בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁלֹּא תַּעֲבוֹר הַדְּלֵיקָה. רַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹסֵר בִּכְלֵי חֶרֶס חֲדָשִׁים מְלֵאִין מַיִם, לְפִי שֶׁאֵין יְכוֹלִין לְקַבֵּל אֶת הָאוּר וְהֵן מִתְבַּקְּעִין וּמְכַבִּין אֶת הַדְּלֵיקָה.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas says: One may spread out a** moist **goat’s hide over a box, a chest, or a closet that caught fire, because** the fire **singes** and does not burn it. The fire does not burn the wet goat’s hide but merely singes it, and by doing so the wooden vessels are preserved. **And one may establish a barrier** against the fire **with all vessels, both full and empty, so that the fire will not pass. Rabbi Yosei prohibits** using **new earthenware vessels full of water, because they cannot withstand** the heat of **the fire and they** will **burst and extinguish the fire,** and it is prohibited to cause the fire to be extinguished on Shabbat even indirectly.

*Rabbi Yose omer shmonah asar keilim* — Rabbi Yose limits this to eighteen standard garments. The *Gemara* enumerates them: a *miktron* (an outer cloak), an *onkli* (a cape), a *punda* (a type of belt), a *kalvuvus shel pishtan* (a linen outer garment), a *chaluk* (a shirt), an *apilyon* (a cape or covering), a *ma'aforet* (a head scarf), two *safarkim* (straps, perhaps for holding up clothing), two *minalim* (shoes), two *anpalyot* (socks), *veis pargad* (boots), a *chagor she-bemotnav* (a belt at the waist), a *kova she-berosho* (a hat), and a *sudar she-be-tzavaro* (a kerchief around the neck) — in sum, eighteen items corresponding to the standard garments typically worn in that era.

### Save with Me vs. For You

The *Gemara* notes an apparent asymmetry between the two loophole formulations in the *Mishnah*: by food, it says *bo'u ve-hatzilu lachem* — come and save for yourselves — while by clothing it says *bo'u ve-hatzilu imi* — come and save with me. The explanation is that by food, the homeowner is limited to three meals for himself, so in order for more to be saved, the food must actually become the property of others — hence *lachem*, for yourselves. By clothing, however, no such per-person limit applies to the homeowner: he may take out as many garments as he can carry on his body. Therefore, the clothing remains his, and he simply recruits others to assist him — *imi*, together with me.

## Fire Protection Methods



### Fireproof Leather: Shimon

The *Mishnah* now shifts from what may be removed to what may be done in place to prevent further burning. *Rabbi Shimon ben Nanas omer*: *porsin or shel gedi al gabei shidah, teivah u-migdal* — one may spread a goat-leather hide over furniture — a chest, a box, a cabinet — *she-achaz bah ha-or* — when fire has already caught one part of it — *mipnei she-hu mechareich* — because the leather will singe but not burn through. The leather functions as a fireproof cover, protecting the remaining portion of the furniture from the spreading flame. This is an active protective measure, and Rabbi Shimon ben Nanas permits it.

### Vessel Barriers

*Ve-osin mechitza be-chol ha-keilim, bein melai'in bein reikanin, bishvil she-lo ta'avor ha-dleikah* — one may also create a barrier using any kind of vessels, whether filled or empty, to prevent the fire from spreading. The idea is that surrounding the burning item with vessels — even empty ones — can contain or redirect the fire.

### Rabbi Yosi: Water Vessels

*Rabbi Yosi oser bi-khlei cheres chadashim melei'im mayim* — Rabbi Yosi prohibits using new pottery vessels filled with water. His reasoning: *lefi she-einan yekholim lekabel et ha-or ve-hem mipakin u-makhabin et ha-dleikah* — new pottery vessels cannot withstand the heat of the fire; they will crack open, and the water inside will spill out and extinguish the fire. From the *Tanna Kamma*'s silence on this point, the *Gemara* infers that the first opinion permits even this — the indirect extinguishment that results when the vessel bursts and the water douses the flames.

### Water Saturation Method

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה אָמַר רַב: טַלִּית שֶׁאָחַז בָּהּ הָאוּר מִצַּד אֶחָד — נוֹתְנִין עָלֶיהָ מַיִם מִצַּד אַחֵר, וְאִם כָּבְתָה — כָּבְתָה. מֵיתִיבִי: טַלִּית שֶׁאָחַז בָּהּ הָאוּר מִצַּד אֶחָד — פּוֹשְׁטָהּ וּמִתְכַּסֶּה בָּהּ, וְאִם כָּבְתָה — כָּבְתָה. וְכֵן סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה שֶׁאָחַז בּוֹ הָאוּר — פּוֹשְׁטוֹ וְקוֹרֵא בּוֹ, וְאִם כָּבָה — כָּבָה.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: **Rav Yehuda said** that **Rav said:** If **a garment caught fire on one side, one may place water on its other side, and if** as a result the fire **is extinguished, it is extinguished.** The Gemara **raises an objection** based on the following *Tosefta*: If **a garment caught fire on one side, one may stretch it** out **and cover himself with it, and if** the fire **is extinguished, it is extinguished. And so too,** if **a Torah scroll caught fire, one may open it and read it, and if** the fire **is extinguished, it is extinguished.** Apparently, it is prohibited to actually pour water, but one may perform a permitted act that will incidentally extinguish.

*Amar Rav Yehuda amar Rav*: *tallit she-achazah bah ha-or mitzad echad, notnin aleha mayim mitzad acher* — if a garment has caught fire on one side, one may pour water on the other side of the garment. *Im kavta kavta* — if it goes out, it goes out. The mechanism is that the water saturates the unburned portion, and the fire extinguishes itself before reaching the saturated area. This is *geram kibbuy* — indirect extinguishment — rather than active dousing of the fire, and the *Tanna Kamma* of the *Mishnah* permits it.

*Tosafot* raises the question of whether saturating the garment with water involves a concern about *libun* — the prohibited melakhah of bleaching — but that analysis belongs elsewhere.

A *baraita* presents a parallel ruling: *tallit she-achazah bah ha-or mitzad echad — poshtah u-mitkasseh bah, ve-im kavtah kavtah* — if a garment has caught fire on one side, one may remove it, put it on, and if the act of wearing it causes the fire to go out, that is permissible. Similarly: *vekhein Sefer Torah she-achazah bo ha-or — poshto ve-korei bo, ve-im kavtah kavtah* — if a *Sefer Torah* has caught fire, one may open it and read from it, and if the fire goes out as a result, that is permissible.

Both of these cases raise a genuine conceptual difficulty. In what sense are these acts truly *eino mitkaven* — without intention? If one spreads the garment or opens the *Sefer Torah* specifically hoping the fire will be extinguished, this looks less like an unintended consequence and more like a desired outcome achieved through an ostensibly different act. Perhaps the best reading of these cases is that the person genuinely is not intending the extinguishment — he is simply wearing his garment or reading the *Sefer Torah* — and if the fire goes out, it goes out. That reading at least preserves the formal structure of *eino mitkaven*, and the later *Gemara* analysis of the door-and-lamp case suggests that this is indeed how the *Gemara* reads these scenarios.

### Natural Extinguish

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הוּא דְּאָמַר כְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן נַנָּס.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **It** was Rav **who said** his statement **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas** in the mishna.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵימַר דְּאָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן נַנָּס ״מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא מְחָרֵךְ״, גְּרַם כִּיבּוּי מִי אָמַר? אִין, מִדְּקָתָנֵי סֵיפָא רַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹסֵר בִּכְלֵי חֶרֶס חֲדָשִׁים מְלֵאִים מַיִם, שֶׁאֵינָן יְכוֹלִים לְקַבֵּל אֶת הָאוּר וְהֵן מִתְבַּקְּעִין וּמְכַבִּין אֶת הַדְּלֵיקָה — מִכְּלָל דְּתַנָּא קַמָּא שָׁרֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Say** that **Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas said** it is permitted to place the goat’s hide on the burning item **because it singes** and does not burn; **did he say** it is permitted to **indirectly cause** the fire to **extinguish?** The Gemara answers: **Yes,** Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas permitted that as well, and we learn this **from that which is taught** in **the latter clause** in the mishna: **Rabbi Yosei prohibits** using **new earthenware vessels** that are **full of water,** because **they cannot withstand the fire and** will **burst and extinguish the fire.** This proves **by inference that the first *tanna*,** Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas, **permits** it.

The *Gemara* asks why the *baraita* does not simply permit the active use of water, as Rabbi Shimon ben Nanos does. The answer: *keman de-amar ke-Rabbi Shimon ben Nanos* — the *baraita* presumably follows Rabbi Shimon ben Nanos's position. But then the *Gemara* objects: *aima de-amar Rabbi Shimon ben Nanos mishum de-mechareich* — Rabbi Shimon ben Nanos permits only the leather cover because it singes rather than extinguishes. *Geram kibbuy mi amar?* — who ever said Rabbi Shimon ben Nanos permits indirect extinguishment? The *Gemara* answers: yes, he does permit it — as is proven from the fact that the *Tanna Kamma* of the *Mishnah*, by contrast with Rabbi Yosi, permits placing vessels of water that will burst and douse the fire, which is itself a form of *geram kibbuy*. This demonstrates that the first opinion endorses *geram kibbuy* in principle.

## Grama & Fire Out



### Grama Permitted?

*Tanu Rabbanan*: the *Gemara* now addresses the question of *grama* — indirect causation — more broadly, and whether the *Tanna Kamma* truly permits indirect extinguishment through water. The inference from Rabbi Yosi's prohibition: *mikhlal de-Tanna Kamma sharei* — since Rabbi Yosi explicitly forbids the water-filled pottery vessels that will burst and put out the fire, the implication is that the *Tanna Kamma* permits this form of indirect extinguishment. This is a significant ruling, and the *Gemara* proceeds to test its limits through a series of related cases.

It must be emphasized that this is a very important *Gemara* for practical *halakha*. Much of the modern discussion around Shabbat-mode appliances, the devices developed by *Tzomet*, and other indirect methods of activating or deactivating electrical systems draws on the principles being developed here around *grama* and its permissibility.

### Shaking Off Lamp

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: נֵר שֶׁעַל גַּבֵּי טַבְלָא — מְנַעֵר אֶת הַטַּבְלָא וְהִיא נוֹפֶלֶת, וְאִם כָּבְתָה — כָּבְתָה. אָמְרִי דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי: לֹא שָׁנוּ אֶלָּא בְּשׁוֹכֵחַ, אֲבָל בְּמַנִּיחַ — נַעֲשָׂה בָּסִיס לְדָבָר הָאָסוּר.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught:** With regard to **a candle that is atop a board, one shakes the board and** the candle **falls, and if it is extinguished, it is extinguished. The** Sages of the **school of Rabbi Yannai said: They only taught** that this is permitted in a case **where one forgets** the candle atop the board when Shabbat began. **However,** in a case **where one places** it there, the board **becomes a base for a prohibited object** and may not be moved.

*Ner she-al gabei tavla* — if there is a lamp on top of a table, one may shake the table and allow the lamp to fall off, *ve-im kavtah kavtah* — and if it goes out, it goes out. This is permitted because the primary act is shaking the table — perhaps for some other purpose, such as clearing or cleaning it — and the extinguishment, if it occurs, is a secondary consequence.

The school of Rabbi Yannai qualifies this: *lo shanu ela be-sho'eiach* — this ruling applies only if the lamp was left on the table by accident, i.e., forgotten there. *Aval be-mani'ach* — but if the lamp was deliberately placed on the table, *na'aseh basis le-davar asur* — the table becomes a *basis* — a supporting base — for a prohibited object, and as such it takes on the status of *muktzeh* itself, making it impermissible to move.

### Door & Lamp

*Ner she-achorei ha-delet* — if there is a lamp burning behind a door, *pote'ach ve-no'el ke-darko, ve-im kavtah kavtah* — one may open and close the door in the normal way, and if the lamp goes out as a result, that is permitted. This case is more straightforwardly *eino mitkaven*: the person genuinely wants to open his door, not to extinguish the lamp. The question is only whether the opening of the door will foreseeably extinguish the flame.

### Rav's Curse

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנָא: נֵר שֶׁאֲחוֹרֵי הַדֶּלֶת — פּוֹתֵחַ וְנוֹעֵל כְּדַרְכּוֹ, וְאִם כָּבְתָה — כָּבְתָה. לָיֵיט עֲלַהּ רַב. אֲמַר לֵיהּ רָבִינָא לְרַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרָבָא, וְאָמְרִי לַהּ רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרָבָא לְרַב אָשֵׁי: מַאי טַעְמָא לָיֵיט עֲלַהּ רַב? אִילֵּימָא מִשּׁוּם דְּרַב סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, וְתָנָא קָתָנֵי לַהּ כְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן. מִשּׁוּם דְּרַב סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, כׇּל דְּתָנֵי כְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן מֵילָט לָיֵיט לֵיהּ?!
> **Translation:** **It was taught:** With regard to **a candle behind a door, one may open and shut** the door **in his** usual **manner, and if it is extinguished, it is extinguished. Rav would curse one** who did so. **Ravina said to Rav Aḥa, son of Rava, and some say** that **Rav Aḥa, son of Rava,** said **to Rav Ashi: What is the reason** that **Rav cursed** and reprimanded **one** who did so? **If you say** it is **because Rav holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda** that it is prohibited to perform an unintentional act from which a prohibited labor could ensue, **and** the ***tanna* taught in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon** that it is permitted to perform an unintentional act in those circumstances; **because Rav holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda, he curses anyone who teaches** the *halakha* **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon?** They rely on the ruling of a *tanna*, whose opinion is legitimate.

*Lait alei Rav* — Rav cursed at this ruling or at one who would act upon it. The *Gemara* asks: *mai ta'ama lait lei Rav?* — why did Rav curse? One might think it is because Rav holds like Rabbi Yehuda that *eino mitkaven* is prohibited, and the *Tanna* who taught the leniency holds like Rabbi Shimon who permits *eino mitkaven*. But surely Rav would not curse everyone who rules like Rabbi Shimon in this domain.

The *Gemara* rejects this explanation: *be-ha afilu Rabbi Shimon modeh* — in this case, even Rabbi Shimon would agree that there is a problem. The reason is the principle of *psik reisha*: *modeh Rabbi Shimon bi-psik reisha ve-lo yamut* — Rabbi Shimon agrees that if an act inevitably produces a prohibited consequence, it is forbidden even if unintended. If the lamp is situated directly behind the door such that opening the door will certainly extinguish it, that is *psik reisha*, and Rabbi Shimon would prohibit it.

### Psik Reisha

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: בְּהָא אֲפִילּוּ רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן מוֹדֶה. דְּהָא אַבָּיֵי וְרָבָא דְּאָמְרִי תַּרְוַיְיהוּ: מוֹדֶה רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בִּ״פְסִיק רֵישֵׁיהּ וְלָא יְמוּת״.
> **Translation:** He **said to him: In this** case, **even Rabbi Shimon concedes** that it is prohibited, **as it was Abaye and Rava who both said** that **Rabbi Shimon concedes in a case of: Cut off its head, will it not die?** In an instance where the unintentional act leads to an inevitable prohibited consequence, even Rabbi Shimon agrees that the person who performs the unintentional act is liable.

The question then becomes one of certainty versus probability. *Psik reisha* requires that the prohibited consequence be definite — if one cuts off a head, it will certainly die. But here, it may not be certain that opening the door will extinguish the lamp; it depends on conditions such as the proximity of the lamp to the door and the force of any draft. This leads some *Acharonim* to develop the category of *karov le-psik reisha* — something approaching but not quite reaching the certainty of *psik reisha*, yet still highly probable. Whether such a near-certainty is treated as *psik reisha* or retains the more lenient status of *eino mitkaven* is itself a significant debate among later authorities.

At minimum, the *Gemara*'s framing here clarifies what was difficult about the earlier cases: the permissibility of wearing the burning garment or opening the *Sefer Torah* rests on the assumption that the extinguishment is genuinely not the person's aim and is not an inevitable consequence of the act. If extinguishment were certain, it would be *psik reisha* and prohibited even for Rabbi Shimon.

### Wind Principle

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: פּוֹתֵחַ אָדָם דֶּלֶת כְּנֶגֶד מְדוּרָה בְּשַׁבָּת. לָיֵיט עֲלַהּ אַבָּיֵי. בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן? אִילֵימָא בְּרוּחַ מְצוּיָה — מַאי טַעְמֵיהּ דְמַאן דְּאָסַר?! אִי בְּרוּחַ שֶׁאֵינָהּ מְצוּיָה — מַאי טַעְמָא דְמַאן דְּשָׁרֵי?! לְעוֹלָם בְּרוּחַ מְצוּיָה: מָר סָבַר גָּזְרִינַן, וּמָר סָבַר לָא גָּזְרִינַן.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yehuda said: A person opens a door opposite a fire on Shabbat.** The Gemara relates that **Abaye would curse** anyone who did **so.** The Gemara clarifies: **With what are we dealing? If you say** this is referring to a case where **a typical wind** is blowing outside, **what is the reason of the one** who **prohibits** opening the door? A typical wind will neither fan nor extinguish the fire. And **if** it is referring to a case where **an atypical wind** is blowing outside, **what is the reason of the one who permits** opening the door? The Gemara answers: **Actually,** it is referring to the case of **a typical wind.** However, one **Sage,** Abaye, **holds** that **we issue a decree** prohibiting to open the door in the case of a typical wind due to a case of an atypical wind, **and** the other **Sage,** Rav Yehuda, **holds** that **we do not issue** that **decree.**

*Amar Rav Yehuda*: *pote'ach adam delet keneged medura be-Shabbat* — one may open a door opposite a bonfire on Shabbat. The assumption is that a full bonfire will not be extinguished by the draft from an opening door.

*Abaye* questions this: *be-mai askinan?* — what conditions are we discussing? *Eileima be-ruach metzuya* — if we are speaking of ordinary, calm wind, *mai ta'ama de-man de-assar?* — who would ever prohibit opening the door? A normal draft will not affect a bonfire. *Ei be-ruach she-eina metzuya* — if we are speaking of a strong, unusual wind, *mai ta'ama de-man de-sharei?* — why should anyone permit it, if the wind is strong enough that extinguishment becomes likely?

The *Gemara* resolves: *le-olam be-ruach metzuya* — the case is indeed ordinary wind. *Mar savar gazrinan* — one authority holds that we make a *gezeirah*: we prohibit opening the door even in ordinary wind, lest a person grow accustomed to doing so and then fail to be careful when the wind is strong. *U-mar savar lo gazrinan* — and the other authority holds that no such *gezeirah* is necessary; when the wind is strong, a person will naturally be alert to the danger and will refrain.

The broader principle emerging from this discussion is significant: it raises the question of whether *psik reisha* reasoning applies only to cases of absolute certainty, or whether cases of high probability — *karov le-psik reisha* — similarly trigger a prohibition, whether as a matter of substantive law or as a *gezeirah*. The *Acharonim* develop this point extensively from the present *Gemara*. The discussion of *geram kibbuy* — indirect extinguishment — will continue in the following daf.
