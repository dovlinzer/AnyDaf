[DAF:45b]
## Shtei Halechem Debate



### Kivsei Atzeret Intro

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַלֶּחֶם מְעַכֵּב אֶת הַכְּבָשִׂים, וְאֵין הַכְּבָשִׂים מְעַכְּבִין אֶת הַלֶּחֶם, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא.
> **Translation:** Failure to bring **the** two **loaves prevents** sacrifice of **the** accompanying peace offering of two **sheep, but** failure to sacrifice **the** two **sheep does not prevent** the bringing of **the** two **loaves;** this is **the statement of Rabbi Akiva.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן נַנָּס: לֹא כִּי, אֶלָּא הַכְּבָשִׂים מְעַכְּבִין אֶת הַלֶּחֶם, וְהַלֶּחֶם אֵינוֹ מְעַכֵּב הַכְּבָשִׂים, שֶׁכֵּן מָצִינוּ כְּשֶׁהָיוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּמִדְבָּר אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה קָרְבוּ כְּבָשִׂים בְּלֹא לֶחֶם, אַף כָּאן יִקְרְבוּ כְּבָשִׂים בְּלֹא לֶחֶם.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas says: No, rather** the opposite is true. Failure to sacrifice **the** peace offering of two **sheep prevents** the bringing of **the** two **loaves, but** failure to bring **the** two **loaves does not prevent** sacrifice of **the** accompanying peace offering of two **sheep. As we found that when the Jewish people were in the wilderness** for **forty years** after the exodus from Egypt, **they sacrificed** the two **sheep** as a peace offering on *Shavuot* **without** the two **loaves,** as the two loaves may be brought only from wheat grown in Eretz Yisrael after the Jewish people entered the land. **Here too,** whenever wheat is unavailable, **they** should **sacrifice** the two **sheep** without the two **loaves.** However, the two loaves are not sacrificed without the peace offering of two sheep.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן: הֲלָכָה כְּדִבְרֵי בֶּן נַנָּס, אֲבָל אֵין הַטַּעַם כִּדְבָרָיו.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Shimon says:** The ***halakha*** is **in accordance with the statement of** Rabbi Shimon **ben Nannas** that failure to sacrifice the two sheep prevents the bringing of the two loaves but failure to bring the two loaves does not prevent sacrifice of the accompanying peace offering of two sheep, **but the reason** for that ruling **is not in accordance with his statement.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁכׇּל הָאָמוּר בְּחוֹמֶשׁ הַפְּקוּדִים קָרֵב בַּמִּדְבָּר, וְכׇל הָאָמוּר בְּתוֹרַת כֹּהֲנִים אֵין קָרֵב בַּמִּדְבָּר, מִשֶּׁבָּאוּ לָאָרֶץ קָרְבוּ אֵלּוּ וָאֵלּוּ.
> **Translation:** **As all** the offerings that must be sacrificed on *Shavuot* **that** are **stated in the book of Numbers** (see 28:27), i.e., two bulls, one ram, and seven sheep as additional offerings and a goat as a sin offering, were **sacrificed** when the Jewish people were **in the wilderness. But all** the offerings **stated in Leviticus** (see 23:18–20), i.e., the offerings accompanying the two loaves, were **not sacrificed** when the Jewish people were **in the wilderness.** Not only were the two loaves not sacrificed, but the accompanying offerings, including the peace offering of the two sheep, were also not sacrificed, because it was only **when they arrived in Eretz** Yisrael that **these** additional offerings **and those** offerings accompanying the two loaves **were sacrificed.** Neither the additional offerings of *Shavuot* nor the two loaves, and the offerings that accompany them, were sacrificed in the wilderness, contrary to the opinion of Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas.

The daf begins at the bottom of 45b, with the statement *halechem ma'akevet hakevasim* — the bread prevents the lambs. This brings us into the *shtei halechem* discussion that closes the perek. While the broader theme of this section concerns which components of a *korban* are *not* mutually *me'akev*, the Gemara pauses on a notable exception: the relationship between the *shtei halechem* and the two lambs that accompany them on Shavuot. These lambs — the *kivsei atzeret* — are *shalmei tzibur*, communal peace offerings brought together with the bread. The Torah states that they are waved together with the bread and that they stand in a relationship of mutual dependency, but the precise direction of that dependency is the subject of a famous debate.

### Rabbi Akiva Position

Rabbi Akiva holds that the bread is *me'akev* the lambs: without the bread, you do not bring the lambs. The lambs exist only for the sake of the bread. The Gemara asks: *mai ta'ama de-Rabbi Akiva* — what is the reasoning behind this position?

The answer is that Rabbi Akiva derives it through a *gezeirah shavah* — *gamar "yihyu" mi-"tihiyena"*. In the passage in Emor, the Torah uses the word *yihyu* when speaking of both the bread and the two lambs together: *kodesh yihyu l'Hashem lakohen*. The underlying assumption is that this *yihyu* refers to only one of the two components, singling out whichever element is truly *me'akev*. This assumption is itself worth pausing on: the verse associates the bread and the two lambs together — *vehinnif hakohen otam al lechem habikkurim... al shnei hakevasim* — and then says *kodesh yihyu*, which would most naturally seem to apply to both components equally. Yet the Gemara proceeds on the premise that the *yihyu* governs only one of them, and the question becomes which one. Why we take that as a given is not made explicit, and it is a somewhat surprising move. Earlier in the same passage, when speaking of the bread alone, the Torah uses the word *tihiyena*: *solet tihiyena, chametz te'afena*. Rabbi Akiva therefore draws the connection: just as *tihiyena* there referred to bread, *yihyu* here refers to bread — *mah lahalan lechem, af kan lechem*. It is the bread that is the essential element.

### Ben Nannas Position

Ben Nannas reaches the opposite conclusion: the lambs are *me'akev*, not the bread. If you have the lambs alone, bring them; if you have the bread alone, do not bring it. His reasoning is also a *gezeirah shavah* — but he learns *yihyu mi-yihyu*. Earlier in the same *parsha*, the Torah uses *yihyu* in connection with the large group of animals that accompany the *shtei halechem*: *shivat kevasim temimim... par ben bakar... ve-eylim shnayim yihyu ola l'Hashem*. This *yihyu* refers to the animal component. Ben Nannas therefore reads the *yihyu* of *kodesh yihyu* as pointing back to the animal — specifically to the lambs — *mah lahalan kevasim, af kan kevasim*.

### Gezeirah Shavah Analysis

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִפְּנֵי מָה אֲנִי אוֹמֵר יִקְרְבוּ כְּבָשִׂים בְּלֹא לֶחֶם? מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהַכְּבָשִׁים מַתִּירִין אֶת עַצְמָן, וְלֹא הַלֶּחֶם בְּלֹא כְּבָשִׂים – שֶׁאֵין לוֹ מִי יַתִּירֶנּוּ.
> **Translation:** Rather, **for what** reason **do I** nevertheless **say that the sheep should be sacrificed without** the **loaves,** in accordance with the opinion of Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas? It is **due to** the fact **that the sheep permit themselves,** as the sprinkling of their blood and the burning of the portions consumed on the altar renders it permitted to partake of their meat. **And** why are **the loaves not** sacrificed **without the sheep?** It is **because there is no** item **to permit** the loaves, as the loaves are permitted only after the sheep are sacrificed.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״וְהִקְרַבְתֶּם עַל הַלֶּחֶם״ – חוֹבָה עַל הַלֶּחֶם, ״שִׁבְעַת כְּבָשִׂים תְּמִימִים״ – אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁאֵין לֶחֶם.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The mishna teaches the *halakhot* of the sacrifices that generally accompany the two loaves on *Shavuot* in a case when the two loaves are not available. The Gemara cites a relevant *baraita*. **The Sages taught:** The verse that mandates these offerings states: “And you shall sacrifice with the bread seven lambs without blemish of the first year, and one young bull, and two rams” (Leviticus 23:18). The phrase **“and you shall sacrifice with the bread”** indicates that it is **obligatory** to sacrifice these burnt offerings **with the loaves** of bread, and if the loaves are not available, then these offerings are not sacrificed. The continuation of the verse: **“Seven lambs without blemish,”** teaches that the lambs are sacrificed **even if there are no loaves** available.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם כֵּן, מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״עַל הַלֶּחֶם״? מְלַמֵּד שֶׁלֹּא נִתְחַיְּיבוּ בַּכְּבָשִׂים קוֹדֶם שֶׁנִּתְחַיְּיבוּ בַּלֶּחֶם, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי טַרְפוֹן.
> **Translation:** **If so,** that the animal offerings may be sacrificed even without loaves, **what** is the meaning when **the verse states “with the bread”?** It **teaches that they were not obligated to** sacrifice **the sheep before they were obligated to** sacrifice **loaves,** i.e., they became obligated to sacrifice all of these offerings only when they entered Eretz Yisrael. This is **the statement of Rabbi Tarfon.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא אוֹמֵר: יָכוֹל הֵן הֵן כְּבָשִׂים הָאֲמוּרִים כָּאן הֵן הֵן כְּבָשִׂים הָאֲמוּרִים בְּחוֹמֶשׁ הַפְּקוּדִים? אָמַרְתָּ, כְּשֶׁאַתָּה מַגִּיעַ אֵצֶל פָּרִים וְאֵילִים אֵינָן הֵן, אֶלָּא הַלָּלוּ בָּאִין בִּגְלַל עַצְמָן, וְהַלָּלוּ בָּאִין בִּגְלַל לֶחֶם.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Akiva says:** One **might** have thought that the **sheep mentioned here** in Leviticus, which accompany the two loaves, **are the very same ones mentioned in the book of Numbers** (see 28:27), in the passage prescribing the additional offerings. You must **say when you reach** the **bulls and rams** that are enumerated in the two passages that the offerings mentioned in one **are not those** mentioned in the other, as the number of bulls and rams are not equal. Whereas in Leviticus the verse requires the sacrifice of one bull and two rams, in Numbers the verse requires the sacrifice of two bulls and one ram. **Rather,** the two passages are referring to different offerings. **These** mentioned in Numbers **come** upon the altar **for their own sake, and those** mentioned in Leviticus **come** upon the altar **for the sake of** the two **loaves.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נִמְצָא, מַה שֶּׁאָמוּר בְּחוֹמֶשׁ הַפְּקוּדִים – קָרֵב בַּמִּדְבָּר, וּמַה שֶּׁאָמוּר בְּתוֹרַת כֹּהֲנִים – לֹא קָרֵב בַּמִּדְבָּר.
> **Translation:** It is therefore **found** that the offerings **that are mentioned in the book of Numbers were sacrificed** even when the Jewish people were **in the wilderness** and could not bring the offering of the two loaves, **but** the offerings **that are mentioned in Leviticus were not sacrificed in the wilderness,** due to the fact that the two loaves could not be sacrificed in the wilderness.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְדִלְמָא פָּרִים וְאֵילִים לָאו אִינְהוּ, הָא כְּבָשִׂים אִינְהוּ נִינְהוּ? מִדְּהָנֵי אִישְׁתַּנּוֹ, הָנֵי נָמֵי דְּאַחֲרִינֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **But perhaps the bulls and rams** mentioned in Numbers **are not those** mentioned in Leviticus, **but the sheep** mentioned in Numbers **are** the same as **those** mentioned in Leviticus. The Gemara explains: **From** the fact **that these** bulls and rams **are different,** it is apparent that **those** sheep **are also different** offerings.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּפָרִים וְאֵילִים, מִמַּאי דְּאִישְׁתַּנּוֹ? דִּלְמָא הָכִי קָאָמַר רַחֲמָנָא: אִי בָּעֵי פַּר וּשְׁנֵי אֵילִים לִיקְרַב, אִי בָּעֵי שְׁנֵי פָּרִים וְאַיִל אֶחָד לִיקְרַב! מִדְּאִישְׁתַּנִּי סִדְרָן, שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ אַחֲרִינֵי נִינְהוּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But from where** is it proven **that** the **bulls and rams** in Numbers **are different** offerings than the bulls and rams mentioned in Leviticus? **Perhaps** they are actually the same offerings, and **this** is what **the Merciful One is saying: If** you **want, sacrifice a bull and two rams,** as the verse states in Leviticus; and **if** you **want, sacrifice two bulls and one ram,** as the verse states in Numbers. The Gemara answers: **From** the fact **that the order of** the offerings **is different** in the two passages, as the verse in Leviticus mentions the sheep, then the bull, and then the rams, whereas the verse in Numbers mentions the bulls, then the ram, and then the sheep, one may **conclude from it** that **they are different** offerings.

The Gemara immediately raises a question: why doesn't Ben Nannas simply learn from *tihiyena*, the same word used for the bread, as Rabbi Akiva does? Tosafot notes that while the words are not identical, the connection is direct — *tihiyena* appeared earlier in the very same passage describing the *shtei halechem*. Why not draw the obvious link?

The Gemara answers: *danin yihyu mi-yihyu, ve-ein danin yihyu mi-tihiyena*. We prefer a *gezeirah shavah* built from identical words. The principle invoked here — drawn from the *tanna de-vei Rabbi Yishmael* regarding *tzara'at*, where even words with merely similar meanings sufficed — is clarified: that leniency applies only when there is no identical word available. When an identical match exists, we must use it. Ben Nannas therefore correctly prefers the *yihyu-yihyu* connection over the *yihyu-tihiyena* one.

The Gemara then turns the question around on Rabbi Akiva: why does he not also use *yihyu mi-yihyu*, connecting to the earlier *yihyu* that referred to the animals? The answer is: *danin davar she-matanah la-kohen mi-davar she-matanah la-kohen*. The verse *kodesh yihyu l'Hashem lakohen* speaks of something that goes to the *kohen*. Both the bread and the lambs go to the *kohen* — the *kohen* eats the bread and eats the *shelamim*. But the other animals — the one bull, two rams, and seven lambs — are *olot*, which go entirely to God and not to the *kohen*. It is therefore more appropriate to link this *yihyu* to an earlier *yihyu* also associated with something given to the *kohen*, rather than to the *yihyu* of *olot* that go exclusively to God.

### Kodesh Yihyu Pasuk

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַלֶּחֶם מְעַכֵּב אֶת הַכְּבָשִׂים, מַאי טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא?
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: Failure to bring **the** two **loaves prevents** sacrifice of **the** accompanying peace offering of two **sheep,** but failure to sacrifice the two sheep does not prevent the bringing of the two loaves; this is the statement of Rabbi Akiva. The Gemara asks: **What is the reasoning of Rabbi Akiva?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גָּמַר ״יִהְיוּ״ מִ״תִּהְיֶינָה״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **He derived** the *halakha* based upon a verbal analogy between two verses. One verse states: “And the priest shall wave them with the bread of the first fruits for a wave offering before the Lord, with the two lambs; **they shall be [*yihyu*]** holy to the Lord for the priest” (Leviticus 23:20). The term “they shall be” indicates that it is essential that the offering be brought precisely as commanded, but it is unclear whether this is referring to the loaves or to the sheep brought as peace offerings. This is clarified by means of a verbal analogy **from** the verse: “You shall bring out of your dwellings two loaves of waving of two tenth parts of an ephah; **they shall be [*tihyena*]** of fine flour” (Leviticus 23:17).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה לְהַלָּן לֶחֶם, אַף כָּאן לֶחֶם.
> **Translation:** The verbal analogy teaches that **just as there** the requirement that it be done as prescribed is referring to the **loaves** rather than the sheep, **so too here** it is referring to the **loaves** rather than the sheep. Consequently, failure to bring the loaves prevents sacrifice of the sheep, but failure to sacrifice the sheep does not prevent the bringing of the loaves.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבֶן נַנָּס, גָּמַר ״יִהְיוּ״ ״יִהְיוּ״, מָה לְהַלָּן כְּבָשִׂים, אַף כָּאן כְּבָשִׂים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** how did Rabbi Shimon **ben Nannas,** who holds that failure to sacrifice the sheep prevents the bringing of the loaves but failure to bring the loaves does not prevent sacrifice of the sheep, derive his ruling? **He derived** the *halakha* through a verbal analogy between the verse: **“They shall be [*yihyu*]** holy to the Lord for the priest,” and the verse that states, concerning the seven sheep brought as burnt offerings: **“They shall be [*yihyu*]** a burnt offering to the Lord” (Leviticus 23:18). **Just as there** the requirement that it be done as prescribed is referring to the **sheep, so too here** it is referring to **sheep** rather than loaves.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבֶן נַנָּס נָמֵי נֵילַף מִ״תִּהְיֶינָה״, מָה לְהַלָּן לֶחֶם אַף כָּאן לֶחֶם? דָּנִין ״יִהְיוּ״ מִ״יִּהְיוּ״, וְאֵין דָּנִין ״יִהְיוּ״ מִ״תִּהְיֶינָה״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **And** according to Rabbi Shimon **ben Nannas also, we should derive** the *halakha* based upon a verbal analogy **from** the word ***tihyena*** as Rabbi Akiva does, and conclude that **just as there** it is referring to the **loaves, so too here** it is referring to the **loaves.** The Gemara responds: It is preferable to **derive** the meaning of the term ***yihyu* from** a verbal analogy using the identical form ***yihyu*, and** one should **not derive** the meaning of the term ***yihyu* from** a verbal analogy using the term ***tihyena*.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי נָפְקָא מִינַּהּ? הָתָנָא דְּבֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל: ״וְשָׁב הַכֹּהֵן״ ״וּבָא הַכֹּהֵן״ – זֶהוּ שִׁיבָה, זֶהוּ בִּיאָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What difference is** there whether or not the words are identical? **Didn’t the school of Rabbi Yishmael teach** a verbal analogy with regard to leprosy of houses? The verse states: **“And the priest shall return [*veshav*]** on the seventh day” (Leviticus 14:39), and another verse with regard to the priest’s visit seven days later states: **“And the priest shall come [*uva*]** and look” (Leviticus 14:44). **This returning** and **this coming** have the same meaning, and one can therefore derive by verbal analogy that the *halakha* which applies if the leprosy had spread at the conclusion of the first week likewise applies if it had spread again by the end of the following week. Certainly, if the *halakha* can be derived via a verbal analogy with the words *veshav* and *uva*, the even slighter difference in form between *yihyu* and *tihyena* should not prevent the application of a verbal analogy.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנֵי מִילֵּי הֵיכָא דְּלֵיכָּא דְּדָמֵי לֵיהּ, אֲבָל הֵיכָא דְּאִיכָּא דְּדָמֵי לֵיהּ – מִדְּדָמֵי לֵיהּ יָלְפִינַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **This matter** applies only **when there are no** terms that are **identical to it. But where there are** terms that are **identical to it, we derive** the verbal analogy **from** terms **identical to it** rather than from the terms that are not precisely identical. Consequently, it is preferable to derive the meaning of the term *yihyu* from the identical term rather than from *tihyena*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא נָמֵי לֵילַף ״יִהְיוּ״ מִ״יִּהְיוּ״? דָּנִין דָּבָר שֶׁמַּתָּנָה לַכֹּהֵן מִדָּבָר שֶׁמַּתָּנָה לַכֹּהֵן, לְאַפּוֹקֵי הָנֵי דְּעוֹלוֹת נִינְהוּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **And let Rabbi Akiva also derive** the meaning of the term ***yihyu* from** a verbal analogy to the identical term ***yihyu*,** as Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas does. The Gemara responds: Rabbi Akiva holds that it is preferable to **derive** the *halakha* concerning **an item that** is given as **a gift to the priest,** such as the loaves or the sheep brought as peace offerings, which are the subject of Leviticus 23:20, **from** the *halakha* concerning **an item that** is also **a gift to the priest,** i.e., the two loaves, which are the subject of Leviticus 23:17. This is **to the exclusion of these** seven sheep that are mentioned in Leviticus 23:18, **which are burnt offerings** and wholly consumed by the altar, and are not a gift to the priest. Consequently, it is preferable to derive the meaning of the term *yihyu* in verse 20 from the term *tihyena* in verse 17, rather than from the term *yihyu* in verse 18.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא, בִּקְרָא גּוּפֵיהּ קָא מִיפַּלְגִי: ״קֹדֶשׁ יִהְיוּ לַה׳ לַכֹּהֵן״, רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא סָבַר: אֵי זֶהוּ דָּבָר שֶׁכּוּלּוֹ לַכֹּהֵן? הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר: זֶה לֶחֶם.
> **Translation:** The Gemara presents an alternative explanation for the basis of their divergent opinions: **And if you wish, say** instead that they **disagree about the** interpretation of the **verse itself: “They shall be [*yihyu*] holy to the Lord for the priest”** (Leviticus 23:20). **Rabbi Akiva holds: Which item is entirely** given **to the priest? You must say** that **it is** the loaves of **bread.** Therefore he concludes that the word *yihyu* is referring to the loaves of bread, and if they are not sacrificed, the two sheep cannot be sacrificed as peace offerings.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבֶן נַנָּס: מִי כְּתִיב ״קֹדֶשׁ יִהְיוּ לַכֹּהֵן״?! ״קֹדֶשׁ יִהְיוּ לַה׳ לַכֹּהֵן״ כְּתִיב, אֵיזֶהוּ דָּבָר שֶׁמִּקְצָתוֹ לַה׳ וּמִקְצָתוֹ לַכֹּהֵן? הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר: אֵלּוּ כְּבָשִׂים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** how would Rabbi Shimon **ben Nannas** respond to this? He would say: **Is it** in fact **written: They shall be [*yihyu*] holy for the priest,** in which case one should interpret it as Rabbi Akiva does? **It is written** in the verse: **“They shall be [*yihyu*] holy to the Lord for the priest.”** Therefore, it should be understood in the following manner: **Which item is partially** given **to the Lord and partially** given **to the priest? You must say** that **it is** the **sheep,** which are sacrificed as a peace offering, part of which is burned on the altar and part of which is consumed by the priests. Consequently, the word *yihyu* should be understood as referring to the sheep, and if they are not sacrificed, the two loaves cannot be sacrificed either.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא, מִי כְּתִיב ״קֹדֶשׁ יִהְיוּ לַה׳ וְלַכֹּהֵן״? ״לַה׳ לַכֹּהֵן״ כְּתִיב, כִּדְרַב הוּנָא, דְּאָמַר רַב הוּנָא: קְנָאוֹ הַשֵּׁם וּנְתָנוֹ לַכֹּהֵן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** how would **Rabbi Akiva** respond to this? He would say: **Is it** in fact **written: They shall be [*yihyu*] holy for the Lord and for the priest? It is written** in the verse: “They shall be [*yihyu*] holy **to the Lord for the priest.”** Therefore, it should be understood to mean that it is given to the Lord, and it is then given by Him entirely to the priests, **in accordance with** the statement of **Rav Huna, as Rav Huna says: The Lord acquired it** initially **and** then **gave it to the priest.**

The Gemara adds a second approach to the debate: *ve-iba'it eima bi-kra gufei kamifligi* — perhaps they are debating the *pasuk* itself. The verse *kodesh yihyu l'Hashem lakohen* is written at the conclusion of the section describing both the bread and the two lambs, and both Rabbi Akiva and Ben Nannas read the same word *yihyu* as pointing to only one of the two components.

Rabbi Akiva asks: *eizehu davar she-kulo la-kohen?* — which of these two components goes entirely to the *kohen*? It must be the bread. The lambs are *shelamim*, and the *eimurim* — the fats and certain innards — go to the altar. So the lambs do not go fully to the *kohen*. Therefore *yihyu l'Hashem lakohen* refers to the bread, which passes entirely to the *kohen*.

Ben Nannas counters: *mi ksiv kodesh yihyu la-kohen?* The verse does not say simply *la-kohen*. It says *l'Hashem lakohen* — to God, and to the *kohen*. *Eizehu davar she-miktzaso la-Shem u-miktzaso la-kohen?* Which component is partly to God and partly to the *kohen*? That is the lambs, which are *shelamim* — the *eimurim* go to God and the meat goes to the *kohen*.

Rabbi Akiva's rejoinder: *mi ksiv kodesh yihyu l'Hashem ve-la-kohen?* The conjunction *ve-* is not there. The verse says *l'Hashem lakohen*, not *l'Hashem ve-la-kohen*. The formulation follows the principle articulated by Rav Huna: *kana'ah Hashem u-netanah la-kohen* — God acquired it and gave it to the *kohen*. This is the general principle that *kohanim mishulchan gavoah zachu* — the *kohanim* eat from God's table, as it were. When a *korban* is consumed by the *kohanim*, it is not simply given to them; rather, it passes through God to them. The *kohen*'s eating is conceptually similar to the altar's consumption and is part of the *kapara* process. Accordingly, *l'Hashem lakohen* means it goes to God, and from God passes fully to the *kohen* — which describes the bread, not the lambs.

Whether the debate turns on the *gezeirah shavah* from *yihyu* and *tihiyena*, or on the internal reading of *kodesh yihyu l'Hashem lakohen*, the Gemara frames the dispute as a technical question about which component the *yihyu* is pointing to — not, as the *mishnah* might have suggested, a purely conceptual argument about which offering is the *ikar*.

## Zikah & One Unit



[DAF:46a]
### Rabbi Yochanan Zikah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: הַכֹּל מוֹדִים
> **Translation:** § With regard to the dispute in the mishna about whether failure to sacrifice the two sheep as peace offerings prevents the bringing of the two loaves or vice versa, **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: Everyone concedes**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁאִם הוּזְקְקוּ זֶה לָזֶה שֶׁמְּעַכְּבִין זֶה אֶת זֶה. (וְאֵיזֶה הוּא) [וְאֵיזוֹ הִיא] זִיקָּה שֶׁלָּהֶן – שְׁחִיטָה.
> **Translation:** **that if** they **became bound to each other** and then one of them became lost, **that** the lost item **prevents** fulfillment of the mitzva with **the other,** i.e., the remaining item is unfit and must be burned. Rabbi Yoḥanan clarifies: **And what is** it that establishes **their bond?** It is the **slaughter** of the sheep. If the loaves existed at the time of the slaughter, then the loaves and sheep are sanctified as one unit. Consequently, if one of them is lost, the other is unfit and must be burned.

Rabbi Yochanan articulates a foundational principle: *hakol modim she-im huzkequ zeh la-zeh she-me'akvin zeh et zeh* — everyone agrees that once the bread and the lambs have become connected to one another, they are mutually *me'akev*. Even Rabbi Akiva, who holds that the bread can be brought without the lambs, concedes that when they come together and that connection is established, they function as a single unit. If any component is then missing, the whole is invalid.

The question is: what act creates this connection — this *zikah*? Rabbi Yochanan answers: *shechita*. When the lambs are slaughtered, that act of *shechita* is *mekadesh* the bread, conferring upon it *kedushat haguf*. Prior to *shechita*, the bread possesses only *kedushat damim* — it is owned by the *beis hamikdash* but not yet sanctified in body. Removing it from the *Mikdash* would not invalidate it, since *kedushat haguf* has not yet been conferred. Once the lambs are slaughtered, the bread acquires *kedushat haguf*, and at that moment the entire offering becomes one coherent unit. This also connects to the point at the end of Rabbi Shimon's passage in the *mishnah*: there must be some act of *hakravah* that gives the bread its significance, and it is the *shechita* of the lambs that supplies it.

This is conceptually parallel to what was said earlier regarding the *musaf* offerings: once you have the full set of components, that defines the whole as a package, and the loss of any one piece renders the whole invalid. Here too, once the *shechita* of the lambs establishes the *zikah*, the bread and lambs are that package.

It is worth noting the word *zikah* itself, familiar to anyone who has learned Yevamos. There, *zikah* describes the quasi-marital connection created between a surviving brother and his sister-in-law by virtue of the deceased brother — a connection that can manifest fully as *yibum* or be severed through *chalitza*. Here the same word appears in an entirely different context, describing the bond that links the bread and lambs into a single sacrificial unit. The word in both contexts captures the same essential concept: a legally meaningful linkage between two entities.

Regarding Rabbi Akiva's position within this framework: one might have thought that since Rabbi Akiva permits bringing the bread without the lambs, he would view the two components as always operating somewhat independently, even when brought together. The Gemara does not accept this. Even Rabbi Akiva agrees that the normative mode of bringing the bread is through the lambs — the *shechita* of the lambs and the sprinkling of their blood constitutes the act of *hakravah* that validates the offering. When they come together, they work as a unit through the lambs. Rabbi Akiva's leniency — allowing the bread to function independently — applies only in exceptional circumstances, not to the normal bringing of the offering. What makes the placement of *zikah* at *shechita* surprising, rather than at *tenufah*, which is performed jointly with both the lambs and the bread present and precedes *shechita*, is a question the Gemara will take up directly.

### Tenufah vs Shechita

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר עוּלָּא: בָּעוּ בְּמַעְרְבָא – תְּנוּפָה עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה, אוֹ אֵינוֹ עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה?
> **Translation:** With regard to the establishment of the bond between the loaves and the sheep, **Ulla said** that the Sages **in the West,** Eretz Yisrael, **raise a dilemma:** Does **waving** of the sheep and loaves before the sheep are slaughtered **establish a bond** between the sheep and the loaves, such that if one is lost the other becomes unfit, **or does it not establish a bond** between them?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פְּשׁוֹט לֵיהּ מִדְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן, דְּאָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: שְׁחִיטָה עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה, מִכְּלָל דִּתְנוּפָה אֵינָהּ עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: Why is there a dilemma about this issue? **Resolve it from** the statement **of Rabbi Yoḥanan, as Rabbi Yoḥanan says** that **slaughter** of the sheep **establishes a bond** between sheep and the loaves. One can conclude **by inference** that **waving,** which precedes the slaughter, **does not establish a bond** between them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן גּוּפָא קָא מִיבַּעְיָא לֵיהּ: מִיפְשָׁט פְּשִׁיטָא לֵיהּ לְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן דִּשְׁחִיטָה עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה, וּתְנוּפָה אֵינוֹ עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה? אוֹ דִלְמָא, שְׁחִיטָה פְּשִׁיטָא לֵיהּ, וּתְנוּפָה מְסַפְּקָא לֵיהּ? תֵּיקוּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: It is with regard to the statement **of Rabbi Yoḥanan itself** that Ulla **raises the dilemma:** Is it **obvious to Rabbi Yoḥanan that slaughter establishes a bond** between them **but waving does not establish a bond** between them? **Or perhaps it is obvious to him** that **slaughter** establishes a bond between them, **but he is uncertain** as to whether or not **waving** establishes a bond between them. According to the second possibility, the reason that he mentioned slaughter is that he was certain about it. The Gemara notes that the question **shall stand** unresolved.

The Gemara cites a question that arose in Eretz Yisrael: *tnufa osa zikah o eina osa zikah?* Before the lambs are slaughtered, the *kohen* waves both the lambs and the bread together in the act of *tenufah*. Perhaps this waving — which is performed jointly with both components present — is itself sufficient to define them as a unit?

The Gemara initially proposes to resolve this from Rabbi Yochanan's statement: since Rabbi Yochanan says *shechita* creates *zikah*, one might infer that *tenufah*, which precedes *shechita*, does not. The Gemara rejects this inference, noting that Rabbi Yochanan's statement may itself be the subject of the question. Perhaps Rabbi Yochanan merely meant to establish with certainty that *shechita* creates *zikah*, without ruling out the possibility that *tenufah* creates it earlier.

The question turns on a meaningful distinction. The *shechita* of the lambs demonstrably changes the legal status of the bread — it is what confers *kedushat haguf* upon it, as established above. *Tenufah*, by contrast, is a ritual act performed jointly over both components, but it does not by itself alter the status of the bread in any halakhic sense. The question, then, is whether a shared ritual act is sufficient to bind two components into a single unit, or whether some concrete halakhic impact on one component — a change of status — is required before we can speak of *zikah*. The Gemara leaves this unresolved: *teiku*.

### Shechita Makes Zikah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַבִּי יְהוּדָה בַּר חֲנִינָא לְרַב הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: וְהָא כִּי כְּתִיב ״קֹדֶשׁ יִהְיוּ לַה׳ לַכֹּהֵן״ בָּתַר תְּנוּפָה כְּתִיב, וּפְלִיגִי בֶּן נַנָּס וְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yehuda bar Ḥanina said to Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua: But when it is written: “They shall be holy to the Lord for the priest”** (Leviticus 23:20), it is **written** immediately **after** the verse mentions **waving, and** nevertheless Rabbi Shimon **ben Nannas and Rabbi Akiva disagree,** based on this phrase, about whether the loaves can be brought without the sheep or the sheep can be sacrificed without the loaves. This indicates that the waving does not establish a bond between these two items.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִיטַעְמָיךְ, בָּתַר תְּנוּפָה וְלָא בָּתַר שְׁחִיטָה?!
> **Translation:** Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua, replied to Rabbi Yehuda bar Ḥanina: **And according to your reasoning** that one can infer the *halakha* based upon the placement of the phrase: “They shall be holy to the Lord for the priest,” is this phrase referring to the time **after waving but not after** the **slaughter** of the sheep? After all, the verse speaks of giving them to the priest, which is done after the sheep have been slaughtered. How then did Rabbi Shimon ben Nannas and Rabbi Akiva disagree concerning this verse?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מַאי אִית לָךְ לְמֵימְרָא, מֵעִיקָּרָא קָאֵי, וּמַאי ״קֹדֶשׁ יִהְיוּ לַה׳ לַכֹּהֵן״ – דָּבָר שֶׁסּוֹפוֹ לַכֹּהֵן. הָכָא נָמֵי, דָּבָר שֶׁסּוֹפוֹ לַכֹּהֵן.
> **Translation:** **Rather, what do you have to say** concerning this verse? It must be **referring** to a time **before** the slaughter, **and what** is meant by: **“They shall be holy to the Lord for the priest”?** It means **an item that is ultimately** given **to the priest. Here too,** one can explain that the verse is referring to a time before the waving, and it means **an item that is ultimately** given **to the priest.** The issue of whether or not waving establishes a bond between the sheep and loaves therefore remains an open question.

An attempt is made to resolve the question from a textual argument: the verse *kodesh yihyu l'Hashem lakohen* is written after the *tenufah* in the *parasha*, yet Rabbi Akiva and Ben Nannas still debate based on it — implying that even after the waving, only one component is *me'akev* the other, not both. If *tenufah* had created *zikah*, they would both be mutually *me'akev* and the debate would be moot. This would seem to prove that *tenufah* does not create *zikah*.

The Gemara deflects this: the verse appears at the end of the entire section, after *tenufah*, after *shechita*, and after the entire offering is brought. It cannot be read as speaking to any particular stage in the process. *Ela meiikara ka'ei* — the verse refers back to an earlier status, describing what the offering was at a prior point, before either waving or slaughtering. *Davar she-sofo la-kohen* — it describes something that would ultimately reach the *kohen*, not necessarily something already at that stage.

The Gemara then returns to the direct question of whether *shechita* truly creates *zikah*, and introduces a *baraita* about the *todah* offering as a challenge.

## Todah Case Study



### Bread Break Before

The *baraita* is elegantly structured, examining three types of invalidity that can befall the bread of a *todah* — a bread breaking, the bread going *yotzei* outside its proper boundary, and the bread becoming *tamei* — each considered at three points in time: before *shechita*, after *shechita*, and after *zerikah* (the sprinkling of the blood). The *todah* comes with forty loaves: ten of *chametz* and thirty of *matzah*, spread across four types, and the bread is eaten within Yerushalayim.

The underlying assumption is that if even one loaf is incomplete — a piece has broken off — the entire collection of bread is invalid. Rashi acknowledges uncertainty about the precise derivation of this rule, suggesting a possible analogy to the *lechem hapanim*.

If a piece of bread broke before *shechita*: there is no problem. Anything that occurs before *shechita* can be corrected. The broken loaf is replaced — *yavi lechem acher ve-shofre* — and the *korban* proceeds normally.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּשְׁחִיטָה עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה? ורְמִינְהִי: עַד שֶׁלֹּא שְׁחָטָהּ נִפְרַס לַחְמָהּ – יָבִיא לֶחֶם אַחֵר וְשׁוֹחֵט.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** is it so that **slaughter establishes a bond** between them? **And** the Gemara **raises a contradiction** to this from a *baraita* concerning a thanks offering, which consists of an animal offering accompanied by forty loaves. The *baraita* states: If one of **its** accompanying **loaves broke before he slaughtered** the thanks offering, **he should bring another loaf and slaughter** the thanks offering.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִשֶּׁשְּׁחָטָהּ נִפְרַס לַחְמָהּ – הַדָּם יִזָּרֵק, וְהַבָּשָׂר יֵאָכֵל, וִידֵי נִדְרוֹ לֹא יָצָא, וְהַלֶּחֶם פָּסוּל.
> **Translation:** But if one of **its** accompanying **loaves broke once he slaughtered** the thanks offering, it is not possible to bring another loaf because the loaves of a thanks offering are sanctified through the slaughter of the animal, which has already taken place. Consequently, **the blood** should **be sprinkled** on the altar **and the meat** should **be eaten, but he has not fulfilled his vow** to bring a thanks offering, **and the loaves are** all **unfit.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נִזְרַק הַדָּם, תּוֹרֵם מִן הַשָּׁלֵם עַל הַפָּרוּס.
> **Translation:** If one of its accompanying loaves broke after **the blood was sprinkled** on the altar, the loaves are not deemed unfit and the individual has fulfilled his vow to bring a thanks offering. **He separates** the four loaves for the priest **from the whole** loaves **for the broken** loaf. The priest receives whole loaves and not the broken one.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עַד שֶׁלֹּא שְׁחָטָהּ יָצָא לַחְמָהּ – מַכְנִיסָהּ וְשׁוֹחֵט.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: If one of **its** accompanying **loaves left** the confines of the walls of Jerusalem **before he slaughtered** the thanks offering, it is not unfit because the loaves were not yet sanctified by the slaughter. Therefore, **he brings it** back **into** the city **and slaughters** the thanks offering.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִשֶּׁשְּׁחָטָהּ יָצָא לַחְמָהּ – הַדָּם יִזָּרֵק, וְהַבָּשָׂר יֵאָכֵל, וִידֵי נִדְרוֹ לֹא יָצָא, וְהַלֶּחֶם פָּסוּל. נִזְרַק הַדָּם – תּוֹרֵם מִמַּה שֶּׁבִּפְנִים עַל שֶׁבַּחוּץ.
> **Translation:** If one of **its** accompanying **loaves left** the confines of the walls of Jerusalem **once he slaughtered** the thanks offering, the loaves are rendered unfit. Consequently, **the blood** should **be sprinkled** on the altar **and the meat** should **be eaten, but he has not fulfilled his vow** to bring a thanks offering **and the loaves are** all **unfit.** If one of the loaves left the confines of the walls of Jerusalem after **the blood was sprinkled** on the altar, the remainder of the offering is fit, and **he separates** the four loaves for the priest **from the** ones **that** remained **inside** the city **for the loaf that** went **outside** the walls of the city.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עַד שֶׁלֹּא שְׁחָטָהּ נִטְמָא לַחְמָהּ – מֵבִיא לֶחֶם אַחֵר וְשׁוֹחֵט; מִשֶּׁשְּׁחָטָהּ נִטְמָא לַחְמָהּ – הַדָּם יִזָּרֵק, וְהַבָּשָׂר יֵאָכֵל, וִידֵי נִדְרוֹ יָצָא, שֶׁהַצִּיץ מְרַצֶּה עַל הַטָּמֵא, וְהַלֶּחֶם פָּסוּל.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: If one of **its** accompanying **loaves became impure before he slaughtered** the thanks offering, **he brings another loaf and slaughters** the thanks offering. But if one of **its** accompanying **loaves became impure once he slaughtered** the thanks offering, then **the blood** should **be sprinkled** on the altar **and the meat** should **be eaten, and he has fulfilled his vow** to bring a thanks offering. This is due to the fact **that the frontplate effects acceptance of** offerings that are **impure.** Nevertheless, **the loaf** that became impure **is unfit,** as the frontplate effects acceptance of the offering but does not render impure items pure.

### Bread Break After


If the bread broke after *shechita*: the situation cannot be repaired. The *shechita* has already conferred *kedushat haguf* upon the bread and defined the offering as a unit; no substitution is possible at this stage. The *baraita* rules: *hadam yizarek ve-habasar ye'achel, ve-yidei nidro lo yatza* — sprinkle the blood and eat the meat, but the owner has not fulfilled his obligation of *todah*. Why? Because the offering was effectively brought without its bread. The entire collection of bread is invalid; the single broken piece invalidates all forty loaves, and since it cannot be replaced after *shechita*, the animal is brought as a *korban* without bread. A *todah* without bread is a *shelamim*. Rashi understands that one must have *kavanah* at the time of *zerikah* to bring it as *shelamim*; alternatively, it may revert automatically, analogous to the laws of *pesach* brought at other times of the year. Either way, the owner has brought a *shelamim*, not a *todah*, and his obligation remains unfulfilled. The bread, now invalid, is burned.

If the bread broke after *zerikah*: the *korban* is already valid. Once the blood has been sprinkled with all forty loaves present, the owner has discharged his obligation of *todah*, and any subsequent defect affects only what may be eaten, not the standing of the offering itself. The *baraita* rules: *tormin min hashaleim al haparus* — the four loaves given to the *kohen* are taken from the whole loaves, not from the broken one, since a *kohen* must receive a whole loaf; Rashi derives this from a separate *pasuk*. The remaining loaves are eaten normally, and the broken loaf is burned. It is worth noting that at this stage one might have thought the breaking does not invalidate at all — after *zerikah* the bread is destined for eating anyway, and it would be strange to say that a loaf becomes *pasul* the moment one begins to eat it. Indeed, it may well be that after *zerikah* a break does not render the bread *pasul* in any formal sense; the only operative rule is the technical requirement that the *kohen* receive a whole loaf, not a broken one.

### Yotzei Before Shechita

If the bread went outside Yerushalayim before *shechita*: no problem. At this stage the bread has only *kedushat damim*, not *kedushat haguf*. Removing property of the *beis hamikdash* from Yerushalayim does not invalidate it, since the prohibition of *yotzei* that renders a *korban* invalid applies only once *kedushat haguf* is present. The solution is simply *machziro* — bring the bread back in — and proceed to *shechita*.

### Yotzei After Shechita

If the bread went outside Yerushalayim after *shechita*: now the bread has acquired *kedushat haguf* through the act of *shechita*, and removing it from Yerushalayim invalidates it. The blood has not yet been sprinkled. The *baraita* rules identically to the case of bread breaking after *shechita*: *hadam yizarek ve-habasar ye'achel ve-yidei nidro lo yatza* — proceed with the *korban*, but you have not fulfilled your obligation of *todah*. The animal reverts to *shelamim*. The bread that went outside is invalid, *ve-halechem pasul*.

If the blood was sprinkled before the bread went outside — *nizrak hadam* — then the *korban* was already valid at the moment of *zerikah*, and the owner has fulfilled his *todah* obligation. The loaves that subsequently went outside cannot be eaten, but the remaining bread inside Yerushalayim is valid. The *kohen* is given his portion — four whole loaves — from the bread that remained inside: *torem mimah shebifnim al shebichutz*.

### Tumah Before Shechita

If the bread became *tamei* before *shechita*: it can be corrected. Bring new bread and proceed to *shechita* — *meivi lechem acher ve-shochet*.

### Tumah After Shechita

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נִזְרַק הַדָּם – תּוֹרֵם מִן הַטָּהוֹר עַל הַטָּמֵא.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* concludes: If one of its accompanying loaves became impure after **the blood was sprinkled** on the altar, **he separates** the four loaves that are given to the priest **from the** loaves that remained **pure for the impure** loaf.


If the bread became *tamei* after *shechita*: the situation is different from the other cases of post-*shechita* invalidity. Here the *baraita* rules: *hadam yizarek ve-habasar ye'achel, u-videi nidro yatza* — the owner *does* fulfill his obligation. Why? *She-ha-tzitz meratzeh al ha-tamei* — the *tzitz*, the golden forehead-plate of the High Priest, atones for and overrides problems of *tumah* in *korbanos*. This requires some analysis. It is not the *korban* itself that is *tamei* — the lambs are *tahor* — it is the bread. The *zerikah* therefore proceeds without any *tzitz* needed to permit it. The question is only whether a *todah* was validly brought, i.e., whether the *dam* was sprinkled in the context of a *todah* that had its bread present. The *tzitz* effectively renders the *tumah* of the bread transparent for purposes of the *korban*'s validity: it is treated as though the bread were present and valid, and the offering counts as a *todah*. The owner is therefore *yotzei*. The bread itself remains *pasul* — the *tzitz* does not permit eating *tamei* bread — but vis-à-vis the validity of the *korban*, it is counted as a *todah*.

After *zerikah* — if the blood was sprinkled while the bread was *tahor* and only then a loaf became *tamei*: the *korban* is fully valid, the obligation fully fulfilled. The *kohen* receives his four loaves from the *tahor* bread — *torem min ha-tahor al ha-tamei* — the *tamei* loaf is disposed of, and the owner eats the remaining *tahor* bread.

### Post-Shechita Defects

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי סָלְקָא דַעְתָּךְ שְׁחִיטָה עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה, כֵּיוָן דְּהוּזְקְקוּ זֶה לָזֶה בִּשְׁחִיטָה, אִיפְּסִיל לֵיהּ לֶחֶם – תִּיפְּסֵל נָמֵי תּוֹדָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comes to its question: **If it enters your mind** to say that **slaughter establishes a bond** between the sheep and the two loaves of *Shavuot*, and similarly between the animal offering and the loaves of a thanks offering, then in the cases where a loaf became unfit after the animal was slaughtered but before the blood was sprinkled, **since** the animal and the loaves **bonded with each other through the slaughtering,** once the **loaf became unfit, the thanks offering should also become unfit.** Consequently, the blood of the offering should not be sprinkled on the altar and the meat should not be eaten, contrary to what is stated in the *baraita*.

The *baraita* establishes a clear structural principle: the moment of *shechita* is decisive. Everything that goes wrong before *shechita* can be remedied; everything that goes wrong after *shechita* — whether breaking, *yotzei*, or *tumah* — cannot be corrected by substitution, and the offering either reverts to *shelamim* (in the cases of breaking and *yotzei*) or is validated by the *tzitz* (in the case of *tumah*). The bread cannot be replaced once the *shechita* has occurred.

This now generates a sharp question. We have said that *shechita* creates *zikah* — it binds the bread and the animal into one unit. If that is so, then when a problem arises after *shechita* that invalidates the bread, the *zikah* should make the whole offering invalid. The animal cannot be brought as a *shelamim* if it has been fused into one unit with the bread. Why, then, is the animal salvageable?

## Todah vs Shtei Halechem



### Torah Names Todah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁאנֵי תּוֹדָה, דְּרַחֲמָנָא קַרְיַיהּ ״שְׁלָמִים״, מָה שְׁלָמִים קְרֵבִים בְּלֹא לֶחֶם, אַף תּוֹדָה קְרֵבָה בְּלֹא לֶחֶם.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: The **thanks offering is different, as the Merciful One called it a peace offering,** as the verse states: “The sacrifice of his peace offerings for thanksgiving” (Leviticus 7:13). Consequently, **just as a peace offering is sacrificed without loaves, so too a thanks offering** can be **sacrificed without loaves.**

The Gemara answers: *shani toda de-Rachmana karyei shelamim* — the *todah* is different because the Torah itself calls it *shelamim*. The verse states *al zevach todat shelamav*, referring to the *todah* as a type of *shelamim*. From this the Gemara derives: *mah shelamim kereivim belo lechem af todah kereivah belo lechem* — just as *shelamim* is brought without bread, so too a *todah* can exist without its bread. The *todah* therefore always retains an independent *shelamim* identity to revert to, even after the *zikah* of *shechita* has been established. The bread can conceptually be separated off and the animal can continue to exist as a valid *korban*.

[DAF:46b]
### Shtei Halechem Unique

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה: אִם תִּמְצָא לוֹמַר תְּנוּפָה עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה, אָבַד הַלֶּחֶם –
> **Translation:** § **Rabbi Yirmeya says: If you say** that **waving establishes a bond** between the loaves and the sheep, then in a case where **the loaves were lost** after the waving,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָבְדוּ כְּבָשִׂים, אָבְדוּ כְּבָשִׂים – אָבַד הַלֶּחֶם.
> **Translation:** the **sheep are lost** as well, i.e., they cannot be sacrificed, and one must bring different loaves and sheep. Similarly, if the **sheep are lost** after the waving, **the loaves are** thereby **lost** as well, since a bond was established between them by means of the waving.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִם תִּמְצָא לוֹמַר, תְּנוּפָה אֵינָהּ עוֹשָׂה זִיקָּה – הֵבִיא לֶחֶם וּכְבָשִׂים וְהוּנְפוּ, וְאָבַד הַלֶּחֶם, וְהֵבִיא לֶחֶם אַחֵר – אוֹתוֹ הַלֶּחֶם טָעוּן תְּנוּפָה, אוֹ אֵינוֹ טָעוּן תְּנוּפָה?
> **Translation:** **And if you say** that **waving does not establish a bond** between the loaves and the sheep, then one can raise the following dilemma: If one **brought loaves and sheep and they were waved, and** then **the loaves were lost and** he **brought other loaves** to replace the original loaves, does **that** second set of **loaves require waving** with the sheep, as it has not yet been waved? **Or does it not require waving,** as the accompanying sheep have already been waved with the original loaves, and the sheep are the subject in the verse that serves as the source of the requirement of waving (see Leviticus 23:20)?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָבְדוּ כְּבָשִׂים – לָא תִּיבְּעֵי לָךְ, דְּוַדַּאי בָּעֵי תְּנוּפָה; כִּי תִּיבְּעֵי לָךְ – אָבַד הַלֶּחֶם.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Yirmeya clarifies the dilemma: In a case where the **sheep were lost** after the waving, **do not raise the dilemma, as** in this case **they certainly require waving,** because the primary obligation of waving is mentioned with respect to the sheep, and these sheep have not yet been waved. **When should you raise the dilemma?** You should raise it in a case where **the loaves were lost** after the waving.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַלִּיבָּא דְּבֶן נַנָּס, לָא תִּיבְּעֵי לָךְ, דְּאָמַר כְּבָשִׂים עִיקָּר. כִּי תִּיבְּעֵי לָךְ אַלִּיבָּא דְּרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא, דְּאָמַר לֶחֶם עִיקָּר – מַאי?
> **Translation:** **And according to** the opinion **of** Rabbi Shimon **ben Nannas,** who holds that failure to sacrifice the sheep prevents one from sacrificing the loaves, **do not raise the dilemma, as he says** that the **sheep are primary.** Consequently, since the sheep have been waved, there is no need to repeat the waving. **When should you raise the dilemma?** Raise it **according to** the opinion **of Rabbi Akiva,** who holds that failure to bring the loaves prevents one from sacrificing the sheep, **as he says** that the **loaves are primary.** According to his opinion, **what** is the *halakha* concerning the loaves that are brought as replacements?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֵּיוָן דְּלֶחֶם עִיקָּר – בָּעֵי תְּנוּפָה, אוֹ דִלְמָא כֵּיוָן דְּמַתִּירִין דִּידֵיהּ כְּבָשִׂים נִינְהוּ – לָא צְרִיךְ תְּנוּפָה. תֵּיקוּ.
> **Translation:** On the one hand, one might say that **since the loaves are primary** and this set of loaves has not yet been waved, **it requires waving. Or** on the other hand, **perhaps** one should say that **since its permitting factors are** the **sheep,** and they were already waved, the new set of loaves **does not require waving.** The Gemara concludes that the question **shall stand** unresolved.

The *shtei halechem* would seem to face the same problem. The lambs too are called *shelamim* — the verse refers to them as *shnei kevasim... lezeva'ch shelamim*. Why can they not similarly revert to an independent *shelamim* status when the bread is invalid after *shechita*?

Rashi and Tosafot note the distinction. The *todah* exists as a category of *korban* entirely independent of its bread. There is a concept of a *todah* animal; the bread is an additional component that elevates it to *todah* status, and without bread it is simply a *shelamim* of the ordinary kind. By contrast, there is no such thing as *shalmei tzibur* in the Torah apart from the *kivsei atzeret*. The entire category of *shalmei tzibur* is defined by and inseparable from the *shtei halechem*. There is no pre-existing *shelamim* identity for the lambs to revert to once they have been defined as part of the *shtei halechem* unit. Alternatively, one might frame it this way: the *todah* is fundamentally a *korban* to which bread is added as an accessory that confers *todah* status; the bread can be stripped away and the core *korban* remains. The *shtei halechem*, however, is fundamentally about the bread — the loaves are the *ikar*, and the lambs are what enable the bread to be brought. Once they form a unit, there is nothing independent for the lambs to revert to. This asymmetry is what makes the *shtei halechem* case genuinely different: once the lambs and the bread are constituted as a unit, you cannot break off one from the other, because unlike the *todah* animal there is no residual, independent *korban* status for the lambs to fall back on. The unit is all there is.

The discussion that follows elaborates on this distinction. If the answer to Abaye's later question about why only the lambs — and not the large *olah* complement — create *zikah* with the bread is to be "because only *shelamim* are linked to bread," then that principle must also explain why the *todah*'s link to its bread persists even after *shechita*. The *todah* is called *shelamim* by the Torah, and the association of bread with *shelamim* — not with *olah* — is the mechanism by which the link is created and sustained.

## Abaye's Question



### Why Only Lambs?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי לְרָבָא: מַאי שְׁנָא שְׁנֵי כְּבָשִׂים דִּמְקַדְּשִׁי לֶחֶם וּמְעַכְּבִי, וּמַאי שְׁנָא שִׁבְעָה כְּבָשִׂים וּפַר וְאֵילִים דְּלָא מְקַדְּשִׁי לֶחֶם וְלָא מְעַכְּבִי?
> **Translation:** § **Abaye said to Rava: What is different** about the **two sheep** brought as peace offerings together with the two loaves of *Shavuot*, such **that their** slaughter **sanctifies the loaves** (see 47a), **and** according to Rabbi Yoḥanan failure to sacrifice them once they have been slaughtered **prevents** the bringing of the loaves; **and what is different** about the **seven sheep, the bull, and** the two **rams** brought on *Shavuot* as an additional offering, such **that their** slaughter **does not sanctify** the **loaves, and** failure to sacrifice them **does not prevent** the bringing of the loaves?

Abaye poses his question to Rava: *mai shna shnei kevasim d'makdeshei lechem u-me'akvi?* — what distinguishes the two lambs, such that their *shechita* sanctifies the bread and makes them mutually *me'akev*? And *mai shna shiva kevasim u-far ve-eilim* — what distinguishes the seven lambs, the bull, and the two rams (written in that order in the Torah) such that their *shechita* does *not* sanctify the bread and creates no *zikah* at all? All of these animals come with the *shtei halechem*. Why should the two lambs alone be linked to the bread?

### Rava Answer



### Tenufah Qualifier

Rava answers: *hov ve-huzkequ zeh la-zeh bi-tenufah* — the Torah specified that only the bread and the two lambs are waved together in *tenufah*. This act of joint waving defines them as a single unit and explains why the *shechita* of the lambs subsequently sanctifies the bread and why they are mutually *me'akev*. The large *olah* component — the bull, rams, and seven lambs — is never waved together with the bread and therefore stands in no such relationship to it.

### Todah Counterargument

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: הוֹאִיל וְהוּזְקְקוּ זֶה לָזֶה בִּתְנוּפָה. וַהֲרֵי תּוֹדָה דְּלֹא הוּזְקְקוּ זֶה לָזֶה בִּתְנוּפָה, וּמְקַדְּשָׁא וּמְעַכְּבָא!
> **Translation:** Rava **said to** Abaye: The reason for the distinction is **because** the two sheep and the loaves brought as peace offerings **are bound to each other through the waving.** This is not so with regard to the additional offerings, which are not waved with the loaves. The Gemara challenges: **But** in the case of **a thanks offering** and its loaves, which are not waved together, **they are not bound to each other through waving, and** nevertheless the slaughter of the animal offering **sanctifies** the loaves **and** failure to sacrifice the animal offering **prevents** the bringing of the loaves. This indicates that the waving is not the critical factor.


The Gemara challenges this: *ve-harei todah de-lo huzkequ zeh la-zeh bi-tenufah* — the *todah* animal is never waved together with its bread, and yet the *shechita* of the *todah* sanctifies its bread and they are mutually *me'akev* in the sense that without the bread the offering does not count as a *todah*, and without a *todah* animal there is no reason to bring the bread. *Tenufah* cannot be the explanation for the link between the *todah* and its bread.

### Bread-Shelamim Link

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, כְּתוֹדָה – מָה תּוֹדָה שְׁלָמִים, אַף הָכָא נָמֵי שְׁלָמִים.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** the reason for the distinction is that the two sheep brought as peace offerings are **comparable to a thanks offering. Just as a thanks offering is a peace offering, so too** the two sheep are **also a peace offering.** Consequently, just as the slaughter of the thanks offering sanctifies the accompanying loaves, and failure to sacrifice the animal prevents one from bringing the loaves, the same applies with regard to the sheep peace offerings and loaves of *Shavuot*.

The Gemara therefore reframes the answer: *ela ke-todah* — the principle is like the *todah*. *Mah toda shelamim, af hacha nami shelamim* — just as by the *todah* it is the *shelamim* component that is linked to the bread, so too here it is the *shelamim* component — the two lambs — that is linked to the bread, and not the *olah* component. This is not merely a formal rule but reflects a substantive principle: whenever the Torah links bread to a *korban*, that link is always to a *shelamim*, never to an *olah*. The association of bread with *shelamim* is built into the structure of *korbanos*.

The Gemara anticipates an objection: if that is so for the *todah*, why does the large *olah* component not also create a link? After all, there are multiple *zevachim* present, and perhaps they should all share equally in the connection to the bread. The Gemara answers: *hatam leika zevachim achrini bahadei* — by the *todah* there are no other *korbanos* present, so it is clear that the *shelamim* alone carries the link. Here, where multiple *korbanos* are present, one might have thought the link should be distributed among all of them. The Gemara will resolve this through the proof from the *nazir*.

### Nazir Ram Proof

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִי דָּמֵי? הָתָם לֵיכָּא זְבָחִים אַחֲרִינֵי בַּהֲדֵיהּ, הָכָא דְּאִיכָּא זְבָחִים אַחֲרִינֵי בַּהֲדֵיהּ – לִיקְדְּשׁוּ הָנֵי וְהָנֵי!
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **Are** the two sheep of *Shavuot* and the thanks offering really **comparable? There,** in the case of the thanks offering, **there are no other animal offerings** brought **with it.** But **here,** in the case of the offerings brought on *Shavuot*, **where there are other animal offerings** brought **with it, let these** peace offerings **and those** additional offerings **sanctify** the loaves. Why is it only the sheep brought as peace offerings that sanctify the loaves?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, כְּאֵיל נָזִיר – מָה אֵיל נָזִיר, אַף עַל גַּב דְּאִיכָּא זְבָחִים אַחֲרִינִי, שְׁלָמִים הוּא דִּמְקַדְּשִׁי, מִידֵּי אַחֲרִינָא לָא, הָכָא נָמֵי לָא שְׁנָא.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** the reason for the distinction is that the two sheep brought as peace offerings are **comparable to a nazirite’s ram,** which is sacrificed as a peace offering when he completes his term of naziriteship, in addition to a lamb that he sacrifices as a burnt offering, a female lamb that he brings then as a sin offering, and the nazirite loaves (see Numbers 6:14–15). **Just as** in the case of a **nazirite’s ram, even though there are other offerings** brought with it, nevertheless **it is** the slaughter of the **peace offering that sanctifies** the nazirite loaves and **not** the slaughter of **anything else, here too,** the *halakha* **is no different,** and it is specifically the slaughter of the peace offerings that sanctifies the loaves.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָתָם מְנָלַן? דְּתַנְיָא: ״וְאֶת הָאַיִל יַעֲשֶׂה זֶבַח שְׁלָמִים לַה׳ עַל סַל הַמַּצּוֹת״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁהַסַּל בָּא חוֹבָה לָאַיִל, וּשְׁחִיטַת אַיִל מְקַדְּשָׁן. לְפִיכָךְ, שְׁחָטוֹ שֶׁלֹּא לִשְׁמוֹ – לֹא קָדַשׁוֹ הַלֶּחֶם.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And there,** in the case of the offerings of the nazirite, **from where do we** derive that it is specifically the slaughter of the peace offering that sanctifies the loaves? The Gemara answers: This is **as it is taught** in a *baraita* concerning a verse stated with regard to the offerings of the nazirite: **“And he shall offer the ram for a sacrifice of peace offerings to the Lord, with the basket of unleavened bread”** (Numbers 6:17). This verse, which connects the ram and the loaves, **teaches that the basket** of the nazirite loaves **comes** as **an obligation for the ram,** which is a peace offering, **and the slaughter of the ram sanctifies** the loaves. **Therefore,** if the slaughter was unfit, e.g., in a case where **he slaughtered** the ram **not for the sake of** a peace offering, **the loaves were not sanctified.**

The proof is brought from the ram of the *nazir*. When a *nazir* completes his term, he brings a set of *korbanos*: a lamb as an *olah*, a ewe-lamb as a *chatat*, and a ram as a *shelamim*, accompanied by a basket of *matzah* — *challot belulot bashamen* and *rekikei matzot meshuchim bashamen*. The Torah then says explicitly: *ve-et ha-ayil ya'aseh zevach shelamim l'Hashem al sal ha-matzot* — the ram shall be made a *shelamim* offering together with the basket of *matzah*. Even though an *olah* and a *chatat* are also being brought simultaneously, the Torah makes clear that the bread is associated exclusively with the *shelamim* ram, not with the other *korbanos*.

The *baraita* derives from this: *melamed she-ha-sal ba chavah la-ayil* — the basket comes as an obligation associated specifically with the ram. *Lefikach, shachto shelo lishmo, lo kidshu ha-lechem* — therefore, if the ram was slaughtered *shelo lishmo* (not for its designated purpose), the bread is not sanctified. The link between the bread and the *nazir*'s offering runs exclusively through the *shelamim* component, even in the presence of other *korbanos*.

This proves the principle definitively: bread is always associated with *shelamim*, not with *olah*. The large *olah* component of the *shtei halechem* offering — the bull, the two rams, the seven lambs — therefore creates no *zikah* with the bread. The *zikah* belongs solely to the two lambs that are *shalmei tzibur*. That is why their *shechita* sanctifies the bread, and that is why they alone are mutually *me'akev* with the bread once the *shechita* has occurred.
