[DAF:35a]
## Cheese Mishnah Review



### Context & Framework

Daf 35 opens with an abundance of practically relevant material — non-Jewish cheese, milk, bread, and a range of other foods — but before engaging the *gemara* directly, it is necessary to review a *mishnah* from 29b, which the *gemara* on our daf references repeatedly. Without familiarity with that *mishnah*, the subsequent discussion would be difficult to follow.

### R. Yishmael vs R. Yehosh.

The *mishnah* records a conversation attributed to Rabbi Yehuda, in which Rabbi Yishmael questioned Rabbi Yehoshua while they were walking together: *mipnei mah asru gevinat ovdei kochavim* — why did the sages forbid non-Jewish cheese? Rabbi Yehoshua's initial answer was *mipnei shema'amidin otah bekeivat neveilah* — because non-Jews coagulate the cheese using stomach gunk from animals that were not slaughtered through proper *shechitah*, rendering the material *neveilah*.

### Olah vs Neveilah

Rabbi Yishmael immediately challenges this reasoning. He notes that the stomach gunk of an *olah* sacrifice is even more stringent than that of a *neveilah*, and yet we know that a *kohen sheda'ato yafah* — a priest who does not mind — may eat it directly, *sorfah chayyah*, without any further preparation. Moreover, the rabbis ruled *ein nehenin velo mo'alin* — one neither derives benefit from it nor violates the laws of *me'ilah* — indicating that the stomach gunk of the *olah* does not carry a full Torah-level prohibition. The upshot of this exchange is that eating stomach gunk of a *neveilah* would not itself be the real concern, thereby undermining Rabbi Yehoshua's first explanation.

### Idol Worship Reason

Forced to offer an alternative, Rabbi Yehoshua provides a second reason: non-Jews coagulate cheese using stomach gunk from animals that were dedicated to idol worship — *bekeivat eglei avodah kochavim*. Rabbi Yishmael responds sharply: if that is the case, then such cheese should be forbidden not merely to eat but also as an *issur hana'ah* — forbidden to derive any benefit from at all — since that is the standard rule for objects of idol worship. Rabbi Yehoshua has no answer.

### Closure & Diversion

At this point, *vehisio ledavar acher* — he changes the subject entirely. Rabbi Yehoshua pivots to a question about *Shir HaShirim*: how do you read the verse, *ki tovim dodecha miyayin* — "for your loves are better than wine" — in the masculine form, *dodecha*, or the feminine, *dodaich*? Rabbi Yishmael answers that it should be read in the masculine, since the parallel phrase *lere'ach shemanecha tovim* — "for the fragrance of your oils is good" — uses the masculine form. The apparent randomness of this digression will be explained shortly.

## Peresh Rulings



### Stoned Ox vs Idol

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: שָׁאַל רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל. אָמַר רַב אַחָדְבוּי אָמַר רַב: הַמְקַדֵּשׁ בְּפֶרֶשׁ שׁוֹר הַנִּסְקָל — מְקוּדֶּשֶׁת, בְּפֶרֶשׁ עֶגְלֵי עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה — אֵינָהּ מְקוּדֶּשֶׁת. אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא סְבָרָא, וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא קְרָא.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that **Rabbi Yehuda said: Rabbi Yishmael asked** Rabbi Yehoshua a series of questions. The Gemara notes that **Rav Aḥadevoi says** that **Rav says:** With regard to **one who betroths** a woman **by** giving her **the excrement of an ox that is** to be **stoned,** the woman is **betrothed,** provided that the excrement was worth one *peruta*. Although no benefit may be derived from the ox itself, it is permitted to derive benefit from its excrement. But if one attempts to betroth her **with the excrement of calves** that were used as offerings **of idol worship, she is not betrothed,** as even their excrement is forbidden. The Gemara remarks: **If you wish, propose logical reasoning, and if you wish, cite a verse** to substantiate this claim.

The *gemara* now opens with a statement from Rav Achdavoy: one who betroths a woman using the *peresh* — the excrement or stomach gunk — of a *shor haniskal*, an ox that was stoned to death for having gored a person, the betrothal is valid (*mekudeshet*). However, one who betroths using the *peresh* of an animal used for idol worship — *eglei avodat kochavim* — the betrothal is not valid (*einah mekudeshet*). The *gemara* proceeds to explain the distinction between these two rulings in two different ways.

### Logical Reasoning

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא סְבָרָא: גַּבֵּי עֶגְלֵי עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה נִיחָא לֵיהּ בְּנִפְחֵיהּ, אֲבָל גַּבֵּי שׁוֹר הַנִּסְקָל לָא נִיחָא לֵיהּ בְּנִפְחֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates: **If you wish, propose logical reasoning: With regard to calves** used **for idol worship,** a calf’s additional **girth** derived from the excrement stored in its body **is satisfactory for** the worshipper, as fatter animals are more impressive offerings. Since the excrement is part of the offering, it is also forbidden. **But with regard to an ox that is** to be **stoned, its** additional **girth is not satisfactory for** the owner, as he gains nothing from it.

The first approach is through *sevara*, logical reasoning. In the case of an animal dedicated to idol worship, it was in fact beneficial — *nichelei benafchei* — for the animal to have this gunk inside it, since a fatter, larger-looking animal was considered more impressive for purposes of idol worship. Because the gunk serves a purpose and benefits the animal's presentation, it is considered part and parcel of the prohibited animal, and is itself forbidden. By contrast, in the case of the *shor haniskal*, there is no benefit in having the gunk inside the ox — *lo nichelei benafchei* — and therefore it retains an independent status that is not subsumed under the prohibition of the animal.

### Textual Reasoning

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא קְרָא: כְּתִיב הָכָא: ״לָא יִדְבַּק בְּיָדְךָ מְאוּמָה״, וּכְתִיב הָתָם: ״סָקוֹל יִסָּקֵל הַשּׁוֹר וְלֹא יֵאָכֵל אֶת בְּשָׂרוֹ״, בְּשָׂרוֹ אָסוּר, הָא פִּרְשׁוֹ מוּתֶּרֶת.
> **Translation:** **If you wish, cite a verse: It is written here,** with regard to an animal used for idol worship: **“And there shall cleave naught** of the dedicated thing **to your hand”** (Deuteronomy 13:18), which indicates that the entire animal is forbidden. **And it is written there,** with regard to an ox to be stoned: **“The ox shall be stoned, and its flesh shall not be eaten”** (Exodus 21:28). This teaches that **its flesh is forbidden, but its excrement is permitted.**

The second approach is through *kra*, derivation from verses. Regarding idol worship, the verse states *lo tidbak beyadcha me'uma min hacherem* — nothing shall cling to your hand from that which is *cherem*, consecrated to idolatry. The word *me'uma*, "anything at all," implies by extension even the *peresh*. Regarding the *shor haniskal*, the verse states *sakol yisakel v'hashor lo ye'achel et besaro* — the ox shall be stoned and its flesh shall not be eaten. The verse specifies *besaro*, its flesh — which implies *ha pirsho mutar*, its excrement is permitted.

### Rava's Integration

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: תַּרְוַיְיהוּ תְּנַנְהִי, מִדְּקָאָמַר לֵיהּ: מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּעֲמִידִין בְּקֵיבַת נְבֵילָה, וְקָא מַהְדַּר לֵיהּ: וַהֲלֹא קֵיבַת עוֹלָה חֲמוּרָה מִקֵּיבַת נְבֵילָה.
> **Translation:** **Rava said: We learned both** of these *halakhot* from the mishna. He elaborates: **From** the fact **that** Rabbi Yehoshua **said to** Rabbi Yishmael that cheese of gentiles is prohibited **because they curdle** it **in the stomach** contents **of** an unslaughtered **animal carcass,** one can derive the *halakha* of the excrement of an ox that is to be stoned. The reason is that Rabbi Yishmael **responded to** Rabbi Yehoshua: **But isn’t the stomach of a burnt**-offering subject to a more **stringent** *halakha* **than the stomach of** an unslaughtered **animal carcass,** and yet although one may not derive benefit from the stomach of a burnt-offering *ab initio*, if one did derive benefit from it he is not liable for misuse of property consecrated to the Temple?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִכְּלָל דְּאִיסּוּרֵי הֲנָאָה שָׁרוּ פִּרְשַׁיְיהוּ.
> **Translation:** One can learn **by inference** from here **that** with regard to animals **from which** deriving **benefit is prohibited, their excrement,** which is the content of their stomach, **is permitted.** Although deriving benefit from both a burnt-offering and an unslaughtered animal carcass is prohibited, the excrement of each is permitted. Similarly, although deriving benefit from an ox that is to be stoned is prohibited, its excrement is permitted.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִדְּקָאָמַר לֵיהּ מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּעֲמִידִין אוֹתָהּ בְּקֵיבַת עֶגְלֵי עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה, וְקָא מַהְדַּר לֵיהּ: אִם כֵּן לָמָּה לֹא אֲסָרוּהָ בַּהֲנָאָה — מִכְּלָל דַּעֲבוֹדָה זָרָה אֲסִיר פִּרְשַׁיְיהוּ.
> **Translation:** **And from** the fact **that** Rabbi Yehoshua **said to** Rabbi Yishmael: Cheese of gentiles is prohibited **because they curdle it with the stomach** contents **of calves** used for **idol worship, and** that Rabbi Yishmael **responded to him: If** that is **so, why didn’t** the Sages **prohibit** deriving **benefit** from the cheese, one may learn **by inference that** with regard to animals of **idol worship, their excrement is prohibited.** Since the cheese formed with the stomach contents of an animal of idol worship is prohibited, it is evident that the excrement formed in the stomach of such an animal is also prohibited.


Rava makes the striking observation that both of these conclusions — *tarveihu teni hi* — can in fact be derived from the very *mishnah* we reviewed at the beginning. The first derivation: the *mishnah* records that Rabbi Yehoshua initially said the prohibition on cheese was because the cheese was made with stomach gunk from a *neveilah*, and Rabbi Yishmael responded that stomach gunk from an *olah* offering — which is itself an *issur hana'ah* — is nevertheless permitted for a *kohen* to eat. This exchange implies by inference that objects which are themselves forbidden for benefit (*isurei hana'ah*) nevertheless have their *peresh* permitted. The second derivation: when Rabbi Yehoshua gave his alternative answer — that the cheese is made with stomach gunk from idol-worshipped animals — and Rabbi Yishmael countered that it should therefore be *asur behana'ah*, this implies that the *peresh* of an idol-worshipped animal is indeed forbidden.

The *gemara* then raises an interesting question. Given Rabbi Yishmael's challenge — that idol-worshipped animal gunk should be forbidden for benefit — why could Rabbi Yehoshua not have simply responded that there is no *issur* at all, since *leita l'issura be'einei*, the prohibition is no longer intact? After all, we find in other contexts (the case of *muryas*, a fish brine to which wine or vinegar is added in small amounts) that *rabbanan* did not forbid it for benefit precisely because the prohibited substance had lost its independent identity by being absorbed.

### Cheese Form Issue

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלַהְדַּר לֵיהּ, מִשּׁוּם דְּלֵיתֵיהּ לְאִיסּוּרָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ!
> **Translation:** The mishna related that rather than addressing Rabbi Yishmael’s final difficulty, Rabbi Yehoshua diverted his attention to another matter. The Gemara inquires: **But let him respond to** Rabbi Yishmael’s query by explaining that the Sages did not prohibit deriving benefit from cheese curdled in the stomach contents of an animal used for idolatry **because there is no substantive prohibited** entity in such cheese.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּהָא מוּרְיָיס, לְרַבָּנַן דְּלָא אַסְרוּהּ בַּהֲנָאָה, מַאי טַעְמָא? לָאו מִשּׁוּם דְּלֵיתֵיהּ לְאִיסּוּרָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ?
> **Translation:** The Gemara reinforces its question: After all, isn’t the *halakha* with regard to **fish stew, according to** the opinion of **the Rabbis,** an application of this rationale, **as they did not prohibit** deriving **benefit** from fish stew prepared by a gentile? **What is the reason** for this leniency? **Is it not because there is no substantive prohibited** entity in it? Although fish stew may contain the wine of a gentile, deriving benefit from it is not prohibited because the wine is not discernible. Why didn’t Rabbi Yehoshua explain that deriving benefit from cheese of a gentile is similarly permitted because it contains no substantive prohibited entity?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרִי: הָכָא, כֵּיוָן דְּאוֹקֹמֵיה קָא מוֹקֵים, חֲשִׁיב לֵיהּ כְּמַאן דְּאִיתֵיהּ לְאִיסּוּרֵיהּ בְּעֵינֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this possibility: The Sages **say** in response that **here,** with regard to cheese, **since** the rennet **curdles** it, **it is considered like** an item **that contains a substantive prohibited** entity. Although the prohibited rennet is not discernible in the cheese, it is nevertheless considered a substantive prohibited entity because it is essential to the formation of the cheese.

The *gemara* answers that cheese is categorically different. Since the stomach gunk is the very substance that gives form and structure to the cheese — *keivan de'okmei ka mokim* — it has sufficient significance to be considered as though it remains fully present and intact. Therefore, Rabbi Yehoshua could not have deflected with that argument; the *issur* is treated as though it is still *be'einei*, still extant, by virtue of the coagulating function it performs.

## Shir HaShirim Diversion



### Oil Verse Chosen

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הִשִּׂיאוֹ לְדָבָר אַחֵר וְכוּ׳. מַאי ״כִּי טוֹבִים דֹּדֶיךָ מִיָּיִן״? כִּי אֲתָא רַב דִּימִי אָמַר: אָמְרָה כְּנֶסֶת יִשְׂרָאֵל לִפְנֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, עֲרֵיבִים עָלַי דִּבְרֵי דוֹדֶיךָ יוֹתֵר מִיֵּינָהּ שֶׁל תּוֹרָה.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that Rabbi Yehoshua **diverted** Rabbi Yishmael’s attention **to another matter,** and began discussing the verse: “For your love is better than wine” (Song of Songs 1:2). The Gemara asks: **What** is the meaning of the verse: **“For your love [*dodekha*] is better than wine”? When Rav Dimi came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia, he **said: The congregation of Israel said before the Holy One, Blessed be He: Master of the Universe, the statements of Your beloved ones [*dodekha*],** i.e., the Sages, **are more pleasant to me than the wine of** the written **Torah** itself.

We now return to the puzzle of why Rabbi Yehoshua specifically chose the verse *lere'ach shemanecha tovim* — "the fragrance of your oils is good" — as his subject-changing distraction. When Rav Dimi came from *Eretz Yisrael*, he reported that *Knesset Yisrael* said before the Holy One: *Ribbono Shel Olam*, the words of your beloved ones — meaning the sages — are more beloved to me than the wine of Torah itself: *arevim alai divrei dodecha yoter mi-yeinah shel Torah*.

### Sages Over Torah

This is a remarkable statement. The words of the sages — the Oral Torah, the elaborations and interpretations of the rabbis — are described as more pleasant than the wine of the written Torah itself. It is the oral tradition that is being celebrated, its capacity to extend and illuminate, and the verse from *Shir HaShirim* becomes a vehicle for that celebration.

### Kiss Verse Citation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי שְׁנָא הַאי קְרָא דְּשַׁיְילֵיהּ? אָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן פַּזִּי, וְאִיתֵּימָא רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בַּר אַמֵּי: מֵרֵישֵׁיהּ דִּקְרָא קָאָמַר לֵיהּ, ״יִשָּׁקֵנִי מִנְּשִׁיקוֹת פִּיהוּ״. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: יִשְׁמָעֵאל אָחִי, חֲשׁוֹק שִׂפְתוֹתֶיךָ זוֹ בָּזוֹ וְאַל תִּבָּהֵל לְהָשִׁיב.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is different** about **this verse that** led Rabbi Yehoshua to **ask** Rabbi Yishmael a question specifically with regard to it? **Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi said, and some say Rabbi Shimon bar Ami** said: He chose that verse because he sought to **tell him** a message that can be derived **from the beginning of the verse: “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth”** (Song of Songs 1:2). In essence, Rabbi Yehoshua **said to him: Yishmael, my brother, press your lips one to the other, and do not be so hasty to retort,** i.e., do not persist in your questioning.

One might ask: why was this particular verse chosen as the distraction, of all the verses in *Shir HaShirim*? Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi — and some say Rabbi Shimon bar Ami — answers that the choice was dictated not by this part of the verse but by its very opening: *yishakeni mi-neshikot pihu* — "let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth." Rabbi Yehoshua was using this verse to send a message to Rabbi Yishmael: *Yishmael achi, chashok siftotecha zo be-zo ve-al tivahel le-hashiv* — Yishmael, my brother, clamp your lips together and do not be so quick to respond. The citation of *Shir HaShirim* was a pointed, if poetic, request to stop asking so many questions.

## Decree & Secrecy



### New Decree Rule

This raises a further question: why did Rabbi Yehoshua not simply say he had no answer, rather than changing the subject entirely? Ulla — and some say Rav Shmuel bar Abba — explains: *gezeirah chadashah hi ve-ein mefakpekin bah* — this was a new decree, and one does not interrogate new decrees. When a new law is issued, one does not publicly challenge or undermine it, even if a compelling logical objection could be raised.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי טַעְמָא? אָמַר עוּלָּא, וְאִיתֵּימָא רַב שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר אַבָּא: גְּזֵרָה חֲדָשָׁה הִיא, וְאֵין מְפַקְפְּקִין בַּהּ. מַאי גְּזֵירְתָּא? אָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן פַּזִּי אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ בֶּן לֵוִי: מִשּׁוּם נִיקּוּר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is the reason** that Rabbi Yehoshua instructed Rabbi Yishmael not to question him further? **Ulla says, and some say Rav Shmuel bar Abba** says: The ordinance prohibiting the cheese of gentiles **was a new decree, and** therefore **one does not scrutinize its** origins. The Gemara asks: **What** was, in fact, the reason for the Sages’ **decree** prohibiting the cheese of gentiles? **Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi says** that **Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi says:** It was **due to** the concern for **puncturing,** i.e., the concern that a snake might have deposited its venom in the cheese, as gentiles are not assumed to be careful about this.

### Snake Venom Reason


What, then, was the actual substance of this decree? Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi says in the name of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: *mishum nikur* — it was because of snake venom. The concern was that non-Jews were not careful about leaving their cheese exposed, and snakes could come and inject venom into it, rendering the cheese dangerous to eat. It is a striking reason, and the question naturally arises: if that was the reason, why was it not simply stated?

### Secrecy Period

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֵימָא לֵיהּ מִשּׁוּם נִיקּוּר! כִּדְעוּלָּא, דְּאָמַר עוּלָּא: כִּי גָּזְרִי גְּזֵירְתָּא בְּמַעְרְבָא לָא מְגַלּוּ טַעְמָא עַד תְּרֵיסַר יַרְחֵי שַׁתָּא, דִּלְמָא אִיכָּא אִינִישׁ דְּלָא סְבִירָא לֵיהּ וְאָתֵי לְזַלְזוֹלֵי בַּהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: **But** if so, **let** Rabbi Yehoshua simply **say to** Rabbi Yishmael: It is prohibited **due to** the concern for **puncturing.** Why did he choose to avoid answering? The Gemara explains: Rabbi Yehoshua reasoned **in accordance with** a statement **of Ulla, as Ulla said: When** the Sages **decreed a decree in the West,** Eretz Yisrael, **they would not reveal the reason** behind it **until twelve months** of **the year** had passed, **lest there be a person who does not agree with it and will come to treat it with contempt.**

The answer is provided by Ulla himself. When a new decree was issued in *Eretz Yisrael*, *lo megalu ta'ama ad treisar yarchei shata* — they did not reveal its reason for twelve months. The concern was that *dilma ika inish d'lo sevira leh*, perhaps there would be someone who disagreed with the reasoning, and he would come to treat the decree lightly — *atei l'zilzulei bah*. By keeping the reason secret for a full year, the practice would become established before anyone had the opportunity to argue against it. Only once people were already observing the law would the reason be disclosed. It is a telling insight into the politics of communal legislation.

## Yirmiya Challenge



### Aged Cheese Test

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מְגַדֵּף בַּהּ רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה: אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה, יְבֵשָׁה תִּשְׁתְּרֵי, יָשָׁן תִּשְׁתְּרֵי! דְּאָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: יָבֵשׁ מוּתָּר, אֵין מַנִּיחוֹ לְיַבֵּשׁ, יָשָׁן מוּתָּר, אֵין מַנִּיחוֹ לְיַשֵּׁן.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yirmeya** would **ridicule [*megaddef* ]** Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi’s explanation that the prohibition was due to the concern for puncturing: **If that is so, dry** cheese **should be permitted,** and likewise **aged** cheese should **be permitted, as Rabbi Ḥanina says:** With regard to exposure, **a dry** substance **is permitted** even if it was originally in the form of an uncovered liquid, because a snake’s venom **does not let it dry,** i.e., congeal. And **an aged** liquid **is permitted,** as a snake’s venom **does not let it age,** as it causes it to spoil instead.

Rabbi Yirmiya is dismissive of the snake venom theory — the *gemara* says *megadef bah*, he mocks it. His challenge: if the concern is truly snake venom, then dried cheese should be permitted, and aged cheese should be permitted. Why? Because Rabbi Chanina rules in the context of *gilluy* — the broader laws of exposed liquids — that dried and aged substances are not subject to the snake venom concern: *yavesh mutar ein manichin l'yavesh, yashan mutar ein manicho l'yashan*. The venom does not allow substances to dry out or age; therefore, if a substance is dry or aged, one knows it could not contain active venom. Since non-Jewish cheese is not conditionally permitted when it is aged or dried, snake venom cannot be the actual reason for the prohibition.

### Milk Residue Theory

Rabbi Chanina therefore offers a different explanation: *lefi she'i efshar lah belo tzichtzuchei chalav* — because it is impossible to make cheese without leaving residual traces of milk in the final product. The concern is that the milk used by non-Jews may have included *treif* milk mixed in with otherwise permissible milk, and those residual traces of non-kosher milk remain present in the cheese.

### Stomach Lining

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: לְפִי שֶׁאִי אֶפְשָׁר לָהּ בְּלֹא צִחְצוּחֵי חָלָב, וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּעֲמִידִין אוֹתָהּ בְּעוֹר קֵיבַת נְבֵילָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara presents two alternative reasons for this decree of the Sages. **Rabbi Ḥanina says:** The cheese is prohibited **because it is not possible for it** to have been made **without** containing **particles of** non-kosher **milk. And Shmuel says:** The cheese is prohibited **because it is curdled with the skin of the stomach of** an unslaughtered **animal carcass.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא קֵיבָה גּוּפָא שַׁרְיָא, וּמִי אָמַר שְׁמוּאֵל הָכִי? וְהָתְנַן: קֵיבַת הַגּוֹי וְשֶׁל נְבֵילָה — הֲרֵי זוֹ אֲסוּרָה,
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: Shmuel’s statement indicates that only the skin of the animal’s stomach is prohibited, whereas the contents of the **stomach,** i.e., the rennet **itself, is permitted.** The Gemara asks: **And did Shmuel** actually **say this? But didn’t we learn** in a mishna (*Ḥullin* 116a): With regard to **the stomach** contents **of** an animal slaughtered by **a gentile and** the stomach contents **of** an unslaughtered **animal carcass,** each of these **is prohibited.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָוֵינַן בַּהּ, אַטּוּ דְּגוֹי לָאו נְבֵלָה הִיא?
> **Translation:** **And we discussed it** and asked: Why does the mishna mention both an animal slaughtered by a gentile and an unslaughtered animal carcass? **Is that to say that** an animal slaughtered by **a gentile is not** classified as **an animal carcass?** By mentioning each of these separately, the mishna indicates that generally they are subject to different *halakhot*. This is difficult, as an animal slaughtered by a gentile has the halakhic status of an unslaughtered animal carcass.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר שְׁמוּאֵל: חֲדָא קָתָנֵי, קֵיבַת שְׁחִיטַת גּוֹי — נְבֵלָה אֲסוּרָה!
> **Translation:** **And** in answer to this difficulty, **Shmuel says:** The mishna **is** in fact **teaching a single** *halakha*, which is that **the stomach** contents **of** an animal **slaughtered by a gentile** are considered to be like the stomach contents of an unslaughtered **animal carcass** and are therefore **prohibited.** Earlier, Shmuel asserted that only the physical skin of an animal’s stomach is prohibited, which indicates that the stomach contents are permitted. In his explanation of the mishna in *Ḥullin*, Shmuel posits that the stomach contents of an unslaughtered animal are prohibited.

Shmuel offers yet another explanation: *mipnei shema'amidin otah b'or keivat neveilah* — because non-Jews coagulate the cheese not with the gunk itself but with an actual piece of the stomach lining of a *neveilah*, a piece of skin from the animal's stomach dropped into the milk to make it curdle. According to Shmuel, the gunk itself is not the problem — it is the stomach lining that is.

The implication of Shmuel's position is striking: *ha keiva gufa sharya* — the stomach gunk itself is permitted. But this creates a difficulty: a *mishnah* elsewhere states that *keivat oved kochavim v'shel neveilah harei zu asurah* — the stomach gunk of a non-Jew and of a *neveilah* is forbidden.

An additional difficulty with that *mishnah* is noted: the phrasing is itself strange. What does it mean to distinguish between the gunk of a non-Jew and of a *neveilah* as though they were separate categories? *Atu d'oved kochavim lav neveilah hi* — is the animal of a non-Jew not itself a *neveilah*? Shmuel resolves this: the *mishnah* is teaching that the slaughter of a non-Jew is to be treated as *neveilah* — they are in fact the same thing.

[DAF:35b]
### Shmuel Resolution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא קַשְׁיָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains that this is **not difficult:**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כָּאן קוֹדֶם חֲזָרָה, כָּאן לְאַחַר חֲזָרָה, וּמִשְׁנָה לֹא זָזָה מִמְּקוֹמָהּ.
> **Translation:** **Here,** with regard to the mishna in *Ḥullin*, Shmuel’s comment reflects the explanation of Rabbi Yehoshua **before** Rabbi Yehoshua’s **retraction** of the assertion that it is prohibited to derive benefit from the stomach contents of an animal carcass. **There,** with regard to the mishna in *Avoda Zara*, Shmuel’s statement is in accordance with the opinion of Rabbi Yehoshua **after** his **retraction** of that claim. **And** although this indicates that the mishna in *Ḥullin* presents an outdated ruling that was later rescinded, **a mishna does not move from its place.** In other words, once it has been taught in a certain manner, the *tanna* will not change the text of a mishna in order to reflect a change of opinion, so as to avoid confusion.

There remains the contradiction between Shmuel's two apparent positions — one permitting the gunk, one forbidding it. The *gemara* resolves this: *kan kodem chazra, kan le'achar chazra* — one statement reflects Shmuel's view before he changed his mind, and the other reflects his view afterward. The chronology matters here: Shmuel's earlier view, which treated the gunk as the problem, was subsequently revised when he encountered the *mishnah* in which Rabbi Yehoshua himself had changed his position — and Shmuel changed his along with it. *Umishnah lo zazah mimkomah* — the *mishnah* was not rewritten to reflect the updated position, because that is simply not how we treat established texts. The earlier version remains in place as a historical artifact of what was once taught. The practical bottom line, according to Shmuel's final position, is that the stomach gunk is permitted, and it is the stomach lining itself that is the problem.

## Multiple Reasons



### Pig Fat Reason

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב מַלְכִּיָּא מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַב אַדָּא בַּר אַהֲבָה אָמַר: מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּחְלִיקִין פָּנֶיהָ בְּשׁוּמַּן חֲזִיר. רַב חִסְדָּא אָמַר: מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּעֲמִידִין אוֹתָהּ בְּחוֹמֶץ. רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק אָמַר: מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמַּעֲמִידִין אוֹתָהּ בִּשְׂרַף הָעׇרְלָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests additional reasons for the decree of the Sages. **Rav Malkiyya says in the name of Rav Adda bar Ahava:** The cheese is prohibited **because** gentiles **smooth its surface with pig fat. Rav Ḥisda says:** It is **because they curdle it with vinegar** produced from their wine, from which it is prohibited to derive benefit. **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak says:** It is **because they curdle it with sap** that is subject to the prohibition against consuming **the fruit of a tree during the first three years after its planting [*orla*].**

The *gemara* now accumulates a striking series of additional reasons offered by various *amoraim* for the prohibition on non-Jewish cheese — a relatively unusual phenomenon. Rav Malachya in the name of Rav Ada bar Ahava states: *mipnei shemachlikim paneha beshoman chazir* — because non-Jews grease the surface or sides of their cheese with pig fat.

### Vinegar Reason

Rav Chisda offers a different reason: *mipnei shema'amidin otah bechomet* — because they use vinegar to coagulate the cheese. Since vinegar functions analogously to wine in halachic terms, this would render the cheese not merely forbidden to eat but also subject to the prohibition of benefit.

### Orla Sap Reason

Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak offers yet another reason: *mipnei shema'amidin otah b'saraf ha'orla* — because non-Jews coagulate their cheese using sap derived from a tree whose fruit is *orla*, meaning a fruit tree within its first three years of growth, from which the fruit is forbidden.

### Orla Dispute

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן? כִּי הַאי תַּנָּא, דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר אוֹמֵר: הַמַּעֲמִיד בִּשְׂרַף הָעׇרְלָה — אָסוּר, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא פֶּירִי.
> **Translation:** Parenthetically, the Gemara asks: **In accordance with whose** opinion is Rav Naḥman’s claim that the cheese of gentiles is prohibited because it is curdled in the sap of *orla*? The Gemara answers: It is **in accordance with the opinion of this *tanna*, as it is taught** in a mishna (*Orla* 1:7): **Rabbi Eliezer says:** With regard to **one who curdles** cheese **with the sap of *orla*,** the cheese is **prohibited, because** the sap **is** considered to be **fruit** of the tree.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ, עַד כָּאן לָא פְּלִיג רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ עֲלֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר אֶלָּא בְּקִטְפָא דִּגְוָזָא, אֲבָל בְּקִטְפָא דְּפֵירָא מוֹדֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: **You** may **even say** that the statement is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yehoshua,** who disagrees with the opinion of Rabbi Eliezer, as **Rabbi Yehoshua disagrees with Rabbi Eliezer only with regard to the sap of a branch, but with regard to the sap of a fruit** Rabbi Yehoshua **concedes** that it is prohibited as *orla*. Rav Naḥman’s statement can be understood as referring specifically to the sap of the fruit, which would mean that it is in accordance with the opinions of both Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַיְינוּ דִּתְנַן, אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: שָׁמַעְתִּי בְּפֵירוּשׁ, שֶׁהַמַּעֲמִיד בִּשְׂרַף הֶעָלִין וּבִשְׂרַף הָעִיקָּרִין — מוּתָּר, בִּשְׂרַף הַפַּגִּין — אָסוּר, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא פֶּירִי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara adds: **And this is** in accordance with **that** which **we learned** in the continuation of that mishna: **Rabbi Yehoshua said: I heard explicitly that** with regard to **one who curdles** cheese **with the sap of the leaves and the sap of the roots** of an *orla* tree, the cheese **is permitted.** But if it is curdled **with the sap of unripe figs it is prohibited, because** that sap **is** considered to be **fruit.**

The *gemara* asks: whose opinion does this follow? It follows the position of Rabbi Eliezer in a *beraita*: *hamamid b'saraf ha'orla asur mipnei shehu peri* — one who coagulates cheese with sap from an *orla* tree is forbidden from the result, because that sap has the legal status of fruit. The *gemara* adds that one need not limit this to Rabbi Eliezer's view — even Rabbi Yehoshua could agree here. The only point of disagreement between Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua in this area is regarding *kitpa d'goza*, the sap of a branch, which Rabbi Yehoshua holds is not considered fruit. But with regard to *kitpa d'peira* — sap that flows directly from the fruit itself — Rabbi Yehoshua agrees that it has the status of fruit and is forbidden. Rabbi Yehoshua himself says explicitly: sap of the leaves or sap of the roots used in cheese-making is *mutar*, perfectly permitted; but sap from the *paggim* — the young figs — *asur*, because *mipnei shehu peri*, it is like the fruit itself.

### Tension Unresolved

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בֵּין לְרַב חִסְדָּא, בֵּין לְרַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק, תִּתְּסַר בַּהֲנָאָה, קַשְׁיָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty against the last two suggested reasons for the decree of the Sages. **According to both Rav Ḥisda,** who holds that the cheese is prohibited because it is curdled with vinegar made from wine of gentiles, **and Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak,** who maintains that it is prohibited because it is curdled with the sap of *orla*, **one should be prohibited from** deriving **benefit** from the cheese, as one may not derive benefit from either the wine of gentiles or *orla*. The Gemara concludes: Indeed, this is **difficult.**

However, a sharp difficulty now surfaces with the vinegar reason and the *orla* sap reason. Both Rav Chisda and Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak posit reasons that would logically render the cheese forbidden not merely to eat but also as an *issur hana'ah* — since vinegar has the status of wine, and *orla* is forbidden for benefit. Yet we know that non-Jewish cheese is in fact only forbidden to eat, not forbidden for benefit. The *gemara*'s response is simply *kashya* — it is a difficulty, and it is left unresolved. This unresolved tension suggests that none of these individual reasons is fully satisfactory as a standalone explanation, and that the prohibition may have originated as a broader decree that does not reduce cleanly to any single rationale.

## Torah Scholar Homily



### Balsam Oil Parable

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דָּרַשׁ רַב נַחְמָן בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב חִסְדָּא, מַאי דִּכְתִיב: ״לְרֵיחַ שְׁמָנֶיךָ טוֹבִים״, לְמָה תַּלְמִיד חָכָם דּוֹמֶה? לִצְלוֹחִית שֶׁל פִּלְיָיטֹין: מְגוּלָּה — רֵיחָהּ נוֹדֵף, מְכוּסָּה — אֵין רֵיחָהּ נוֹדֵף.
> **Translation:** § **Rav Naḥman, son of Rav Ḥisda, interpreted** a verse **homiletically: What** is the meaning of that **which is written: “Your ointments have a goodly fragrance”** (Song of Songs 1:3)? This is a metaphor for a Torah scholar: **To what is a Torah scholar comparable? To a flask of *pelaitin*:** When it is **exposed, its scent diffuses;** when it is **covered, its scent does not diffuse.**

Rav Nachman the son of Rav Chisda expounds: what is the meaning of the verse *lere'ach shemanecha tovim* — "the fragrance of your oils is good"? To what is a *talmid chacham* compared? To a covered plate of *paltin* — balsam oil. When the plate is uncovered, *reicha nodef* — its fragrance wafts and spreads, a beautiful and pleasant smell. When it is covered, *mekhusah ein reicha nodef* — the fragrance is contained and no one benefits from it. So too the Torah scholar: when he is out in public, teaching and sharing his wisdom, the fragrance of his knowledge spreads; when he is hidden away and not teaching, the world does not benefit.

### Revealed Secrets

But the homily goes further: *v'lo od ela devarim she'mechusim mimenu mitgalim lo* — not only does his fragrance spread when he teaches, but the things that were hidden even from himself become revealed to him. Rashi explains: *mitgalim lo me-aleihen belo torach k'shehu melamdan* — they become revealed on their own, without labor, in the very act of teaching. The teacher discovers, as he teaches, depths he had not previously accessed.

### Alamot Wordplay

There now follows a wordplay on the verse *alamot ahevucha* — "young women love you." The *gemara* says: do not read *alamot*, young women, but rather *alumot*, hidden things. The hidden things will love you — that is, they will come to you, they will be revealed to you, as you engage in Torah.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֹא עוֹד, אֶלָּא דְּבָרִים שֶׁמְּכוּסִּין מִמֶּנּוּ מִתְגַּלִּין לוֹ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״עֲלָמוֹת אֲהֵבוּךָ״, קְרִי בֵּיהּ ״עֲלוּמוֹת״; וְלֹא עוֹד, אֶלָּא שֶׁמַּלְאַךְ הַמָּוֶת אוֹהֲבוֹ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״עֲלָמוֹת אֲהֵבוּךָ״, קְרִי בֵּיהּ ״עַל מָוֶת״; וְלֹא עוֹד, אֶלָּא שֶׁנּוֹחֵל שְׁנֵי עוֹלָמוֹת, אֶחָד הָעוֹלָם הַזֶּה וְאֶחָד הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״עֲלָמוֹת״, קְרִי בֵּיהּ ״עוֹלָמוֹת״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara remarks: **And moreover,** when a Torah scholar spreads his knowledge, **matters that are** generally **hidden from him are revealed to him, as it is stated: “Maidens [*alamot*] love You”** (Song of Songs 1:3), and one may **read into** the verse: **The hidden [*alumot*]. And moreover, the Angel of Death loves him, as it is stated: “Maidens [*alamot*] love You,”** and one may **read into** the verse: The one appointed **over death [*al mot*]** loves you. **And moreover,** a Torah scholar **inherits two worlds: One** is **this world, and** the other **one** is **the World-to-Come, as it is stated: “Maidens [*alamot*]** love You,” and one may **read into** the verse: **Worlds [*olamot*].**

### Two Worlds Inherit


The homily continues: *v'lo od ela she'malach hamavet ohavo* — not only that, but the Angel of Death loves him. The *gemara* reads *alamot* as *al-mavet* — he who presides over death will love the Torah scholar, which is to say, the Angel of Death cannot touch him while he is engaged in Torah learning. And finally, *v'lo od ela she'nochel shnei olamot* — he inherits two worlds, *echad olam hazeh ve'echad olam haba*, this world and the world to come. The word *alamot* is now read as *olamot* — worlds. The Torah scholar inherits worlds.

## Food Mishnah



### Forbidden to Eat

The *mishnah* now shifts to a broader enumeration: *ve-eilu devarim shel ovdei kochavim asurin ve-ein isuran issur hana'ah* — these are things associated with non-Jews that are forbidden, but whose prohibition does not extend to *issur hana'ah*, forbidden benefit. One may not eat them but one may derive benefit from them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ וְאֵלּוּ דְּבָרִים שֶׁל גּוֹיִם אֲסוּרִין וְאֵין אִיסּוּרָן אִיסּוּר הֲנָאָה: חָלָב שֶׁחֲלָבוֹ גּוֹי וְאֵין יִשְׂרָאֵל רוֹאֵהוּ, וְהַפַּת וְהַשֶּׁמֶן שֶׁלָּהֶן. רַבִּי וּבֵית דִּינוֹ הִתִּירוּ הַשֶּׁמֶן.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: This mishna lists items belonging to gentiles which it is prohibited to consume, but from which it is permitted to derive benefit. **And these are items** that belong **to gentiles** and are **prohibited, but their prohibition is not** that of **an item from which** deriving **benefit is prohibited: Milk that was milked by a gentile and a Jew did not see him** performing this action, **and their bread and oil.** The mishna notes that **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **and his court permitted the oil** of gentiles entirely.

### Non-Jewish Milk

The first item is *chalav she-chalavo oved kochavim ve-ein Yisrael ro'ehu* — milk milked by a non-Jew without Jewish supervision. This is the foundation of the well-known discussion around *chalav stam*. Rav Moshe Feinstein's *heter* — largely accepted in the Modern Orthodox community and in significant portions of the *Charedi* world in earlier generations — holds that government regulation of the dairy industry functions as a form of constructive supervision. Since the regulatory framework makes adulteration effectively impossible, it is *anan sahadei*, as if we are all present watching. Under this view, commercially produced milk is treated as *chalav Yisrael*. It is not that one abandons the requirement of *chalav Yisrael*; rather, one holds that the regulatory environment satisfies it.

### Bread & Oil


The *mishnah* continues: *ve'haphat ve'hashemen shelahem* — their bread and their oil. Regarding oil: *Rebbi u'veit dino hitiru ha-shemen* — Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and his court permitted non-Jewish oil, reversing an earlier prohibition. The *mishnah* thus records a historical leniency regarding oil.

### Brined Foods

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַשְּׁלָקוֹת, וּכְבָשִׁין שֶׁדַּרְכָּן לָתֵת לְתוֹכָן יַיִן וָחוֹמֶץ, וְטָרִית טְרוּפָה, וְצִיר שֶׁאֵין בָּהּ דָּגָה כִּלְבִּית שׁוֹטֶטֶת בּוֹ, וְהַחִילֵּק, וְקוֹרֶט שֶׁל חִלְתִּית, וּמֶלַח שְׂלָקוֹנְדִית — הֲרֵי אֵלּוּ אֲסוּרִין, וְאֵין אִיסּוּרָן אִיסּוּר הֲנָאָה.
> **Translation:** The mishna resumes its list: **And boiled and pickled** vegetables, **whose** usual **manner** of preparation involves **adding wine and vinegar to them, and minced *tarit*** fish, **and brine that does not have a *kilbit* fish floating in it, and *ḥilak*, and a sliver of *ḥiltit*, and *salkondit* salt** (see 39b); all **these are prohibited, but their prohibition is not** that of **an item from which** deriving **benefit is prohibited.**

The *mishnah* then lists a series of additional forbidden foods: *shlakot u-kvashim she-darkan latet letochan yayin ve-chometz* — cooked vegetables and brined foods into which wine or vinegar are customarily added; *tarit trufah* — a kosher fish that has been so thoroughly mashed that one cannot verify whether non-kosher fish has been mixed in; *sir she-ein ba daga kalbit shotetet bo* — fish brine in which a *kalbit* fish is not floating, since the *kalbit* fish, according to Rashi, floats only in kosher fish brine and therefore serves as a sign of kashrut; *ha-chilek* — a fish that is itself permissible but resembles a non-kosher species; *kurt shel chiltit* — a measure of asafoetida, a pungent spice used in ancient cooking and known in contemporary Indian cuisine; and *melach salkundit*. All of these are forbidden to eat but permitted for benefit.

It is worth noting that the appearance of *chiltit* here connects to a broader halachic principle discussed elsewhere in *Avodah Zarah*: *devarim charifim*, sharp and pungent substances, have the capacity to awaken dormant tastes. Even a taste that would ordinarily be considered *pagum* or neutralized by time can be reactivated when cooked or cut with something as potent as *chiltit* — a principle that is later applied to onions, garlic, and similar sharp foods.

## Milk Concerns



### Switching Worry

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ חָלָב לְמַאי נֵיחוּשׁ לַהּ? אִי מִשּׁוּם אִיחַלּוֹפֵי — טָהוֹר חִיוָּר, טָמֵא יָרוֹק! וְאִי מִשּׁוּם אִיעָרוֹבֵי — נֵיקוּם! דְּאָמַר מָר: חָלָב טָהוֹר עוֹמֵד, חָלָב טָמֵא אֵינוֹ עוֹמֵד.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara asks: Concerning **milk, with regard to what** need **we be concerned?** Why is the milk prohibited? **If** it is **due to** the concern that a gentile might **exchange** the milk of a kosher animal with the milk of a non-kosher animal, this concern is unfounded, as **kosher** milk is **white** whereas **non-kosher** milk has **a green** tinge to it, and therefore they are easily distinguishable. **And if** it is prohibited **due to** the concern that it might be **mixed** with non-kosher milk, let the Jew **curdle** the milk obtained from the gentile, **as the Master said: Milk** from **a kosher** animal **curdles,** but milk from **a non-kosher** animal **does not curdle.**

The *gemara* now probes the rationale behind the prohibition on non-Jewish milk. The challenge is direct: why worry? If the concern is *achlufi* — that a non-Jew switched out kosher milk for non-kosher milk — the answer seems obvious: *tahor chivver, tamei yarok* — kosher milk is white and non-kosher milk is a different color, greenish or yellowish. They look different. Switching would be immediately detectable.

### Cheese Test


If the concern is not outright switching but *eiruvei* — mixing a small amount of non-kosher milk into kosher milk — there is also an easy solution. One should simply attempt to make cheese from it, because *chalav tahor omed, chalav tamei eino omed* — kosher milk can be made into cheese, while non-kosher milk cannot. This is apparently a real physical phenomenon, having to do with the enzymatic composition of milk from non-kosher animals. If the milk coagulates properly, it is kosher; if not, there is a problem.

### Cheese Test Fails

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי דְּקָא בָעֵי לִגְבִינָה, הָכִי נָמֵי. הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן? דְּקָא בָעֵי לֵיהּ לְכַמְכָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **If one desires to** eat it as **cheese, indeed,** one can simply curdle it, as the milk of non-kosher animals does not curdle. **What are we dealing with here?** We are dealing with a case **where one desires to** use the milk in ***kamkha*,** also known as *kutaḥ*, a food item that contains milk.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְנִשְׁקוֹל מִינֵּיהּ קַלִּי וְנֵיקוּם! כֵּיוָן דִּבְטָהוֹר נָמֵי אִיכָּא נַסְיוּבֵי דְּלָא קָיְימִי, לֵיכָּא לְמֵיקַם עֲלַהּ דְּמִילְּתָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **But** in that case, **let him take a bit of** milk **and curdle** it, to test whether or not it has been mixed with the milk of a non-kosher animal: If it curdles completely, it is kosher; if some milk is left over, it is not. The Gemara explains: **Since there is also whey in kosher milk, which does not curdle, there is no** way **to establish** the halakhic **matter with regard to it.** Even kosher milk will not curdle completely, and therefore this is not a reliable method to determine the halakhic status of the milk.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא דְּקָבָעֵי לֵהּ לִגְבִינָה, אִיכָּא דְּקָאֵי בֵּינֵי אִטְפֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara presents an alternative suggestion: **And if you wish, say** instead that **you** may **even say** that the concern applies **where he intends** to use the milk **to** make **cheese,** as **there is** milk **that remains between the crevices** of curdled cheese, and therefore there is a concern that drops of non-kosher milk might be mixed with it.

However, the *gemara* notes that the cheese test only solves the problem if one is using the milk to make cheese. What if one intends to use it for *chamcha* — a dish that uses raw, uncongealed milk? One might still propose that a small amount be taken aside and tested. But the *gemara* responds: *keivan d'v'tahor nami ika nisyubei d'lo kaima*, even with fully kosher milk there will be *nisyuvei* — whey — that does not coagulate into cheese. Non-kosher milk mixed in with kosher could blend with the whey, and one would mistake the adulterated portion for ordinary kosher whey, rendering the test unreliable. Alternatively, even if one is making cheese, *ika d'ka bini atifei* — there will be milk remaining hidden within the holes of the cheese, much like the holes in Swiss cheese, meaning small pockets of non-kosher milk could be concealed within the finished product. The test therefore does not provide a reliable certification.

## Bread Story



### Admiration Question

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַפַּת. אָמַר רַב כָּהֲנָא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: פַּת לֹא הוּתְּרָה בְּבֵית דִּין, מִכְּלָל דְּאִיכָּא מַאן דְּשָׁרֵי?
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And bread** belonging to gentiles is prohibited for consumption. **Rav Kahana says** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says:** Unlike oil, **bread was not permitted by a court.** The Gemara asks: **From the fact** that Rabbi Yoḥanan states that bread was not permitted in court, can it be inferred **that there is** a different opinion **that** claims that a court **did permit** it?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִין, דְּכִי אֲתָא רַב דִּימִי אָמַר: פַּעַם אַחַת יָצָא רַבִּי לַשָּׂדֶה, וְהֵבִיא גּוֹי לְפָנָיו פַּת פּוּרְנִי מַאֲפֵה סְאָה. אָמַר רַבִּי: כַּמָּה נָאָה פַּת זוֹ, מָה רָאוּ חֲכָמִים לְאוֹסְרָהּ? מָה רָאוּ חֲכָמִים?! מִשּׁוּם חַתְנוּת!
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Yes, as when Rav Dimi came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia, he **said: Once Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **went out to the field, and a gentile brought before him a *se’a* of bread baked in a large baker’s oven [*purnei*]. Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **said: How exquisite is this** loaf of **bread! What did the Sages see** that caused them **to prohibit it?** The Gemara asks, incredulously: **What did the Sages see** that caused them to prohibit it? It was prohibited **due to** the concern that Jews might befriend gentiles while breaking bread with them, which could lead to **marriage** with gentiles.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, מָה רָאוּ חֲכָמִים לְאוֹסְרָהּ בַּשָּׂדֶה. כִּסְבוּרִין הָעָם הִתִּיר רַבִּי הַפַּת, וְלָא הִיא, רַבִּי לֹא הִתִּיר אֶת הַפַּת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi was not asking why bread was prohibited in general. **Rather,** he asked: **What did the Sages see** that caused them **to prohibit** bread even **in the field,** where this concern does not apply? The Gemara notes that upon hearing of this incident **the people thought** that **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **permitted the bread** of gentiles. **But** that **is not so; Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **did not** actually **permit** such **bread.** This is why Rabbi Yoḥanan emphasized that the bread of gentiles was never permitted by Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi’s court.

The *mishnah* listed bread among the forbidden items. The *gemara* opens this discussion with an unusual statement. Rabbi Kahana says in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: *pat lo hutra beveit dino* — bread was never permitted by his court, and some versions read *beveiti*, in his house. The very fact that Rabbi Yochanan felt compelled to say this implies that someone held otherwise. The *gemara* confirms: *in* — yes, there is indeed such a view.

When Rav Dimi came from *Eretz Yisrael*, he reported the following story. Once Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi went out into the countryside, and a non-Jew brought before him *pat purnei* — bread baked in a large oven, *ma'afeh se'ah*. Rabbi exclaimed: *kama na'eh pat zu* — how beautiful is this bread! And then: *mah ra'u chachamim le'osrah* — what did the sages see that led them to forbid it? This is a striking statement. Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, the *redactor* of the *Mishnah* and the preeminent rabbinic authority of his generation, is expressing apparent bewilderment at the prohibition.

### Intermarriage Fear

The *gemara* responds with a *kasha*: what do you mean, what did they see? The reason is well established — *mishum chatnut*, because of the concern that eating together leads to social intimacy which leads to *chatnut*, intermarriage. But then the *gemara* reframes Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's question more precisely: *ela mah ra'u chachamim le'osrah basadeh* — what did the sages see to forbid it specifically in the countryside? In a rural setting, the social dynamics that lead to intermarriage are presumably absent. This was the real thrust of Rabbi's question — not challenging the prohibition in cities, but wondering about its application in the countryside.

### Jewish Baker Query

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב יוֹסֵף, וְאִיתֵּימָא רַב שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר יְהוּדָה, אָמַר: לֹא כָּךְ הָיָה מַעֲשֶׂה, אֶלָּא אָמְרוּ: פַּעַם אַחַת הָלַךְ רַבִּי לְמָקוֹם אֶחָד וְרָאָה פַּת דָּחוּק לַתַּלְמִידִים, אָמַר רַבִּי: אֵין כָּאן פַּלְטֵר? כִּסְבוּרִין הָעָם לוֹמַר פַּלְטֵר גּוֹי, וְהוּא לֹא אָמַר אֶלָּא פַּלְטֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל.
> **Translation:** The Gemara records an alternate version of this episode. **Rav Yosef, and some say Rav Shmuel bar Yehuda, says:** The **incident did not occur** in **this** manner. **Rather, they said: Once Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **went to a certain place and saw** that **bread** was **scarce for the students** in the study hall. **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **said: Is there no baker [*palter*] here** who can prepare bread? Upon hearing of this incident, **the people thought to say** that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi was referring to **a gentile baker,** which would indicate that bread baked by a professional baker is permitted, even if he is a gentile. **But** in reality, Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **stated** his question **only** in reference to **a Jewish baker.**

Rav Yosef — and some say Rav Shmuel bar Yehuda — disputes the story entirely. The incident did not happen this way. Rather, this is what actually occurred: Rabbi traveled to a certain place and observed that his students were *dachuk* — in real distress, without access to bread. He said: *ein kan palter* — is there no baker here? The people assumed he meant a non-Jewish baker, thus implying that even such bread would be acceptable under these circumstances. But Rabbi's intent was entirely otherwise.

### No Jewish Option

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי חֶלְבּוֹ: אֲפִילּוּ לְמַאן דְּאָמַר פַּלְטֵר גּוֹי, לָא אֲמַרַן אֶלָּא דְּלֵיכָּא פַּלְטֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל, אֲבָל בִּמְקוֹם דְּאִיכָּא פַּלְטֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל — לָא. וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: אֲפִילּוּ לְמַאן דְּאָמַר פַּלְטֵר גּוֹי, הָנֵי מִילֵּי בַּשָּׂדֶה, אֲבָל בָּעִיר — לָא, מִשּׁוּם חַתְנוּת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites two qualifications of the leniency that people inferred from the above incident. **Rabbi Ḥelbo said: Even according to the one who** thought to **say** that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi was referring to **a gentile baker, we said** that the bread is permitted **only where there is no Jewish baker, but in a place where there is a Jewish baker,** the leniency would certainly **not** apply. **And Rabbi Yoḥanan said: Even according to the one who** thought to **say** that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi was referring to **a gentile baker, that statement** applies only **in the field, but in the city** it would **not** apply, and the bread would still be prohibited **due to** the possibility of **marriage** with a gentile.

Rabbi Chelbo rules: even according to the interpretation that assumed Rabbi was asking about a non-Jewish baker, *lo amran ela delika palter Yisrael* — this leniency applies only where there is no Jewish bakery available. Where a Jewish baker exists, there is no dispensation to eat non-Jewish bread under any reading of the incident.

### City vs Country

Rabbi Yochanan adds a further qualification: even the lenient reading of Rabbi's statement — that non-Jewish bread might be permissible in some circumstances — *hani milei basadeh*, applies only in the countryside. *Aval ba'ir lo* — but in a city, one may not eat non-Jewish bread, *mishum chatnut*, because the concern of social proximity leading to intermarriage is fully operative in an urban setting.

### Avu Example

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַיְבוּ הֲוָה מְנַכֵּית וְאָכֵיל פַּת אַבֵּי מִצְרֵי, אֲמַר לְהוּ רָבָא, וְאִיתֵּימָא רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: לָא תִּשְׁתַּעוּ בַּהֲדֵיהּ דְּאַיְבוּ, דְּקָאָכֵיל לַחְמָא דַּאֲרַמָּאֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara relates: **Aivu would bite and eat bread** of gentiles **at the boundaries** of the fields. **Rava said to** the students in the study hall, **and some say** that it was **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak** who said to them: **Do not speak with Aivu, as he eats bread of Arameans** in deliberate violation of a rabbinic decree.

The *gemara* closes with a pointed anecdote. A certain Ibu would take and eat non-Jewish bread *abei metzrei* — along the borders of the fields, apparently reasoning that this qualified as the permitted countryside context. Rava — and some say Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak — declared to the people: *lo tishte'u minei* — do not associate with Ibu, because *dek'achil lachma arma'ei*, he eats non-Jewish bread. It is a striking conclusion: not merely a theoretical ruling, but a social distancing from one who treats the prohibition lightly.
