[DAF:13a]
## Yom Kippur Atonement



### Opening & Transition

Today's daf is 13, and we are finally turning from the last part of the *mishnah*, which devoted its opening portion to working out the distinct functions of the various *musafim* — the *chatat* offerings and their relationship to *tumat mikdash* — and then a lengthy derivation establishing that *shevuot shtayim she'hein arba* follows the same structural pattern as other cases of "two that are four." The final portion of the *mishnah*, however, shifts to a topic more directly connected to our own lives: not the question of *tumat mikdash* and the *korbanot* that address it, but the broader question of what the goat of *Yom Kippur* atones for.

### Goat of Yom Kippur

The *mishnah* states that the goat sent over the cliff on *Yom Kippur* atones for sins whether they are weighty or light, *mezid* or *shogeg*, or whether the sinner knew or did not know — with the *gemara* clarifying that "knew or did not know" refers to a case of initial *shogeg* where there is now uncertainty as to whether an *asham* is required. One thing the goat does not accomplish is releasing someone from the obligation of a *chatat*: if you sinned *beshogeg* and are certain of it, the *chatat* obligation remains. But with that established, we pick up at the bottom of 12b.

### Lack of Teshuvah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַאי עֲשֵׂה הֵיכִי דָמֵי? אִי דְּלָא עֲבַד תְּשׁוּבָה – ״זֶבַח רְשָׁעִים תּוֹעֵבָה״! אִי דַּעֲבַד תְּשׁוּבָה – כֹּל יוֹמָא נָמֵי, דְּתַנְיָא: עָבַר עַל מִצְוַת עֲשֵׂה וְעָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה, לָא זָז מִשָּׁם עַד שֶׁמּוֹחֲלִין לוֹ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What are the circumstances** of **this positive mitzva** in the mishna? **If** it is a case **where he did not repent,** the offering cannot atone for him, as the verse states: **“The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination”** (Proverbs 21:27). **If he did repent,** then why is the mishna referring to Yom Kippur? He will achieve atonement on **any** other **day as well, as it is taught** in a *baraita*: If **one transgressed a positive mitzva and repented, he does not move from there until he is forgiven.**

The *gemara* asks: what is the case where the goat atones for a positive *mitzvah* one failed to perform? If one did not do *teshuvah* — *idelo avid teshuvah* — why should a sacrifice atone at all? *Zevach reshaim to'evah* — the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination. The *gemara* is framing the question in a striking way: it is not merely asking whether atonement is deserved, but suggesting that if one remains entirely unrepentant toward a particular sin, the very act of bringing a sacrifice in that context borders on the contemptible — a kind of invalid or unwanted offering. It is worth pausing on why this framing feels surprising. We can understand the idea that a sacrifice might atone even for a willful sin, since normally sacrifices are reserved for unintentional transgressions; but there is a meaningful difference between a sin that was initially willful and a sinner who now, at the moment of atonement, is genuinely remorseful and seeking forgiveness. In that case, at least the person is in a posture of contrition, and we can accept that the sacrifice reaches even the original willfulness. But if someone is not now in a state of remorse — if toward this specific sin they remain indifferent, saying in effect "I did it and I don't care" — then not only does it seem wrong that anything should wipe the slate clean, but the citation of *zevach reshaim to'evah* goes further still: it frames the offering itself as something we should not even want, as though this person's unrepentant relationship to the sin renders the sacrifice unwanted rather than merely ineffective. On the other hand, if one *has* done *teshuvah* — *id'avid teshuvah* — then *kol yoma nami*: any day on which *teshuvah* is done for a *mitzvat aseh* suffices, and *Yom Kippur* with the goat seems superfluous.

### Teshuvah for Mitzvat Aseh

A *baraita* is cited: *avar al mitzvat aseh ve'asah teshuvah*, if one transgressed a positive *mitzvah* and did *teshuvah*, *lo zaz mi-sham ad she-mochalin lo* — one does not move from there before God forgives. The imagery evokes something like *Choni HaMa'agal* standing his ground: immediate forgiveness is available upon *teshuvah* for a *mitzvat aseh*. So who then needs the goat, and who needs *Yom Kippur*?

### Omed BeMiddo

Rabbi Zeira answers: this case concerns someone who is *omed be-meredo* — standing in a state of rebellion. The point is subtle and important. The failure to do *teshuvah* is not a passive oversight; it is an active ongoing choice to remain unrepentant, an implicit declaration of indifference. Nevertheless, *Yom Kippur* forgives even this.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי זֵירָא:
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira said:**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּעוֹמֵד בְּמִרְדּוֹ, וְרַבִּי הִיא. דְּתַנְיָא: רַבִּי אוֹמֵר, עַל כׇּל עֲבֵירוֹת שֶׁבַּתּוֹרָה, בֵּין עָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה בֵּין לֹא עָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה – יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים מְכַפֵּר; חוּץ מִפּוֹרֵק עוֹל וּמְגַלֶּה פָּנִים בַּתּוֹרָה וּמֵפֵר בְּרִית בְּבָשָׂר – שֶׁאִם עָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה, יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים מְכַפֵּר; וְאִם לָאו, אֵין יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים מְכַפֵּר.
> **Translation:** The mishna is referring to a case **where** the person did not repent and **persists in his rebellion, and it is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, that even for such a case Yom Kippur and the scapegoat will atone. **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **says: For all transgressions that are** stated **in the Torah, whether one repented,** or **whether one did not repent, Yom Kippur atones, except for one who divests** himself of the **yoke** of Heaven, by denying God’s existence, **and one who reveals facets of the Torah** that differ from its true meaning, **and one who nullifies** the **covenant of** circumcision of **the flesh. For** these, **if one repented, Yom Kippur atones, and if not, Yom Kippur does not atone.**

### Ramban on Teshuvah


This connects to a remarkable observation of the Ramban in his *drasha le-Rosh Hashanah* — a work that is essentially a fifty-page commentary on *Masechet Rosh Hashanah*, with only its final paragraph devoted to *mussar*. There the Ramban writes that the opportunity for *teshuvah* God has granted us is simultaneously a great gift and a great liability. It is a gift because *teshuvah* earns forgiveness. But it is a liability because: had God never given us *teshuvah*, a person who sinned would have sinned only once. Now that *teshuvah* exists, choosing not to do *teshuvah* is itself an ongoing sin — every passing moment becomes a renewed act of *omed be-meredo*, a continued embrace of one's transgression. This is precisely what Rabbi Zeira captures: not doing *teshuvah* is not simply the absence of an action; it is an active posture of rebellion. And yet, *Yom Kippur* still atones.

## Rabbi's Position



### All Sins Atoned

The *mishnah*, the *gemara* tells us, follows the position of *Rabbi* — that is, Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi. A *baraita* states his teaching explicitly: *al kol averos she-ba-Torah bein asah teshuvah bein lo asah teshuvah Yom HaKippurim mechaper* — for all the transgressions in the Torah, whether one has done *teshuvah* or has not done *teshuvah*, *Yom Kippur* atones. The vision is of a kind of divine amnesty: God desires that each year, every person begin with a clean slate. Even a willful *mechalel Shabbat* with no remorse whatsoever — come *Yom Kippur*, the slate is wiped clean.

It is worth noting a subtle but critical shift that occurs here and continues throughout the day's discussion: the *gemara* has quietly transitioned from speaking about the goat going over the cliff as the instrument of atonement to speaking of *Yom Kippur* itself as *mechaper*. This shift is obviously momentous for us, who no longer have the goat. The *gemara* allows this transition to happen organically without drawing explicit attention to it.

### Three Exceptions

There is, however, an exception — in fact, three. *Chutz* — excepted from the blanket atonement of *Yom Kippur* are: *porek ol*, *megaleh panim ba-Torah*, and *mefeir brit basar*.

### Porek Ol Defined

*Porek ol* means casting off the yoke — an outright rejection of God and His *mitzvot*. This is not mere willful sinning driven by desire or appetite; it is an ideological rejection of the entire covenantal relationship.

### Megaleh Panim

*Megaleh panim ba-Torah* is frequently misread, particularly when a gloss of *she-lo ka-halacha* is inserted, suggesting it refers to someone who interprets a *halacha* incorrectly. That misreading obscures what is actually a far more serious category. The phrase *gilui panim* means acting brazenly, with insolence. Applied to *Torah*, it describes someone whose relationship to the words of the *Torah* is one of dismissive scoffing — not sincere philosophical questioning, but a contemptuous "ugh, why would God have said such a thing?" — a posture of mockery and denigration toward the *Torah* itself.

### Rashi on Megaleh Panim

Rashi makes this vivid. Three lines from the top of the relevant passage he writes: *ba al divrei Torah be-chutzpah u-vegillui azut panim, ke-gon Menashe she-haya yoshev ve-doresh be-agadot shel dofi* — like Menashe who would sit and expound stories of *Torah* in a manner of derision: Why did Moshe need to write the story of Timna who was a concubine? What is the episode of Reuven going out in the days of the wheat harvest doing in the *Torah*? Not as a genuine question, but as contemptuous dismissal. Rashi thus reads *megaleh panim* as describing a specific relationship to the *Torah* — one of brazen scoffing — rather than to God or *mitzvot* directly. The Rambam in his *Peirush HaMishnayot*, by contrast, reads it as referring to someone who sins *le-hachis*, in active defiance and provocation.

### Mefir Brit

*Mefeir brit basar* — annulling the covenant of the flesh — refers to reversing *brit milah*. The word *basar* makes clear this is not merely metaphorical. It is the cancellation of the physical covenant. Whether this extends to someone who grows up in a household that never performed the *brit* and makes a conscious ideological choice to remain uncircumcised is an interesting question; the language suggests active rejection rather than mere passivity.

For these three, *teshuvah* does work — *she-im asah teshuvah Yom HaKippurim mechaper* — if *teshuvah* is done, *Yom Kippur* then atones. But without *teshuvah*, *Yom Kippur* does not atone for these three. By contrast, one might wonder whether the classic three cardinal sins — *gilui arayot*, *shefichut damim*, and *avodah zarah* — fall within or outside this framework. According to Rabbi, as long as the person has not crossed into ideological rejection of God, Torah, or the covenant — as long as they remain within the *berit* even while sinning grievously — *Yom Kippur* atones even for those sins without *teshuvah*. What is more, the scope of that atonement is striking: even a sin that was entirely willful, even one the person has no remorse for whatsoever, is covered. One could have been *mechallel Shabbat* deliberately and remained perfectly content about it, and *Yom Kippur* still wipes the slate clean. *Beit din* is another matter — *Yom Kippur* does not extract a person from the hands of *beit din* where witnesses exist — but to the extent the person has escaped legal consequence, *Yom Kippur* functions as a kind of amnesty, unconditional and unrequested. That is a remarkable thing to say after an entire tractate devoted to cataloguing the punishments that attend willful sin.

One should also be clear about what atonement without *teshuvah* means and does not mean. As Rav Soloveitchik once articulated, there is a distinction between *kapparah* — the erasure of culpability, the cleaning of the slate — and the personal transformation that genuine *teshuvah me-ahavah* accomplishes. A person whose slate is wiped clean by *Yom Kippur* without *teshuvah* is still, *be-gavra*, a *rasha* — they have simply escaped punishment for their past sins. *Teshuvah* in its deepest sense transforms who a person is, converting the *rasha* into a *tzaddik*. The mechanisms are not equivalent.

### Hikareit Tikareit

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי? דְּתַנְיָא: ״כִּי דְבַר ה׳ בָּזָה״ – זֶה הַפּוֹרֵק עוֹל וּמְגַלֶּה פָּנִים בַּתּוֹרָה. ״וְאֶת מִצְוָתוֹ הֵפֵר״ – זֶה הַמֵּפֵר בְּרִית בַּבָּשָׂר. ״הִכָּרֵת תִּכָּרֵת״ – הִכָּרֵת לִפְנֵי יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים, תִּכָּרֵת לְאַחַר יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is the reasoning of Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi? It is **as it is taught** in a *baraita* in interpretation of the verse: “For he scorned the word of the Lord and nullified His commandment; that person will be cut off [*hikkaret tikkaret*], his sin is upon him” (Numbers 15:31): **“For he scorned the word of the Lord”; this** is referring to **one who divests** himself **of** the **yoke** of Heaven **and one who reveals facets of the Torah** that differ from its true meaning. **“And nullified His commandment”; this** is referring to **one who nullified the covenant of** circumcision of **the flesh.** The use of the double verb form ***hikkaret tikkaret*** teaches that **he will be cut off,** i.e., he is liable to receive *karet*, **before Yom Kippur, and he will** still **be cut off after Yom Kippur,** as Yom Kippur does not atone for him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יָכוֹל אֲפִילּוּ עָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״עֲוֹנָהּ בָּהּ״ – לֹא אָמַרְתִּי אֶלָּא בִּזְמַן שֶׁעֲוֹנָהּ בָּהּ.
> **Translation:** One **might** have thought that this applies **even** if **he repented.** To counter this, **the verse states: “His sin is upon him,”** by which God indicates: **I said** that Yom Kippur does not atone for these sins **only when his sin is** still **upon him,** as he did not repent. It is apparent from this *baraita* that it is only for the three sins mentioned that Yom Kippur does not atone without repentance, but Yom Kippur atones for other sins even if one did not repent.

How does Rabbi derive that these three are not covered by the general atonement of *Yom Kippur*? A *baraita* cites: *ki devar Hashem baza* — because he has denigrated the word of God. Rashi explains that *devar Hashem* in its primary sense refers to *Anochi Hashem Elokecha* — the first word of the *aseret ha-dibrot* — so that *ki devar Hashem baza* describes the person who denigrates God's word entirely: *ze ha-porek ol u-megaleh panim ba-Torah*. And *ve-et mitzvato hefar* — he has annulled His commandment: *ze ha-mefeir brit basar*. What *mitzvah* symbolizes the covenant more than any other? The *brit* of the flesh.

Then the verse continues: *hikaret tikaret* — he shall surely be cut off. The doubling is exegetically significant. *Hikaret* — before *Yom Kippur*. *Tikaret* — after *Yom Kippur*. The verse is teaching that even after *Yom Kippur* passes, these three are not atoned for. This is Rabbi's proof that *Yom Kippur* does not atone for these three — not that he needs a proof that *Yom Kippur* atones for everything else, which seems to be his starting assumption. That is worth pausing on. Rabbi's starting point appears to be that *Yom Kippur* atones for everything, including willful sins for which no *teshuvah* was done. From that baseline, the verse carves out three exceptions. Rabbi is not building up from nothing; he is reading the doubling of *hikaret tikaret* as a limiting clause within what would otherwise be a comprehensive atonement.

One might also ask where *avodah zarah* falls in this framework — whether idol worship constitutes *porek ol* and therefore belongs to these three. The answer depends on which form of idolatry one has in mind. There is an idolatry of worshipping God through images, which, however severe a sin and however firmly it falls under the rule of *yehareg ve'al ya'avor*, does not constitute a rejection of God Himself — it corrupts the worship rather than denying the One being worshipped, and so it would presumably not be *porek ol*. Then there is the idolatry of genuinely believing in and turning to other gods in outright rejection of the God of Israel — that would clearly be *porek ol*. The more interesting case is the syncretist, the person who believes in the Jewish God alongside other deities without rejecting God entirely but without affirming His uniqueness either. Whether that constitutes a form of rejection sufficient to qualify as *porek ol* is genuinely unresolved, and the spectrum of possible forms of *avodah zarah* — from image worship to outright rejection to a kind of theological pluralism — maps onto the question of when exactly the threshold of *porek ol* is crossed.

### Avona Bah

But the verse then adds: *avona vah* — the sin is in her, or more precisely, the sin persists with the person. *Lo amarti ela bi-zman she-avona vah* — this is only said when the sin continues to persist, when the person is still grasping it. Once *teshuvah* is done — once the person is no longer holding fast to the sin — even these three receive atonement. Implicitly, this also establishes Rabbi's view for everything else: since the verse specifies that these three are not atoned for specifically due to *avona vah* — because the sin persists — we can derive that for everything else, *Yom Kippur* atones even without *teshuvah*.

## Rabbanan's Position



### Olam HaBa Reading

The *rabanan* who disagree with Rabbi — who hold that *Yom Kippur* does *not* atone without *teshuvah* — read the verse differently. *Hikaret* — cut off in *olam hazeh*. *Tikaret* — cut off *le-olam haba*. On this reading, the doubling of *hikaret tikaret* says nothing about *Yom Kippur* at all; it is describing the punishment of *karet* in its two dimensions across this world and the next. The reference to *olam haba* then implies that we are discussing what happens after death.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן – ״הִכָּרֵת״ בָּעוֹלָם הַזֶּה, ״תִּכָּרֵת״ לָעוֹלָם הַבָּא, ״עֲוֹנָהּ בָּהּ״ – שֶׁאִם עָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה וָמֵת, מִיתָה מְמָרֶקֶת.
> **Translation:** **And** with regard to **the Rabbis** who disagree with Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, how do they interpret the verse? If someone commits one of the three sins mentioned, then **he is cut off [*hikkaret*]** from life **in this world,** and **he will be cut off [*tikkaret*] in the World-to-Come.** The phrase **“His sin is upon him”** teaches **that if he repented and died,** his **death cleanses** him of his sin.

### Death Atones


For the *rabanan*, therefore, the verse is about *misa* — death. Death, *misa memarkeset*, cleanses. The *rabanan* hold that death atones even for those who have not done *teshuvah*, at least for all sins other than the three cardinal ones. Rashi adds that this works in conjunction with *Yom Kippur*. The term *memarkeset* is telling: it suggests something more like scouring than the gentle *mechaperes* — the erasure is complete but the process is more severe, perhaps less total in its redemptive quality.

For the big three, the *rabanan* hold that even death atones only if *teshuvah* was done. And as for *Yom Kippur*: in the view of the *rabanan*, *Yom Kippur* always requires *teshuvah*.

### Atonement Chart

To map the positions clearly: Rabbi holds that *Yom Kippur* atones for everything without *teshuvah*, with the exception that for the three — *porek ol*, *megaleh panim*, and *mefeir brit* — one needs *Yom Kippur* plus *teshuvah*. The *rabanan* hold that *Yom Kippur* always requires *teshuvah*, and that death (*misa*) atones without *teshuvah* for everything other than the three, while the three require *teshuvah* even for death to atone. This raises a pointed question for the *rabanan*: if one has already done *teshuvah*, why does *Yom Kippur* still need to be involved at all — what does it add once *teshuvah* is in place? That question, it turns out, applies only to the case of a *mitzvat aseh*, as we will see next.

### Mitzvat Aseh Agreement

One important point of agreement: for a *mitzvat aseh*, both Rabbi and the *rabanan* agree that *teshuvah* alone suffices — no *Yom Kippur* and no other mechanism is required. Forgiveness is immediate upon genuine *teshuvah*.

## Mishnah Attribution



### Challenge to Mishnah

The *gemara* now asks: if our *mishnah* follows Rabbi — who holds that *Yom Kippur* atones even without *teshuvah* — how does that square with the apparent evidence from the end of the *mishnah*?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִי מָצֵית מוֹקְמַתְּ לַהּ כְּרַבִּי?! וְהָא מִדְּסֵיפָא רַבִּי יְהוּדָה הִיא, רֵישָׁא נָמֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה הִיא! דְּקָתָנֵי סֵיפָא: אֶחָד יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאֶחָד כֹּהֲנִים וְאֶחָד כֹּהֵן מָשׁוּחַ; וּמַאן אִית לֵיהּ הַאי סְבָרָא – רַבִּי יְהוּדָה; מִכְּלָל דְּרֵישָׁא רַבִּי יְהוּדָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And can you interpret** the mishna to be **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi? **But from** the fact **that the latter clause is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda,** by inference, **the first clause is also** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda,** but not of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, **as the latter clause** of the mishna **teaches: Israelites and priests and** the **anointed priest,** i.e., the High Priest, all equally achieve atonement from the scapegoat. **And who accepts this reasoning? Rabbi Yehuda,** as the Gemara will demonstrate. **By inference, the first clause** is also in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda,** not of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi.

### End of Mishnah


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: רַבִּי הִיא, וְסָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yosef said:** It is possible that the entire mishna **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **and** the latter clause does not pose a difficulty, because with regard to whether priests achieve atonement through the scapegoat, **he holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda.**

The *seifa* of the *mishnah* states: *echad Yisrael ve-echad Kohanim ve-echad Kohen Mashuach* — all three categories, the ordinary Israelite, the *Kohen*, and the *Kohen Gadol*, receive equal atonement. This reflects the position that when it comes to *she'ar aveiros* — the sins other than *tumat mikdash* — the goat that goes over the cliff provides atonement equally for all. Whereas for *tumat mikdash*, Kohanim have a distinct atonement through the ox and Israelites through the goat, for all other sins they share one vehicle. Now, this position — that they share equally in the goat for *she'ar aveiros* — is identified as belonging to Rabbi Yehudah. If the *seifa* is Rabbi Yehudah, the *gemara* argues, so must be the *reisha*, and the *mishnah* should therefore not be attributed to Rabbi.

### Rav Yosef's Solution

Rav Yosef responds: the *mishnah* is indeed Rabbi — *Rabbi hi* — and Rabbi simply adopts Rabbi Yehudah's position regarding the equality of Kohen and Yisrael for the goat. *Ve-savar lah ke-Rabbi Yehudah*. In other words, Rabbi, as author of the *mishnah*, held like Rabbi Yehudah on this particular *halacha* while maintaining his own distinctive position that *Yom Kippur* atones without *teshuvah*.

### Abaye's Challenge

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי: דַּוְקָא קָאָמַר מָר – רַבִּי סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, אֲבָל רַבִּי יְהוּדָה לָא סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי? אוֹ דִלְמָא, מִדְּרַבִּי סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, אַף רַבִּי יְהוּדָה סָבַר לַהּ נָמֵי כְּרַבִּי; מִיהוּ אוֹרְחָא דְמִילְּתָא קָתָנֵי – לְמֵימַר דְּתַלְמִיד סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבֵּיהּ?
> **Translation:** **Abaye said to him:** Does **the Master** mean **specifically** what **he is saying,** i.e., that **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda** with regard to whether the scapegoat atones for both Israelites and priest, **but Rabbi Yehuda does not hold in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi with regard to atonement for one who did not repent? **Or perhaps from** the fact **that Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda,** it follows that **Rabbi Yehuda also holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **but** the reason that Rav Yosef did not make this clear is that **he teaches the matter in the manner in which it** typically occurs, which is **to say that a disciple,** in this case, Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, **holds in accordance with** the opinion of **his teacher,** i.e., Rabbi Yehuda.

Abaye presses further: was Rav Yosef being precise in saying that *Rabbi* holds like Rabbi Yehudah — as opposed to saying the *mishnah* is Rabbi Yehudah who holds like Rabbi? After all, either formulation could account for the evidence.

### Mutual Agreement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אִין, דַּוְקָא קָאָמֵינָא – רַבִּי סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, אֲבָל רַבִּי יְהוּדָה לָא סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי.
> **Translation:** Rav Yosef **said to him: Yes,** I mean **specifically** what **I was saying: Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda, but Rabbi Yehuda does not hold in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi.

Rav Yosef responds: *davka ka-amina* — yes, I was being precise. Rabbi holds like Rabbi Yehudah, but Rabbi Yehudah does not hold like Rabbi regarding *Yom Kippur* atoning without *teshuvah*. Why phrase it with Rabbi as the one borrowing? *Orcha de-milsa* — it is simply more natural to say that the later authority, Rabbi (who postdates Rabbi Yehudah), embraced an earlier position, rather than the reverse. But the precision is real: Rabbi Yehudah actively disagreed with Rabbi's core position on *teshuvah* and *Yom Kippur*.

## Baraita Evidence



### Sifra Source

The evidence comes from a *baraita* drawn from the *Sifra* — the collection of tannaitic legal exegesis on *Vayikra*, whose anonymous voice is by tradition attributed to Rabbi Yehudah. The *baraita* opens: *yachol yehei Yom HaKippurim mechaper al shavim ve-al she-einam shavim* — perhaps *Yom Kippur* should atone both for those who have done *teshuvah* and for those who have not?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא: יָכוֹל יְהֵא יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים מְכַפֵּר עַל שָׁבִים וְעַל שֶׁאֵינָן שָׁבִים? וְדִין הוּא – הוֹאִיל וְחַטָּאת וְאָשָׁם מְכַפְּרִין, וְיוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים מְכַפֵּר; מָה חַטָּאת וְאָשָׁם – אֵין מְכַפְּרִין אֶלָּא עַל הַשָּׁבִים, אַף יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים – אֵין מְכַפֵּר אֶלָּא עַל הַשָּׁבִים! מָה לְחַטָּאת וְאָשָׁם – שֶׁאֵין מְכַפְּרִין עַל הַמֵּזִיד כַּשּׁוֹגֵג; תֹּאמַר לְיוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים – שֶׁמְּכַפֵּר עַל הַמֵּזִיד כַּשּׁוֹגֵג?!
> **Translation:** **As it is taught** in a *baraita* recorded in the *Sifra*: One **might** have thought that **Yom Kippur would atone for those who repent and for those who do not repent, and** this assertion **is** supported by the following **logical inference:** Although it would appear that **since a sin-offering and a guilt-offering atone and Yom Kippur atones,** it should follow that **just as a sin-offering and a guilt-offering atone only for those who repent, so too, Yom Kippur atones only for those who repent,** this comparison is flawed. One can claim: **What** is notable **about a sin-offering and a guilt-offering?** They are notable in **that they do not atone for intentional** sins **like** they do for **unwitting** sins. **Can you say** the same **about Yom Kippur, which does atone for intentional** sins **as** it does for **unwitting** sins?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הוֹאִיל וּמְכַפֵּר עַל הַמֵּזִיד כַּשּׁוֹגֵג, יְכַפֵּר עַל שָׁבִים וְעַל שֶׁאֵינָן שָׁבִים! תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״אַךְ״ – חָלַק.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: **Since** it is the case that the atonement of Yom Kippur is more far-reaching in that **it atones for intentional** sins **as** it does for **unwitting** sins, it follows that **it should atone** both **for those who repent and for those who do not repent.** To counter this, **the verse states: “Yet** on the tenth day of this seventh month it is Yom Kippur” (Leviticus 23:27). The word “yet” serves to **divide** and limit the atonement of Yom Kippur in that it atones only for those who repent.

### Logical Derivation


A logical analogy is offered: *chatas* and *asham* atone, and *Yom Kippur* atones; just as *chatas* and *asham* only atone for those who have done *teshuvah* — since bringing a sin-offering includes the act of *viduy*, verbal confession — so too *Yom Kippur* should atone only for those who have done *teshuvah*. *Af Yom HaKippurim ein mechaper ella al shavim*. The analogy has a particular internal logic worth pausing on: a sin-offering requires not only that the person have done *teshuvah* at the time of bringing it, but also that the original sin was committed *bishogeg* — unintentionally. There are, in other words, two distinct moments that bear on whether atonement is available: the character of the act when it was committed, and the state of the person now seeking forgiveness. A *chatas* or *asham* operates only when both conditions are favorable — the sin was unintentional at the outset, and the person has repented by the end. These are, precisely, the sins most deserving of forgiveness. Because *chatas* and *asham* are calibrated exclusively to that most forgivable category of sin, their atoning power is inherently limited — and it is that limited scope which makes the additional requirement of *teshuvah* unsurprising. Whether the same reasoning extends to *Yom Kippur*, which operates on a broader range of sins, is the question the analogy will now have to confront.

### Counter-argument

But this analogy is challenged: *mah le-chatas ve-asham she-ein mechaperim al ha-mezid ke-shogeg* — one could distinguish *chatas* and *asham* by saying their atoning power is inherently limited, applying only to unintentional sins. Their restricted scope explains why *teshuvah* is also required: the most forgivable sins (unintentional at the outset, repented at the end) earn the atonement of a *chatat*. *Tomar le-Yom HaKippurim she-mechaper al ha-mezid ke-shogeg* — but *Yom Kippur* is explicitly described in the *pasuk* as atoning even for willful sins, *u-mipisheihem le-chol chatosam*. Since *Yom Kippur* possesses this expanded atoning power, perhaps it functions as a complete and unconditional mechanism, working even without *teshuvah*.

### Ach Limitation

The *baraita* responds with a verse from Vayikra 23: *ach be-yom hazeh yechapper*. The word *ach* introduces a limitation. *Ach* — only, but. *Yom Kippur* does not have total scope. And therefore: it atones only for those who have done *teshuvah*. Even if the sin was *mezid*, *Yom Kippur* still requires *teshuvah* to function. The combination is: yes, it can cover even willful sins, but only when accompanied by *teshuvah*.

### Sifra Attribution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סְתָם סִיפְרָא מַנִּי – רַבִּי יְהוּדָה; וְקָאָמַר: שָׁבִים אִין, לֹא שָׁבִים לָא.
> **Translation:** Rav Yosef attributes the *baraita* to Rabbi Yehuda: **Whose** opinion is expressed by the **unattributed** *baraitot* in the ***Sifra*? Rabbi Yehuda. And he says:** For **those who repent, yes,** Yom Kippur atones, but for **those who do not repent,** Yom Kippur does **not** atone.

This entire *derasha* comes from the *Sifra*. *Stam Sifra mani? Rabbi Yehudah* — the anonymous voice of the *Sifra* is Rabbi Yehudah. The *baraita*'s conclusion that *Yom Kippur* works only *al shavim* — only for those who have returned — is therefore explicitly Rabbi Yehudah's position. This confirms Rav Yosef's claim: the part of our *mishnah* that implies *Yom Kippur* atones even without *teshuvah* cannot be Rabbi Yehudah; it must be Rabbi.

## Sifra Contradiction



### Violating Yom Kippur

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרָמֵי סְתָם סִיפְרָא אַסְּתַם סִיפְרָא – דְּתַנְיָא: יָכוֹל לֹא יְהֵא יוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים מְכַפֵּר, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן הִתְעַנָּה בּוֹ, וּקְרָאוֹ מִקְרָא קֹדֶשׁ, וְלֹא עָשָׂה בּוֹ מְלָאכָה; לֹא הִתְעַנָּה בּוֹ, וְלֹא קְרָאוֹ מִקְרָא קֹדֶשׁ, וְעָשָׂה בּוֹ מְלָאכָה – מִנַּיִן? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״יוֹם כִּפֻּרִים הוּא״ מִכׇּל מָקוֹם!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But raise a contradiction,** setting one **unattributed** *baraita* in the ***Sifra*,** i.e., the one just cited, **against** another **unattributed** *baraita* in the ***Sifra*, as** in another *baraita* there **it is taught:** One **might** have thought that **Yom Kippur would atone only if one fasted on it and declared it a holy convocation and did not perform labor on it. From where** is it derived that even if **one did not fast on it and did not declare it a holy convocation and performed labor on it,** that it still atones? **The verse states:** “Yet on the tenth day of this seventh month **it is Yom Kippur”** (Leviticus 23:27). The additional emphasis on “it is” serves to teach that the day atones **in any case.** This *baraita* contradicts the one cited above that states clearly that Yom Kippur atones only for those who repent.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: לָא קַשְׁיָא; הָא רַבִּי, וְהָא רַבִּי יְהוּדָה.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said:** This is **not difficult: This** second *baraita* is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **and that** first *baraita* is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רָבָא אָמַר: הָא וְהָא רַבִּי, וּמוֹדֶה רַבִּי בְּכָרֵת דְּיוֹמָא. דְּאִי לָא תֵּימָא הָכִי, כָּרֵת דְּיוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים לְרַבִּי לֵית לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rava said:** Both **this** *baraita* **and that** *baraita* are in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, who holds that Yom Kippur atones for those who do not repent, **but** even **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **concedes** that **with regard to** avoiding the punishment of ***karet*** incurred **for** violating **the day** of Yom Kippur, Yom Kippur atones and one is not liable to be punished with *karet* only if one repented for the sin of violating the day of Yom Kippur. Perforce he must concede this point, **as if you do not say so,** then **according to Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **there would be no** instance in which one would be liable to be punished with ***karet* for** violating **Yom Kippur.**

The *gemara* now raises a striking contradiction between two anonymous passages of the *Sifra* — *rami stam Sifra a-stam Sifra*. The first *Sifra* we just cited says *Yom Kippur* works only for those who have done *teshuvah*. But a second *baraita* says the opposite, and in a dramatically expanded direction: *yachol lo yehei Yom HaKippurim mechaper ela im kein hisana bo ve-kara mikra kodesh ve-lo asah bo melachah* — one might think *Yom Kippur* atones only for one who fasted on it, acknowledged its sacred character, and refrained from *melachah*.

### Non-Observant Scenario

The *gemara* then entertains a scenario that borders on the radical: what about someone who did none of those things? Who did not fast, did not acknowledge the day, and actively performed *melachah*?

### Day of Atonement

*Talmud lomar: Yom Kippurim hu* — the verse simply states, it is *Yom Kippur*. *Mikol makom* — regardless. The day carries its atoning power independent of the person's acknowledgment or observance. This is a far more expansive claim than anything in the first *Sifra* passage, and it seems to posit that *Yom Kippur* atones even for someone actively violating it.

### Abaye's Resolution

Abaye resolves the contradiction by attribution: *ha Rabbi ve-ha Rabbi Yehudah*. The first *Sifra* — requiring *teshuvah* — is Rabbi Yehudah's position. The second *Sifra* — atoning even for the violator — is Rabbi's position.

### Rava's Position

Rava disagrees: *ha ve-ha Rabbi* — both passages represent Rabbi's position. The tension between them will be resolved not by attributing them to different authorities but by reading them in a more nuanced way.

## Rava's Interpretation



### Teshuvah for Violation

Rava's resolution is elegant. Both *Sifra* passages represent Rabbi. The earlier statement — that *Yom Kippur* requires *teshuvah* — is not a general statement about all sins. It refers specifically to the violation of *Yom Kippur* itself. *Ve-modeh Rabbi be-kareit di-yoma* — Rabbi concedes that *Yom Kippur* does not atone for its own violation without *teshuvah*.

### Other Sins Atoned

And yet — and this is the crucial move — even someone who violates *Yom Kippur* still benefits from *Yom Kippur*'s atonement for all his other sins. Violating *Yom Kippur* does not strip the day of its atoning power vis-à-vis everything else. The person eating on *Yom Kippur* will emerge, if he does not repent for the eating itself, with that particular sin unatoned — but his *mechalel Shabbat* and whatever else he has done throughout the year will be atoned for by the day.

### Karet Problem

This creates an internal pressure: if Rabbi holds that *Yom Kippur* atones for everything including its own violation, then why does the *Torah* prescribe *karet* for eating on *Yom Kippur*? If I am eating and *Yom Kippur* is atoning for me with every bite, what is left to punish? *De-i lo tima hachi kareit di-yoma le-Rabbi leit leh* — if you do not make this concession, Rabbi would have no scenario in which someone who eats on *Yom Kippur* receives *karet*, which the *Torah* explicitly prescribes.

### Karet Scenarios

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַלְּמָה לָא? מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ – כְּגוֹן דַּעֲבַד בְּלֵילְיָא וּמִית, דְּלָא אֲתָא יְמָמָא לְכַפּוֹרֵי לֵיהּ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara questions this proof: **Why not? You** could **find it** in a case **where he performed** labor **on the night** of Yom Kippur **and died** that night, **as** in such a case, **the daytime** of Yom Kippur, which is the part of Yom Kippur that effects atonement, **never came to atone for him.**

The *gemara* briefly entertains the possibility of constructing a scenario where *Yom Kippur* atones for its own violation and *karet* still applies in some edge case: perhaps someone ate at night and died before the day of *Yom Kippur* arrived (*ke-gon de-avid be-layla u-mis ve-lo havi yemama le-khapporei leh*), since the *pasuk* says *ki ba-yom hazeh yechapper* — it is the *day* that atones. Or perhaps someone ate and choked as they were swallowing (*de-achal umtza ve-chankei u-mis*), dying before the *aveira* was complete enough for atonement to apply. Or someone ate right up until *shekiat ha-chama* with no remaining time for atonement to take effect.

[DAF:13b]
### Rabbi's Agreement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אֵימָא:
> **Translation:** **Rather, say** that Rava’s proof is as follows:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כָּרֵת דִּימָמָא לְרַבִּי לֵית לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** It must be that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi concedes that Yom Kippur does not atone for one who violates the day itself and does not repent, as if you do not say so, **according to Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **there would be no** instance in which a person would be liable to receive ***karet* for** violating Yom Kippur in **the daytime.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַלְּמָה לָא? מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ דַּאֲכַל אוּמְצָא וְחַנְקֵיהּ וּמִית; אִי נָמֵי דַּאֲכַל סָמוּךְ לִשְׁקִיעַת הַחַמָּה – דְּלָא הֲוָה שְׁהוּת לְכַפּוֹרֵי לֵיהּ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara questions this proof: **Why not? You** could **find it** in a case **where he ate a piece of meat, and** while he was eating **it choked him and he died, or** in a case **where he ate immediately before sunset** at the end of the day. Even if one holds that Yom Kippur does atone for violations of the day itself, in these cases it could not, **as there was no time** after the violation for the day **to atone for him,** in the first case because he was already dead, and in the second case because it was no longer Yom Kippur.


Ultimately, Rava establishes: Rabbi agrees that *Yom Kippur* does not atone for its own violation without *teshuvah*. The two *Sifra* passages are both Rabbi's, and they are not contradictory: the first addresses the one thing *Yom Kippur* cannot atone for without *teshuvah* (its own violation), and the second addresses everything else (which *Yom Kippur* atones for regardless, even if one is violating the day). To the earlier framework of Rabbi's three exceptions — *porek ol*, *megaleh panim*, *mefeir brit* — we now add a fourth category requiring *teshuvah* according to Rabbi: the violation of *Yom Kippur* itself.

## Rambam's Ruling



### Concern for Incentive

One might reasonably worry about the practical implications of Rabbi's position. If people learn that *Yom Kippur* wipes the slate clean without *teshuvah*, the incentive to actually engage in *teshuvah* would be severely weakened.

### Teshuvah Essential

The Rambam rules definitively in *Hilchos Teshuvah*: *kol mitzvot she-ba-Torah bein asei bein lo ta'asei, im avar adam al achat mehem bein be-zadon bein bi-shigaga, she-ya'aseh teshuvah ve-yashuv me-cheto chayav le-hitvadot* — for every commandment in the *Torah*, positive or negative, intentional or unintentional, the obligation is to do *teshuvah* and *viduy*. And further: all the various mechanisms of atonement — *mitat beit din*, *korban*, and the rest — are insufficient without *teshuvah*.

### Se'ir HaMishtaleach

Concerning the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach*, the Rambam states: *she-hu kapparah kol Yisrael* — it is an atonement for all of Israel, atoning for all transgressions in the *Torah*, light and grave, *mezid* and *shogeg*, our *mishnah* — *ve-hu she-asah teshuvah*. Even the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* requires *teshuvah*. The Rambam is applying the *rabanan*'s framework — or Rabbi Yehudah's — rather than Rabbi's more expansive position, plainly because he does not want *Yom Kippur* to become a reason to defer *teshuvah*.

### Without Temple

Finally: *bi-zman ha-zeh she-ein Beit ha-Mikdash kayam, ein lanu mizbe'ach kapparah, ein sham ela teshuvah. Ha-teshuvah mechaperet al kol ha-aveiros* — in our time, without the *Beit ha-Mikdash*, we have no altar of atonement, and there is nothing but *teshuvah*. *Teshuvah* alone atones for everything.

## Kohen Equality



### Mishnah Statement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶחָד יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאֶחָד כֹּהֲנִים וְאֶחָד כֹּהֵן מָשׁוּחַ.
> **Translation:** § The mishna (2b) states: **Israelites and priests and** the **anointed priest,** i.e., the High Priest, equally achieve atonement. What is the difference between Israelites, priests, and the anointed priest? The difference is only that the priests achieve atonement for their defiling of the Temple or its sacrificial foods through the bull that the High Priest offers on Yom Kippur, whereas the Israelites achieve atonement for their transgressions through the goats that are sacrificed on Yom Kippur.

The *mishnah* states: *echad Yisrael ve-echad Kohanim ve-echad Kohen Mashuach* — the Israelite, the *Kohen*, and the *Kohen Gadol* are all equal. The question is what precisely they are equal in, because the *mishnah*'s formulation seems to say they are all the same and then immediately asks what distinguishes them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא גּוּפַאּ קַשְׁיָא – קָתָנֵי: אֶחָד יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאֶחָד כֹּהֲנִים וְאֶחָד כֹּהֵן מָשׁוּחַ; וַהֲדַר תָּנֵי: מָה בֵּין יִשְׂרָאֵל לְכֹהֲנִים וּלְכֹהֵן מָשׁוּחַ! אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה, הָכִי קָאָמַר: אֶחָד יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאֶחָד כֹּהֲנִים וְאֶחָד כֹּהֵן מָשׁוּחַ מִתְכַּפְּרִין בְּשָׂעִיר הַמִּשְׁתַּלֵּחַ בִּשְׁאָר עֲבֵירוֹת, וְאֵין חִילּוּק בֵּינֵיהֶן. וּמָה בֵּין יִשְׂרָאֵל לְכֹהֲנִים וּלְכֹהֵן מָשׁוּחַ? אֶלָּא שֶׁהַפָּר מְכַפֵּר עַל הַכֹּהֲנִים עַל טוּמְאַת מִקְדָּשׁ וְקָדָשָׁיו וְכוּ׳.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **This** mishna **itself** is **difficult,** as **it teaches: Both Israelites and priests and** the **anointed priest** equally achieve atonement. **But then it teaches: What is** the difference **between Israelites, priests, and** the **anointed priest? Rav Yehuda said** that **this** is what the mishna **is saying: Both Israelites and priests and** the **anointed priest** equally **achieve atonement through the scapegoat for transgressions other** than the defiling of the Temple or its sacrificial foods, **and** in this regard **there is no distinction between them. But what is** the difference **between Israelites, priests, and** the **anointed priest?** The difference is **only that the bull** of the High Priest that he offers on Yom Kippur **atones for the priests for** their **defiling of** the **Temple or its sacrificial** foods, whereas the Israelites achieve atonement for their transgressions through the goats that are sacrificed on Yom Kippur.

### Clarification


Rav Yehudah clarifies: *echad Yisrael ve-echad Kohanim ve-echad Kohen Mashuach miskapprin bi-se'ir ha-mishtale'ach bi-she'ar aveiros* — all three categories receive atonement from the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* for all sins other than *tumat mikdash*. In the domain of *she'ar aveiros*, there is no distinction among them.

### Tumat Mikdash Distinction

The distinction exists only for *tumat mikdash*: *ella she-ha-par mechapper al Kohanim al tumat mikdash*. For the sins of *tumat mikdash*, the ox atones for the *Kohanim* and the goat atones for *Yisrael*. The *Kohanim* require separate atonement for *tumat mikdash* for two reasons: they are present in the *Mikdash* constantly and therefore more likely to render it impure, and they bear the special responsibility of maintaining and protecting its purity.

### Rabbi Yehudah Confirmed

This idea — that for *she'ar aveiros*, Kohanim and Yisrael share equally in the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* — is Rabbi Yehudah's position, as the *gemara* confirms will be demonstrated from a *baraita*.

## Atonement Sources



### Mikdash Cleansing

The *baraita* from the *Sifra* begins with the *Kohen Gadol*'s service on *Yom Kippur* and traces through the various spaces it atones for: *ve-kipper et Mikdash ha-Kodesh* — he should atone for the sanctuary.

### Inner Sanctuary

*Zeh lifnai ve-lifnim* — that is the innermost chamber, the *Kodesh Kodashim*. *Et Ohel Mo'ed zeh Heichal* — the *Heichal*, where the *shulchan* and *menorah* stood. *Ve-al ha-mizbe'ach ke-mashma'o* — the altar, which Rashi explains refers to the *mizbe'ach ha-zahav*, the inner golden altar where the incense was offered.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַנִּי – רַבִּי יְהוּדָה הִיא. דְּתַנְיָא: ״וְכִפֶּר אֶת מִקְדַּשׁ הַקֹּדֶשׁ״ – זֶה לִפְנַי וְלִפְנִים; ״אֶת אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד״ – זֶה הֵיכָל; ״מִזְבֵּחַ״ – כְּמַשְׁמָעוֹ; ״יְכַפֵּר״ – אֵלּוּ עֲזָרוֹת; ״כֹּהֲנִים״ – כְּמַשְׁמָעוֹ; ״עַם הַקָּהָל״ – אֵלּוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל; ״יְכַפֵּר״ – אֵלּוּ הַלְוִיִּם;
> **Translation:** **And whose** opinion is expressed by the mishna? It **is Rabbi Yehuda, as it is taught** in a *baraita* in exposition of the verse: “He shall bring atonement upon the sanctum of the sacred, and he shall effect atonement upon the Tent of Meeting and the altar, and upon the priests and upon all the people of the congregation shall he bring atonement” (Leviticus 16:33): **“He shall bring atonement upon the sanctum of the sacred”; this** is referring to **the innermost sanctum,** i.e., the Holy of Holies. “**Upon the Tent of Meeting”; this** is referring to the **Sanctuary.** “And the **altar”;** this is understood **in accordance with its plain meaning. “He shall effect atonement”; this** is referring to the Temple **courtyards.** “And upon the **priests”;** this is understood **in accordance with its plain meaning.** “And upon **all the people”; these are the Israelites. “Shall he bring atonement”; this** is referring to **the Levites.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הוּשְׁווּ כּוּלָּן לְכַפָּרָה אַחַת, שֶׁמִּתְכַּפְּרִין בְּשָׂעִיר הַמִּשְׁתַּלֵּחַ בִּשְׁאָר עֲבֵירוֹת. דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה.
> **Translation:** **All of them are equated with regard to** the fact that they are all atoned for through **one atonement,** i.e., **that they are atoned for by the scapegoat for** all **transgressions other** than the defiling of the Temple or its sacrificial foods. This is **the statement of Rabbi Yehuda.**

### Outer Areas

*Yekhapper — elu azarot* — the *azarot*, the outer courtyards including the *ezrat Kohanim* and *ezrat Yisrael* where the outer altar stood. All of these spaces are atoned for through the blood of the ox and the blood of the goat.

### Both Groups

Then the *pasuk* concludes: *ve-al ha-Kohanim ve-al kol am ha-kahal yekhapper* — for the Kohanim and for all the congregation of the people he will atone. *Kohanim ke-mashma'o am ha-kahal elu Yisrael* — the Kohanim and the people of Israel. At this point, the blood of the ox is working for the Kohanim and the blood of the goat is working for Yisrael, for their respective *tumat mikdash* obligations.

### Extra Yechapper


But the verse ends with a final word: *yekhapper* — he will atone. What does this extra *yekhapper* come to include? *Elu ha-Levi'im. Hushvu kullam le-kapparah achat* — the Levites. They are all made equal to a single atonement. But wait: one has an ox and one has a goat. In what sense are they all equivalent? The *baraita* answers: *she-miskapprin bi-se'ir ha-mishtaleach bi-she'ar aveiros — divrei Rabbi Yehudah*. The equivalence indicated by that final *yekhapper* is that for all sins other than *tumat mikdash*, they all receive atonement through the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach*. That is Rabbi Yehudah's reading.

## Yehudah vs. Shimon



### Yehudah: Equality

Rabbi Yehudah holds that for *she'ar aveiros* — all sins other than *tumat mikdash* — both Kohanim and Yisrael receive atonement from the same source: the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* that goes over the cliff. The extra *yekhapper* in the verse establishes their equality in this domain.

### Shimon: Separation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: כְּשֵׁם שֶׁדַּם שָׂעִיר הַנַּעֲשֶׂה בִּפְנִים מְכַפֵּר עַל יִשְׂרָאֵל עַל טוּמְאַת מִקְדָּשׁ וְקָדָשָׁיו, כָּךְ דַּם הַפָּר מְכַפֵּר עַל הַכֹּהֲנִים עַל טוּמְאַת מִקְדָּשׁ וְקָדָשָׁיו. וּכְשֵׁם שֶׁוִּידּוּיוֹ שֶׁל שָׂעִיר הַמִּשְׁתַּלֵּחַ מְכַפֵּר עַל יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּשְׁאָר עֲבֵירוֹת, כָּךְ וִידּוּיוֹ שֶׁל פָּר מְכַפֵּר עַל הַכֹּהֲנִים בִּשְׁאָר עֲבֵירוֹת.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: **Rabbi Shimon says: Just as** the **blood of the goat whose** blood presentation is **performed inside** the Sanctuary **atones for Israelites for** their **defiling of** the **Temple or its sacrificial** foods, **so too,** the **blood of the bull** of the High Priest, whose blood presentation is also performed inside the Sanctuary, **atones for the priests for** their **defiling of** the **Temple or its sacrificial** foods. **And just as** the **confession** made **over the scapegoat atones for Israelites for other transgressions, so too,** the **confession** made **over** the **bull atones for the priests for other transgressions.** It is apparent from the *baraita* that it is only according to the opinion of Rabbi Yehuda that the scapegoat atones for both Israelites and priests.


Rabbi Shimon disagrees. He too reads the *pasuk* carefully: the blood of the inner goat atones for *tumat mikdash* for Yisrael; the blood of the ox atones for *tumat mikdash* for Kohanim — *keshem she-dam sa'ir ha-na'aseh bi-fnim mechapper Yisrael al tumat mikdash ve-kodashav kakh dam par mechapper al Kohanim al tumat mikdash ve-kodashav*. So far there is agreement. But Rabbi Shimon argues that even for *she'ar aveiros*, there is a parallel split. The *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* does not atone for Kohanim for *she'ar aveiros*; something else does.

That something else is the *par* — the ox — but not its blood. Rabbi Shimon draws a distinction within the *par* itself: it has two separate atoning mechanisms. The blood of the ox atones for *tumat mikdash* for Kohanim, just as the blood of the inner goat atones for *tumat mikdash* for Yisrael. But the *viddui* of the ox — the confession the Kohen Gadol recites with his hands on the animal — atones for Kohanim for *she'ar aveiros*, just as the *viddui* of the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* atones for Yisrael for *she'ar aveiros*. The Kohen Gadol performs his *viddui* over the ox, not over the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach*; it is by virtue of that *viddui* that the ox carries away the other sins of the Kohanim. The *se'ir ha-mishtaleach*, over which the *viddui* for Yisrael is recited, handles *she'ar aveiros* for Yisrael alone. Thus *ukheshem she-viddui shel sa'ir ha-mishtale'ach mechapper Yisrael bi-she'ar aveiros kakh viddui shel par mechapper Kohanim bi-she'ar aveiros* — the two mechanisms of the ox map precisely onto the two mechanisms of the goat, one side for *tumat mikdash* and one for *she'ar aveiros*, and in every domain Kohanim and Yisrael have entirely separate sources of atonement. The *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* plays no role for the Kohanim whatsoever — a point Rabbi Shimon will derive from the verse itself, as the next step of the argument shows.

### Shimon's Link

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן, הָא וַדַּאי הוּשְׁווּ! מַאי הוּשְׁווּ – דִּבְנֵי כַּפָּרָה נִינְהוּ; מִיהוּ כֹּל חַד וְחַד מְכַפַּר בִּדְנַפְשֵׁיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara analyzes Rabbi Shimon’s opinion: **And according to Rabbi Shimon,** one can ask: **Weren’t** both Israelites and priests **certainly equated** in the verse in Leviticus? The Gemara explains: According to his opinion, in **what** way **are they equated** in the verse? They are equated in **that they are all subject to atonement** on Yom Kippur; **but each one** of the groups **achieves atonement in its own** way.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי טַעְמֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן? דִּכְתִיב: ״וְלָקַח אֶת שְׁנֵי הַשְּׂעִירִם״ – אִיתַּקַּשׁ שָׂעִיר הַמִּשְׁתַּלֵּחַ לְשָׂעִיר הַנַּעֲשֶׂה בִּפְנִים; מָה שָׂעִיר הַנַּעֲשֶׂה בִּפְנִים אֵינוֹ מְכַפֵּר עַל הַכֹּהֲנִים עַל טוּמְאַת מִקְדָּשׁ וְקָדָשָׁיו, דִּכְתִיב בֵּיהּ: ״אֲשֶׁר לָעָם״; אַף שָׂעִיר הַמִּשְׁתַּלֵּחַ אֵינוֹ מְכַפֵּר עַל הַכֹּהֲנִים בִּשְׁאָר עֲבֵירוֹת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is the reason of Rabbi Shimon,** who holds that priests are not atoned for by the scapegoat? **As it is written: “He shall take the two goats”** (Leviticus 16:7); one is used for the scapegoat and the other for the internal goat. With this verse, the **scapegoat is juxtaposed with the goat whose** blood presentation is **performed inside** the Sanctuary. It teaches that **just as the goat whose** blood presentation is **performed inside does not atone for the priests for** their **defiling of** the **Temple or its sacrificial** foods, **as it is written with regard to** that goat: “The goat sin-offering **of the people”** (Leviticus 16:15), which indicates that it atones for Israelites and not for the priests, **so too, the scapegoat does not atone for the priests for** their **other transgressions.**

What is his proof? *Ve-lakach et shenei ha-se'irim* — take the two goats: the inner goat and the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach*. These two are linked by the verse. Just as the inner goat is specified as *asher la-am* — belonging to the people — and therefore atones only for Yisrael (not for Kohanim) with respect to *tumat mikdash*, so too the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach*, being linked to the inner goat, atones only for Yisrael for *she'ar aveiros* as well. *Af se'ir ha-mishtaleach eino mechapper al Kohanim bi-she'ar aveiros*.

### Bull Viduy

What then atones for the Kohanim for *she'ar aveiros*? The *viduy* recited over the ox. The *Kohen Gadol* places his hands on the ox and confesses: *chatasi, avisi, pashati, ani u-veiti* — and it is this *viduy* on the ox that carries the atonement for the Kohanim's other sins, just as the *viduy* on the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* carries that atonement for Yisrael. The ox, therefore, has two distinct atoning functions: its blood atones for *tumat mikdash* for Kohanim, and its *viduy* atones for *she'ar aveiros* for Kohanim.

### Same Result

On this reading, the extra *yekhapper* in the verse is not establishing that they all share the same vehicle of atonement. Rather, *hushvu kullam le-kapparah achat* means only that they all end up atoned — *de-bnai kapparah ninhu* — the outcome is the same. But the process differs: *kol chad ve-chad mechapper mi-denafshei* — each group receives atonement from its own distinct source.

### Yehudah Counter

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה אָמַר לָךְ: לְהָכִי אִיתַּקּוּשׁ – שֶׁיִּהְיוּ שָׁוִים בְּמַרְאֶה וּבְקוֹמָה וּבְדָמִים הוּא דַּאֲתָא.
> **Translation:** **And** as for **Rabbi Yehuda,** how does he understand the juxtaposition? He could have **said to you:** It is only **for this** reason that the goats **are juxtaposed:** The juxtaposition **comes** to teach **that they should be similar in appearance and in height and in value.** They are not similar in the atonement that they effect.

Rabbi Yehudah responds to Rabbi Shimon's linkage argument: yes, the two goats are linked by the verse — but they are linked only in the sense that *shehayu shavin be-mareh u-ve-komah u-ve-damim*: they must be identical in appearance, equal in height, and purchased at the same price. *Hu de-asa* — that is all the linkage establishes. It does not follow that because one is exclusively for Yisrael, so too must the other be exclusively for Yisrael.

### Own Source

For Rabbi Yehudah, therefore, the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach* atones for *she'ar aveiros* for everyone — Kohen and Yisrael alike. For Rabbi Shimon, even in the domain of *she'ar aveiros*, each group draws atonement from its own separate source: Yisrael from the *se'ir ha-mishtaleach*, and Kohanim from the *viduy* of the ox.
