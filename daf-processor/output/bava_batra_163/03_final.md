## Body-Witness Gap



### Two-line rule

The *gemara* on 163a continues its discussion of the white space between the body of a *shtar* and the signatures of the *eidim*. The central concern is straightforward: if too much blank space is left between the end of the document's text and the witnesses' signatures, a forger could insert an additional line of content — one line that would be incorporated into the *shtar* itself, followed by a second line of boilerplate — thereby altering the meaning of the document. For this reason, two full lines of gap are prohibited.

### One-line gap permitted

A single line of space, however, is permissible. The *eidim* need some room to sign; they cannot always be expected to inscribe their names immediately flush against the last line of text. Even if that one-line space were filled in, the principle that we do not derive legal force from the final line of a document means we would not act on whatever is inserted there.

### Dots prevent abuse

The *gemara* turns to the space between the witnesses' signatures and the *beit din*'s *kiyum* — its formal validation of those signatures. Why are we not afraid that someone will fill in the gap between the *eidim* and the *kiyum*, insert forged witnesses, and then exploit the *kiyum* as if it validates the inserted material? The answer is that the blank area is rendered unusable by filling it with dots and splotches, preventing that space from being written upon in any meaningful way.

## Erasure concerns



### Rav: erasure kasher

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֵיחוּשׁ דִּלְמָא גָּיֵיז לֵיהּ לְעֵילָּא, וּמָחֵיק לֵיהּ לִטְיוּטָא, וְכָתֵב מַאי דְּבָעֵי, וּמַחְתִּים סָהֲדֵי; וְאָמַר רַב: שְׁטָר הַבָּא הוּא וְעֵדָיו עַל הַמְּחָק – כָּשֵׁר!
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises another issue: **And let** there be **a concern** that **perhaps** the holder of the document will **excise** the entire text that appears **above** the signatures, **and** then **erase the inked-in** part **and write whatever he wants** in that erased area, **and** have unscrupulous **witnesses sign** it. **And** this would be a valid document, as **Rav says: A document that comes** before the court for ratification in which **its** content **and** the signatures of **its witnesses are** both written **over an erasure** is **valid.** The court ratification would then be assumed to be referring to this new, forged document.

At the bottom of 163a, the *gemara* raises a deeper question. The dots and splotches prevent someone from simply writing in the blank space, but what about a more sophisticated attack: cutting away the top portion of the *shtar*, erasing the smudgy area, and then rewriting whatever one wishes? The obvious defense is that anything written on an erased background is suspect — but the *gemara* immediately complicates this by citing Rav's ruling: *shtar ha-ba hu v'eidav al ha-machak kasher* — a *shtar* in which both the body and the witnesses' signatures appear on an erased background is valid.

To appreciate why this ruling is relevant, one must understand that the *gemara* will establish later — though it is worth anticipating here — that a single erasure looks visibly different from a double erasure. With that principle in mind, consider what concerns might attach to a *shtar* written entirely on an erased background. One might worry that the document originally had different content written on a clean background, which was then erased and replaced. But if both the body and the *eidim* are on the erased background, there is nothing particularly alarming: even if the entire document was composed on a previously erased surface, the witnesses' genuine signatures are there, and the *shtar* functions normally. The forger cannot gain anything simply by using erased parchment.

[DAF:163b]
### Full erasure risk

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנִיחָא לְרַב כָּהֲנָא – דְּמַתְנֵי לַהּ מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דִּשְׁמוּאֵל, שַׁפִּיר; אֶלָּא לְרַב טָבְיוֹמֵי – דְּמַתְנֵי לַהּ מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַב, מַאי אִיכָּא לְמֵימַר?
> **Translation:** **This works out well,** i.e., this concern does not apply, **according to Rav Kahana, who teaches in the name of Shmuel** that a document in which its content and its witnesses’ signatures are both written over an erasure is valid; according to him, all **is well.** It is Rav who says that an inked-in gap between the witnesses’ signatures and the court’s ratification is acceptable, and it is Shmuel who says that a document that is written and signed over an erasure is acceptable. **But according to Rav Tavyumei, who teaches** this latter statement **in the name of Rav, what can be said?** According to him, Rav said both statements, and taken together they pose a difficulty: The inked-in gap between the witnesses’ signatures and the ratification can easily be erased and a new document with signatures can be written over the erasure.

The more serious concern is this: since the background is already erased, a forger could erase the top portion a second time and rewrite it with different content, leaving the witnesses' signatures intact on their once-erased section. He would then present the document with a *kiyum* — and the *kiyum*, having been issued on the original signatures, would appear to validate his fraudulent version. This is not, however, a concern unique to the specific scenario involving smudgy space below the *kiyum*. It is a general challenge to Rav's ruling: whenever there is a *kiyum* on a *shtar*, what prevents someone from erasing the entire top, rewriting it, and reusing the *kiyum*?

The *gemara* addresses the internal tension. It notes that according to Rav Kahana, who transmits the ruling that *shtar chuv eidav al ha-machak kasher* in the name of Shmuel, there is no contradiction — because Rav himself may have held that a *shtar* with both body and witnesses on erased background is invalid. Under Rav's own view, then, such a forged *shtar* — with the rewritten top and the witnesses both on an erased background — would be disqualified, and the *kiyum* would be of no use. But according to Rav Tavyumi, who transmits this ruling in the name of Rav himself, the difficulty remains: if Rav considers such a *shtar* valid, how does he protect against the reuse of a *kiyum*?

### Validation voided

The *gemara* resolves this with a direct ruling: *kol ki hai gavna ein mekaymin oso min ha-asharta shebo ela min ha-eidim shebo* — in precisely this case, where the entire *shtar* is written on an erased background, one does not rely on the embedded *kiyum* at all. The *kiyum* is effectively voided. Instead, the witnesses must be validated directly before the court. The fear that someone erased the entire top and reused the existing *kiyum* is neutralized precisely because we will never rely on a *kiyum* when the full document appears on a *machak* background — we insist on direct testimony about the witnesses' authenticity.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָסָבַר: כׇּל כִּי הַאי גַוְונָא, אֵין מְקַיְּימִין אוֹתוֹ מִן הָאַשַּׁרְתָּא שֶׁבּוֹ, אֶלָּא מִן הָעֵדִים שֶׁבּוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: Rav **maintains** that in **all cases like this,** where a document and its witnesses’ signatures are written over an erasure and there is a court ratification on a non-erased part of the paper, the later court **ratifies** the document **not on the basis of the** previous court’s **ratification that is on it, but** only **on the basis of the** signatures of the **witnesses that are on it.** Therefore, the forging of a document in this manner is impossible, as the prior ratification of the court is disregarded, and the witnesses will attest to what they signed upon.

## Witness-Validation Gap



### One-line doc valid

Rabbi Yochanan now introduces a set of distinctions about the permissible gap between the witnesses' signatures and the *kiyum*. He begins with a related rule: *lo shanu ela bein eidim le-chtav* — the rule permitting one line of space (and prohibiting two) applies between the body of the document and the witnesses. However, between the witnesses and the *ashrata* — the validation mark — *afilu shita achas pasul*: even a single blank line is invalid.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: לֹא שָׁנוּ אֶלָּא בֵּין הָעֵדִים לַכְּתָב, אֲבָל בֵּין עֵדִים לָאַשַּׁרְתָּא – אֲפִילּוּ שִׁיטָה אַחַת פָּסוּל.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites another opinion: **And Rabbi Yoḥanan says: They taught** in the *baraita* that a gap of one line does not invalidate the document **only** in the case where that space is **between the witnesses’** signatures **and the text** of the document. **But** if the gap is **between the witnesses’** signatures **and** a court’s **ratification** of the document, then a space of **even one line** renders the document **not valid.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי שְׁנָא בֵּין עֵדִים לָאַשַּׁרְתָּא – דִּלְמָא גָּיֵיז לְעִילַּאי, וְכָתֵב הוּא וְעֵדָיו בְּשִׁיטָה אַחַת, וְקָסָבַר: שְׁטָר הַבָּא הוּא וְעֵדָיו בְּשִׁיטָה אַחַת – כָּשֵׁר;
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is different** about the case where there is a one-line gap **between the witnesses’** signatures **and** the court’s **ratification** that you say it is not valid? The Gemara answers: There is a concern that **perhaps** the holder of the document will **excise** the entire text of the document at **the top** of the paper **and** then **write** a new, brief document, with **its** text **and** the signatures of **its witnesses on one line.** The court’s ratification will appear to verify the new, forged document. **And** Rabbi Yoḥanan **holds** that **a document that comes** before the court with **its** text **and** the signatures of **its witnesses** appearing **on one line is valid.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, בֵּין עֵדִים לַכְּתָב נָמֵי – דִּלְמָא גָּיֵיז לֵיהּ לְעִילַּאי, וְכָתֵב מַאי דְּבָעֵי, וַחֲתִימִי סָהֲדֵי! קָא סָבַר: שְׁטָר הַבָּא הוּא בְּשִׁיטָה אַחַת, וְעֵדָיו בְּשִׁיטָה אַחֶרֶת – פָּסוּל.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **If so,** the same problem **also** exists when there is a one-line gap **between the witnesses’** signatures **and the text:** There should be a concern that **perhaps** he will **excise** the entire text of the document at **the top** of the paper **and** then **write whatever he wants** in a brief, one-line document. **And the witnesses’** signatures on the next line, which are there from the old document, will still be **signed** there, appearing to attest to the veracity of the new, one-line document. The Gemara answers: Rabbi Yoḥanan **holds** that **a document that comes** before the court with **its** text **on one line and** the signatures of **its witnesses** appearing **on another line,** i.e., on the following line, is **not valid.**

### Separate lines invalid

The concern with a one-line gap between the witnesses and the *kiyum* becomes clear with the *gemara*'s next question. Since Rabbi Yochanan holds that a one-line *shtar* — that is, a document whose entire content occupies a single line — with the witnesses signing on that same line is valid, even a one-line gap before the *kiyum* is dangerous. A forger could cut away the body of the document, write his own one-line *shtar*, place the *eidim* on the same line, and then exploit the *kiyum* that follows.

### Small doc concern



The *gemara* articulates this precisely: *dilma megayez lei le-ila'i ve-chasav hu ve-edav be-shita achas* — perhaps the forger will cut away the top and write a one-line document together with the witnesses all on a single line, thereby producing a valid *shtar* backed by an existing *kiyum*. Since one-line documents with witnesses on the same line are in principle valid, no gap at all may be left between the witnesses and the *kiyum*.

Tosafot raises the question of why the forger would not be exposed by the *machak* — if he cuts away and rewrites, the erased area would be visible. Tosafot answers that Rabbi Yochanan is addressing a different scenario than Rav. Rav's ruling concerns a case where the space before the *kiyum* has already been rendered smudgy, and any erasure would be detectable. Rabbi Yochanan is speaking of a case where the background is clean — no smudgy section separates the *eidim* from the *kiyum*. In that case, there is no *machak* evidence to expose the forgery, and even one clean blank line cannot be tolerated.

The Rashbam explains the underlying logic: since the Rabbis did not wish to define a minimum size for a *shtar*, a one-line document remains valid. But because a one-line *shtar* is possible, the single-line gap rule that applies between the body and the witnesses cannot be carried over to the gap between the witnesses and the *kiyum*. For the body-witness gap, we permit one line because witnesses need some space to sign. But permitting even one line between the witnesses and the *kiyum* would enable the precise fraud Rabbi Yochanan describes.

### Witness notation


The *gemara* then asks: since a one-line *shtar* with witnesses on the same line is valid, why isn't there a risk even when we allow one line between the *eidim* and the body? Could someone not cut that line off, leaving the witnesses' signatures intact, insert their own one-line document, and then present the valid witnesses alongside a *kiyum*? The *gemara* addresses this: *ve-kasavar shtar ha-ba hu ve-edav be-shita achas kasher* — yes, such a document is valid. But *shtar ha-ba hu be-shita achas ve-edav be-shita acheres pasul* — a one-line *shtar* with the witnesses signing on a *different* line is invalid.

The Rashbam explains: any full-sized *shtar* must allow one line of space for the witnesses to sign, because they cannot always be expected to crowd right up against the last line of text. Since one line must be permitted, we are in effect vulnerable to the attack of cutting off that line and using the witnesses elsewhere. Therefore, a one-line *shtar* with witnesses on a separate line is ruled invalid — exactly because this is the scenario the forger would construct. A one-line *shtar* with witnesses on the *same* line is safe, because then the *eidim* cannot be detached and reused: the forger would have to forge them himself, which exposes him in a different way.

It is worth pausing to note that none of this reasoning is derived from *pesukim* or from a claim that these rules constitute *halacha le-Moshe mi-Sinai*. The entire framework is built on logic, scenario analysis, and assessments of human behavior — much like other areas we have seen in *Bava Batra*, such as *hezek re'iyah* or *chazakah gimel shanim*. The Rabbis are constructing a rational system for governing commercial life, drawing on their judgments about what forgers are likely to attempt, what a *beis din* can be expected to detect, and how much burden can reasonably be placed on ordinary signatories. This is what can make *Bava Batra* feel somewhat different in character from areas of *gemara* rooted in *pesukim* or in clearly ritual *de-rabbanan* enactments: the authority is real, but the grounding is the Rabbis' role as architects of a functioning legal order rather than interpreters of a specific textual or ritual tradition.

### Extra witness risk

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֵיחוּשׁ דִּלְמָא כָּתֵב הוּא וְעֵדָיו בְּשִׁיטָה אַחַת, וְאָמַר: אֲנָא לְרַבּוֹת בְּעֵדִים הוּא דַּעֲבַדִי!
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **And let** there be **a concern** that **perhaps** the holder of the document will excise the entire document, leaving only the blank line and the signatures that follow it, and he will **write** a brief document in which **its** text **and** the signatures of **its witnesses** are **on one line,** followed by the original signatures that remained from the original document, **and** he will **say: I did this** in order **to increase** the number of **witnesses,** the more to publicize the matter written in the document. The document is therefore still forgeable.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָסָבַר: כֹּל כִּי הַאי גַוְונָא – אֵין מְקַיְּימִין אוֹתוֹ מִן הָעֵדִים שֶׁלְּמַטָּה, אֶלָּא מִן הָעֵדִים שֶׁלְּמַעְלָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: Rabbi Yoḥanan **maintains** that in **all cases like this,** where a document and its witnesses’ signatures are written on one line, followed by other signatures on subsequent lines, the court **ratifies** the document **not on the basis of the** signatures of the **witnesses that** are **on the bottom, but on the basis of the** signatures of the **witnesses that** are **on top,** on the same line as the text. Therefore, the forging of a document in this manner is impossible. The false signatures of the witnesses will be discovered when those witnesses attest to what they signed upon.

The *gemara* raises one further challenge. Given that a one-line *shtar* with witnesses on the same line is valid, could a forger not write his own one-line *shtar* with forged *eidim* on the same line, and then additionally present the genuine *eidim* whose signatures appear below, arguing he added extra witnesses? He would say: *ana l'rabot be-eidin hu d'avidna* — I was merely adding supplementary witnesses. He would then ask that the *kiyum* be applied to those additional witnesses, since they can be validated directly.

The *gemara* dismisses this: *katav be-che hai gavna ein mekaymin lei ela min eidim she-l'malah* — precisely because of this risk, we insist on relying only on the witnesses who appear on the same line as the one-line document, and we do not validate the supplementary witnesses below. The *kiyum* follows the witnesses on the shared line, and any witnesses appended below are disregarded.

## Erasure detection



### Visual difference

Having resolved the questions about white space, the *gemara* now turns formally to the topic introduced earlier — the principle that a single erasure looks different from a double erasure. The *gemara* states: *eino domeh nimchak pa'am achat le-nimchak shtei pe'amim* — a parchment erased once does not look the same as one erased twice. This principle is what permits Rav's ruling that a *shtar* with body and witnesses both on an erased background is valid, for if the top were re-erased and rewritten, the discrepancy would be visible.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוּפָא – אָמַר רַב: שְׁטָר הַבָּא הוּא וְעֵדָיו עַל הַמְּחָק – כָּשֵׁר.
> **Translation:** § Having cited Rav’s statement, the Gemara discusses **the** matter **itself. Rav says: A document that comes** before the court in which **its** content **and** the signatures of **its witnesses are** both written **over an erasure** is **valid.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִם תֹּאמַר: מוֹחֵק וְחוֹזֵר וּמוֹחֵק! אֵינוֹ דּוֹמֶה נִמְחַק פַּעַם אַחַת, לְנִמְחַק שְׁתֵּי פְעָמִים.
> **Translation:** **And if you say:** There is a possibility of forgery with such a document, as the holder of the document can **erase** the original writing on the paper, then write the text of the document and have witnesses sign on the part that had been erased, **then erase** the document text **once again,** substituting for it a text that is more to his advantage, leaving the original signatures in place, this is not a valid argument. Paper that has been **erased once is not similar** in appearance **to** paper that has been **erased twice.** It will be seen that the signatures are on a place that had been erased once and that the text is written on a place that had been erased twice, and the forgery will be noticed.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֵיחוּשׁ דִּלְמָא שָׁדֵי דְּיוֹתָא אַמְּקוֹם עֵדִים מֵעִיקָּרָא, וּמָחֵיק לֵיהּ – דְּכִי הָדַר מָחֵיק לֵיהּ לְהַאי, הָוֵה לֵיהּ אִידֵּי וְאִידֵּי נִמְחַק שְׁתֵּי פְעָמִים!
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **But let** there be **a concern** that **perhaps** the holder of the document will **initially,** after the entire original document, including the signatures, has been erased, but before the second one is written, **throw** some **ink on the place** where the **witnesses** are to sign under the text of the second document, **and** then **erase** that ink. He will do this **so that when,** after the witnesses have signed, **he then erases** the document text and writes a false text, it will emerge that both **this,** the new document’s text, **and that,** the signatures, will be on paper that had been **erased twice.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי, קָסָבַר רַב: אֵין הָעֵדִים חוֹתְמִין עַל הַמְּחָק, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן נִמְחַק בִּפְנֵיהֶם.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said** in response: **Rav holds** that **witnesses may not sign** a document written over **an erasure unless** the paper **was erased in their presence,** i.e., unless they saw the paper after its old text had been erased, before the new text was written. They will then see that the place where they are to sign has been erased twice, while the place where the document is to be written has been erased only once. They will realize that this leaves open an opportunity of subsequent erasing and falsification, and they will refrain from signing.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵיתִיבִי: הוּא עַל הַנְּיָיר וְעֵדָיו עַל הַמְּחָק – כָּשֵׁר. וְנֵיחוּשׁ דִּלְמָא מָחֵיק לֵיהּ, וְכָתֵיב מַאי דְּבָעֵי, וְהָוֵי לֵיהּ הוּא וְעֵדָיו עַל הַמְּחָק!
> **Translation:** The Gemara **raises an objection** to the opinion of Rav from a *baraita*: A document in which **its** text is **on** a part of the **paper** that has never had writing erased **and** the signatures of **its witnesses** are **on an erasure** is **valid.** The Gemara suggests: **But let us be concerned** that **perhaps** the holder of the document will **erase** the text **and write** in its place **whatever he wants, and it will** then **be** a document where both **its** text **and** the signatures of **its witnesses** are **on an erasure.** Since Rav maintains that such a document is valid, it is easily forgeable in this manner.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּכָתְבִי הָכִי: ״אֲנַחְנָא סָהֲדֵי – חֲתַמְנָא עַל מְחָקָא, וּשְׁטָרָא – כְּתִב עַל נְיָירָא״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: Such a document is valid only in a case **where** the witnesses **write this: We,** the **witnesses, signed on an erasure, but the document** text **was written on** a part of the **paper** that never had writing erased. If the holder of the document then tries to erase the original text and write new text in its place, the forgery will be noticed.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּכָתְבִי הֵיכָא? אִי מִלְּתַחַת – גָּיֵיז לֵיהּ; אִי עִילַּאי – מָחֵיק לֵיהּ! דְּכָתְבִי בֵּין סָהֲדָא לְסָהֲדָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Where** do the witnesses **write** this declaration? **If** they write it **underneath** their signatures, the holder of the document can simply **excise it.** And **if** they write it **above** their signatures, the holder of the document can **erase it** along with the text of the document. The Gemara answers: **They write** the declaration **between** the signature of one **witness and** the signature of the other **witness.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, אֵימָא סֵיפָא: הוּא עַל הַמְּחָק, וְעֵדָיו עַל הַנְּיָיר – פָּסוּל. אַמַּאי פָּסוּל? הָכָא נָמֵי, נִכְתְּבוּ הָכִי: ״אֲנַחְנָא סָהֲדֵי – חֲתַמְנָא עַל נְיָירָא, וּשְׁטָרָא – עַל מְחָקָא״!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If that is so,** that the *baraita* is discussing a case in which the witnesses write a declaration about the circumstances of the document’s condition, **say the latter clause** of the *baraita*: A document in which **its** text **is on an erasure and** the signatures of **its witnesses are on** a part of **the paper** that never had writing erased is **not valid.** The Gemara presents its question: **Why** is such a document **not valid? Here, too, let** the witnesses **write this: We,** the **witnesses, signed on paper** that never had writing erased, **but the document** text was written **on an erasure.**

### Double erase risk

The *gemara* challenges even this: *v'lichush dilma shadi deyuta amkom eidim me'ikara u'machik lei* — perhaps a forger will deliberately spill ink near the area where the witnesses will sign, erase it in their presence, and thereby arrange for them to sign on a background that has already been erased twice. Then when he erases the top and rewrites it, both sections will appear to have been erased twice, and no discrepancy will be visible.

### Witnesses witness erasure

Abaye resolves this by invoking a further principle attributed to Rav: *ein eidim chotmim al ha-machak ela im ken nimchak bifneihem* — witnesses may sign on an erased background only if the erasure was performed in their presence. The Rashbam explains: the witnesses will examine the full background before signing. If they observe any inconsistency — if the area where they are asked to sign looks different from the rest of the document — they will refuse to sign. Having seen the full document erased in their presence, they will know whether the background is uniform. A deliberate ink-spill and re-erasure, done immediately before signing, would produce a visible discrepancy that the witnesses themselves would notice and reject.

### Clean body valid


The *gemara* now presents a *baraita*: *hu al ha-niyar ve-adav al ha-machak kasher* — if the body of the document is on a clean, unerased background and the witnesses have signed on an erased background, the *shtar* is valid. What is there to fear? One might worry that there were previously different witnesses, whose signatures were erased and replaced with new ones — but this would be an absurd and harmful substitution from the forger's perspective, since it would only weaken rather than strengthen his document. So there is no obvious concern.

Yet the *gemara* probes further: this scenario seems to invite abuse. What prevents someone from writing a *shtar* on clean parchment, having witnesses sign on the erased bottom portion, and then immediately erasing the top and rewriting whatever he wishes? Once the top is erased, the entire *shtar* is on a *machak* background, and Rav's ruling validates it. The *gemara* responds that such a *shtar*, in the form described, requires a special notation: the witnesses write between their own signatures — *bein sahada le-sahada* — that the body of the document was written on a clean background while they signed on an erased one. This embedded notation protects the document: cutting it off would sever one witness's signature, and erasing it would produce a double erasure detectable by comparison to the once-erased section directly adjacent.

### Erased body invalid

The opposite configuration — *hu al ha-machak ve-eidav al ha-niyar pasul* — where the body is on an erased background and the witnesses sign on a clean background, is invalid. The reason is that this configuration shouts its own fraudulence: the most natural explanation for the top being erased while the bottom is clean is that the document originally had a legitimate text on a clean background, which was erased and replaced with a forgery.

### Notation solution

The *gemara* asks: why not apply the same solution here — have the witnesses insert a notation between their signatures stating that the body was on an erased background? This would explain the anomaly innocently. The *gemara* pushes back: if we allow this, we must address the concern that the top will be re-erased and rewritten — and the response, as before, is that a double erasure is visible. But then the *gemara* asks why the *pasul* ruling stands at all. Cannot the witnesses simply note between their signatures that the body was on a *machak*, and then we are protected against re-erasure by the double-erasure principle?

### Comparison needed

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** [הַשְׁתָּא נָמֵי] מַאי אָמְרַתְּ – מוֹחֵק חוֹזֵר וּמוֹחֵק? הָא אָמְרַתְּ: אֵינוֹ דּוֹמֶה נִמְחַק פַּעַם אַחַת, לְנִמְחַק שְׁתֵּי פְעָמִים! הָנֵי מִילֵּי – הֵיכָא דַּחֲתִימִי סָהֲדֵי אַמְּחָקָא; הֵיכָא דְּלָא חֲתִימִי סָהֲדֵי אַמְּחָקָא אֶלָּא אַנְּיָירָא – לָא יְדִיעַ.
> **Translation:** **Now** in this case **as well** it should be valid, as **what** can **you say** to argue that it is a forgeable document? If you say that the holder of the document, having **erased** the original document, can **erase** the writing **once again** and write a new, false document, this is not a concern, as **didn’t you say** that paper that has been **erased once is not similar** in appearance **to** paper that has been **erased twice?** It would therefore be noticeable that the document had been erased a second time, and the forgery would be noticeable. The Gemara answers: **That statement** applies only **when the witnesses are signed on an erasure,** and the appearance of that erasure can be compared with the appearance of a double erasure. But in a case **where the witnesses are signed not on an erasure but on paper** that has not had its writing erased, so that there is no contrast between a single erasure and a double erasure, the forgery would **not** be **known.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלַיְתֵי מְגִילְּתָא אַחֲרִיתִי, וְלִמְחוֹק וְלִידַמֵּי! לָא דָּמֵי מְחָקָא דְּהָא מְגִילְּתָא, לִמְחָקָא דְּהָא מְגִילְּתָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **But** let the court **bring another parchment,** write something on it **and erase it, and** then **compare** this single erasure with the erasure on the document in question. If the document were erased twice, a contrast would be seen between a single erasure and a double erasure. The Gemara answers: **The erasure of this parchment is not** necessarily **similar to the erasure of that parchment.** A single erasure on one parchment might resemble a double erasure on a different parchment.


The *gemara* answers with a critical limitation: *hani milei heicha de-ichas sahadei a-machaka* — the double-erasure is detectable only when there is a basis of comparison within the same document. In the case where the witnesses sign on a *machak* background, the top can be compared to the bottom: if the top is re-erased, it will look different from the once-erased bottom. But where the witnesses are on clean parchment and the top alone is on a *machak*, there is no internal reference point for comparison. One cannot tell whether the top was erased once or twice when the only available comparison is clean parchment, not a once-erased section.

The *gemara* considers whether one could bring a separate scroll, erase a portion of it, and compare it to the top of the suspect document. This is rejected: *lo dami machaka de-hai megilta le-machaka de-hai megilta* — no two scrolls erase identically. The texture, age, and composition of each piece of parchment differ, so a once-erased patch on one scroll cannot serve as a reliable reference for detecting a twice-erased patch on another. The principle that twice-erased looks different from once-erased is not a freestanding perceptual fact; it only holds when both erasures appear on the same piece of parchment, allowing a direct internal comparison. In the cross-scroll test, we would be introducing false positives and false negatives, precisely because natural variation between documents swamps the signal we are trying to detect.

The *gemara* then asks whether one might validate the witnesses' signatures before the court, then erase those very signatures and use the resulting once-erased area on the same document as a comparison. If the top looks similar to this freshly erased area, the top was erased only once; if it looks different, the top was erased twice.

### Age of erasure

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְקַבְּלַהּ לַחֲתִימוּת יְדָא דְּסָהֲדִי בְּבֵי דִינָא, וְלִמְחוֹק וְלִידַמֵּי! אָמַר רַב הוֹשַׁעְיָא: אֵינוֹ דּוֹמֶה נִמְחַק בֶּן יוֹמוֹ, לְנִמְחַק בֶּן שְׁנֵי יָמִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara continues to suggest: **But let us accept,** i.e., verify, **the signatures of the witnesses** on the document **in court,** after which they may safely be erased; **and** then **erase** the signatures **and compare** that erasure to the erasure of the document text, to see if it was erased once or twice. In response to this question **Rav Hoshaya says:** That which is **erased on the same day** that it was written **is not** necessarily **similar to** that which was **erased two days** ago, i.e., more than a day after it was written. An older erasure looks different from a new one, so the comparison might not show that there was a double erasure in the document.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִישַׁהֲיֵיהּ! אָמַר רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה: חָיְישִׁינַן לְבֵית דִּין טוֹעִין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **But let us retain** the document for an extra day, at which point both erasures will be old and can be compared. **Rabbi Yirmeya said** in response: **We are concerned for** the possibility of **an erring court.** If such complicated procedures were used in order to declare a document valid, there would be a chance that a particular court would not apply them properly, and that court would end up ratifying a document that was not valid.

Rav Hoshaya rejects this: *ein domeh nimchak bein yomo le-nimchak bein shenei yamim* — a freshly erased area does not look the same as an area that was erased some time ago. The original erasure on the top of the document may have been performed months or years before the signatures were affixed. A fresh comparison erasure will therefore not produce a reliable match, yielding false positives and false negatives. One might propose waiting a period of time before making the comparison, allowing the fresh erasure to age to match the original — but this merely pushes the problem back, since we do not know how long ago the original erasure was made, and in any case an aged erasure on one scroll still cannot perfectly replicate the aging of another.

### Beit Din error risk

Rabbi Yirmiyah closes the discussion with a broader principle: *chiyshinan le-vei dina to'in* — once the analysis reaches this level of complexity, we are concerned that the *beit din* will err in making the required distinctions. A system of document validation that depends on comparing the visual texture of erasures, with all the variables of parchment type, erasure age, and comparison methodology, is simply too prone to judicial mistake. Accordingly, the invalid scenario remains invalid, and no notation between the witnesses' signatures can rescue it.

To summarize the full picture: a *shtar* with body and witnesses both on a *machak* background is valid, provided the *kiyum* is disregarded and the witnesses are validated directly. A *shtar* with a clean body and witnesses on a *machak* is valid, provided the witnesses insert a notation between their signatures. A *shtar* with the body on a *machak* and the witnesses on a clean background is invalid, and no notation can repair it.

## Dating systems



### Mekushar form

The *gemara* transitions to a new topic, returning to the *mekushar* — the tied or folded document. By way of reminder: a *get mekushar* is structured so that the text appears on both sides of the fold, with the witnesses signing on the outside. According to the standard ruling, witnesses on the inside of a *mekushar* disqualify it.

### Inside witnesses valid

Rabbi Chanina ben Gamliel dissents: he holds that if the witnesses signed on the inside, the document remains valid, because one can simply undo the knots, open the document, and it will function as a standard *pashut* document. Tosafot notes that Rabbi Chanina ben Gamliel's position implies that the writing in a *mekushar* appeared on both sides of the fold — otherwise, when opened, the document would display large blank spaces and not resemble a normal *shtar*.

### Dating difference

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי חֲנִינָא בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל אוֹמֵר: מְקוּשָּׁר וְכוּ׳. הֵשִׁיב רַבִּי לְדִבְרֵי רַבִּי חֲנִינָא בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל:
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that **Rabbi Ḥanina ben Gamliel says: A tied** document whose witnesses wrote their signatures inside of it is valid, because one can transform it into an ordinary document by untying it. **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **raised an objection to the statement of Rabbi Ḥanina ben Gamliel:**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַהֲלֹא אֵינוֹ דּוֹמֶה זְמַנּוֹ שֶׁל זֶה לִזְמַנּוֹ שֶׁל זֶה! פָּשׁוּט; מָלַךְ שָׁנָה – מוֹנִין לוֹ שָׁנָה, שְׁתַּיִם – מוֹנִין לוֹ שְׁתַּיִם. מְקוּשָּׁר; מָלַךְ שָׁנָה – מוֹנִין לוֹ שְׁתַּיִם, שְׁתַּיִם – מוֹנִין לוֹ שָׁלֹשׁ.
> **Translation:** **But the date of this one,** a tied document, **is not the same as the date of that one,** an ordinary document. In **an ordinary** document, when the king has **reigned** for one **year,** one **year is counted for him,** and when he has reigned for **two** years, **two** years **are counted for him.** By contrast, in **a tied** document, when the king has **reigned** for one **year, two** years **are counted for him,** and when he has reigned for **two** years, **three** years **are counted for him.** If a tied document is simply opened up and used as an ordinary document, then it will emerge that it is postdated by a year.

Rabbi responds to Rabbi Chanina ben Gamliel's position at the top of 164b: *she-lo eino domeh zemano shel zeh le-zemano shel zeh* — the date in one cannot be converted to match the date of the other, because the dating systems for a *pashut* and a *mekushar* differ. In a *pashut*, the standard reckoning applies: if a king has reigned for one year, the document says "year one"; if two years, "year two." In a *mekushar*, however, the system adds one: a king who has reigned for one year is recorded as "year two," and a king of two years is recorded as "year three." The Rashbam explains this increment as a form of *kavod* — a gesture of honor implying the king had already been ruling when the document was drawn. One might also suggest that the difference reflects competing conventions about whether "year one" means the king has completed one year or is currently in his first year — analogous to the ambiguity discussed in tractate *Rosh Hashanah*.

Whatever the precise origin of the discrepancy, the Rabbis deliberately maintained different systems for the two document types, as part of a broader policy of making *mekushar* and *pashut* documents as formally distinct as possible, to prevent confusion between them.

### Postdating risk

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְזִימְנִין דְּיָזֵיף מִינֵּיהּ זוּזֵי בִּמְקוּשָּׁר, וּמִיתְרְמֵי לֵיהּ זוּזֵי בֵּינֵי בֵּינֵי וּפָרַע לֵיהּ; וְאָמַר לֵיהּ: הַב לִי שְׁטָרַאי, וְאָמַר לֵיהּ: אִירְכַס לִי; וְכָתֵב לֵיהּ תְּבָרָא;
> **Translation:** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi continues: **And** there are **times** this can be problematic, as in a case **where** the debtor **borrows money from** the creditor, and the details of the loan are written **in a tied** document. **And** the debtor **chances upon** some **money in the interim,** i.e., during the first year after the document was written, **and** he **repays** the creditor, **and says to him: Give me** back **my** promissory **note,** as I have just repaid you. **And** the creditor **says to** the debtor: **I lost** the document and cannot give it to you. **And** in lieu of returning the promissory note, the creditor **writes a receipt** for the debtor, as protection against a second collection.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי מָטֵי זִמְנֵיהּ, מְשַׁוֵּי לֵיהּ פָּשׁוּט, וְאָמַר לֵיהּ: הָנֵי הַשְׁתָּא דִּיזַפְתְּ מִינַּאי!
> **Translation:** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi continues: **And** then, **when the time for** repayment written in the promissory note **arrives,** the creditor will **make it** into **an ordinary** document by undoing its stitches and opening it up, **and** he can then **say to** the debtor: **It is now that you borrowed this** money **from me,** as attested in this promissory note, and the receipt you have in your possession is for a previous debt, as its date precedes the date on my document.

The practical danger of converting a *mekushar* to a *pashut* — as Rabbi Chanina ben Gamliel would permit — is illustrated as follows. A borrower takes a loan from a lender during year one of a king's reign. A *mekushar* is drafted, and since *mekushar* dating adds one, the document reads "year two." The borrower repays the loan within the year and asks for his *shtar* back. The lender claims it was lost — *irchas li be-chutla iteyehiv* — and instead provides a receipt dated "year one" using standard *pashut* dating.

Now the lender waits. Once year two actually arrives, he opens the *mekushar* — since, according to Rabbi Chanina ben Gamliel, opened witnesses can convert it to a *pashut* — and presents it as a *pashut shtar* purporting to establish a debt from "year two." The receipt the borrower holds says "year one," so it appears to predate the debt. The lender can now claim the debt was never repaid, seize property from purchasers who acquired land during year two, and harm innocent *lakuchot* who had no knowledge of a loan that never actually existed at that point.

The crucial asymmetry is that neither party necessarily acted in bad faith. The borrower accepted his receipt in good faith, not realizing that the dating systems would diverge when the *mekushar* was converted. A standard postdated *shtar* would have been flagged and rejected; this situation arises precisely because the postdating is structural and invisible.

### No receipts

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָא סָבַר: אֵין כּוֹתְבִין שׁוֹבָר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: Rabbi Ḥanina ben Gamliel **holds** that **one does not write a receipt** in such cases. If a creditor loses his promissory note, the debtor need not pay him at all, out of concern that the debt may one day be collected again when the promissory note is found. He is not required to pay the debt and accept only a receipt, which he will then have to guard permanently to protect himself against a second collection.

The *gemara* resolves this by attributing to Rabbi Chanina ben Gamliel a consistent secondary position: *kasavar ein kotvin shovar* — he holds that receipts should not be written at all. The correct procedure when a loan is repaid is to demand the return of the original *shtar*; no receipt should substitute for it. If receipts are prohibited, the entire scenario collapses — the borrower's only protection is retrieving his *shtar*, and if the *shtar* is genuinely lost, the lender cannot open and repurpose it. The system remains internally coherent.

### Rabbi's learning

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִי בָּקִי רַבִּי בִּמְקוּשָּׁר? וְהָא הָהוּא מְקוּשָּׁר דַּאֲתָא לְקַמֵּיהּ דְּרַבִּי, וְאָמַר רַבִּי: שְׁטָר מְאוּחָר זֶה! וְאָמַר לוֹ זוּנִין לְרַבִּי: כָּךְ מִנְהָגָהּ שֶׁל אוּמָּה זוֹ; מָלַךְ שָׁנָה – מוֹנִין לוֹ שְׁתַּיִם, שְׁתַּיִם – מוֹנִין לוֹ שָׁלֹשׁ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks with regard to the previous discussion: **And was Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **expert in** the *halakhot* of **tied** documents? **But** wasn’t there **a certain tied** document **that came before Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **and** when **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi saw the date he **said: This is a postdated document. And** a Sage named **Zunin said to Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi: **Such is the custom of this nation;** when the king has **reigned** for one **year, two** years **are counted for him,** and when he has reigned for **two** years, **three** years **are counted for him.** The document is therefore not postdated. From this anecdote it is clear that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi himself had not been familiar with this practice.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בָּתַר דְּשַׁמְעַהּ מִזּוּנִין, סַבְרַהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **After** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi **heard it from Zunin** he accepted the explanation **and held this** way himself, and that is what prompted him to raise his objection.


The *gemara* raises an intriguing question: did Rabbi actually know the details of *mekushar* dating well enough to make the objection he raised? The evidence suggests he did not, at least not initially. A *shtar mekushar* was once brought before Rabbi, and he declared it a *shtar me'uchar* — a postdated document — failing to recognize that what appeared to be a discrepancy was simply the standard *mekushar* dating convention. It was Zonim — the name of a person, perhaps related to the Baisos ben Zonim known elsewhere in the *gemara* — who informed him: *kach minhaga shel uma zu* — this is the practice of this nation; a king of one year is recorded as two, a king of two as three.

The *gemara* resolves the apparent contradiction simply: *basar de-shamei mi-Zonim savra* — after Rabbi learned this from Zonim, he incorporated it into his reasoning. His later objection to Rabbi Chanina ben Gamliel's ruling was informed by what he had learned from Zonim. The *gemara* treats this straightforwardly — Rabbi learned something during his life and applied it thereafter.

## Roman archon titles



### Archon dating

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָהוּא שְׁטָרָא דַּהֲוָה כְּתִב בֵּיהּ: ״בִּשְׁנַת פְּלוֹנִי אַרְכָן״. אָמַר רַבִּי חֲנִינָא: יִבְדֹּק אֵימָתַי עָמַד אַרְכָן בְּאַרְכָנוּתֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** § There was **a certain document on which was written,** as its date: **In the year of so-and-so, Archon [*Arkhan*],** a title for a ruler, without stating any particular year of his reign. **Rabbi Ḥanina said: Let it be investigated when** it was that this **Archon rose to his** position of **archon,** i.e., find out the year he came to power, and the validity of the document is established from that year.

A *shtar* is presented to the court in which the date is recorded as "in the year of so-and-so the *arkan*." Tosafot notes that *arkan* does not literally mean king; it derives from the Greek *archon*, referring to an appointed official or governor — in the Roman context, roughly analogous to a consul. The document does not specify which year of this official's tenure; it simply names him and uses his title.

### Archon=year 1

Rabbi Chanina rules: examine when this official began his term in office — *eimasai amad arkan be-arkanutei* — and assume the document refers to the first year of that tenure. Since the title appears without qualification, it defaults to year one.

### Multiple terms

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְדִלְמָא דַּאֲרִיךְ מַלְכוּתֵיהּ! אָמַר רַב הוֹשַׁעְיָא: כָּךְ מִנְהָגָהּ שֶׁל אוּמָּה זוֹ; שָׁנָה רִאשׁוֹנָה – קוֹרִין לוֹ ״אַרְכָן״, שְׁנִיָּה – קוֹרִין לוֹ דִּיגוֹן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **But perhaps** the writer of the document was using an Aramaic or Hebrew term, and intended to say **that the reign of** so-and-so had already **extended [*arikh*]** for several years. **Rav Hoshaya says: Such** is **the custom of this nation** where the document was written: In the **first year** of the king’s reign **they refer to him** with the title **Archon;** in his **second** year **they refer to him** with the title ***Digon*.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְדִלְמָא עַבּוֹרֵי עַבְּרוּהוּ, וַהֲדַר אוֹקְמוּהוּ! אָמַר רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה: הָהוּא ״אַרְכָן דִּיגוֹן״ קָרְאוּ לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **But perhaps** the people **deposed** the ruler **and then reinstated him,** and the document was written in the first year of his second reign. **Rabbi Yirmeya said:** In **that** case, **they** would **refer to him** with the title **Archon *Digon*.**

The *gemara* challenges this: *ve-dilma de-arich malchusei* — perhaps the official served for multiple years, and the document could refer to any one of them. How can we assume year one?

Rav Hoshaya resolves this by explaining the Roman convention: *kach minhaga shel uma zu* — this is the practice of this nation. During his first year in office, an official is called *arkan*. During his second year, he is called *digon* — from the Greek prefix *di-*, meaning two. Since the *shtar* uses the title *arkan* without qualification, it unambiguously refers to the first year.

### Term designation

The *gemara* raises one remaining difficulty: perhaps the official's original term ended, someone else served, and then he was reelected. In that case he would again be in his "first year" of a new tenure, yet the situation is not the same as his original first year. Rabbi Yirmiyah resolves this: *hahu arkan digon karu lei* — if an official returns to office after an interruption, he is not called simply *arkan* but *arkan digon*, signifying that this is his second term of office. The unmodified title *arkan* therefore reliably identifies the first year of his first continuous tenure.

## Greek geometry terms



### Shape-number terms

The *gemara* concludes with a brief but elegant discussion of Greek geometric terminology and its application in two distinct areas of *halacha*. The terms in question are: *acta* (one), *digon* (two), *trigon* (three), *tetragon* (four), and *pentagon* (five). These are shape names — polygon, triangle, square, pentagon — repurposed in Talmudic literature as numerical designations.

### Nazir multiplicity

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״הֲרֵינִי נָזִיר הֵינָא״ – סוֹמְכוֹס אָמַר: ״הֵינָא״ – אַחַת. ״דִּיגוֹן״ – שְׁתַּיִם. ״טְרִיגוֹן״ – שָׁלֹשׁ. ״טֶטְרִיגוֹן״ – אַרְבַּע. ״פֶּנְטִיגוֹן״ – חָמֵשׁ.
> **Translation:** § Apropos these Greek terms, the Gemara cites two *baraitot* that mention them. **The Sages taught** (*Tosefta*, *Nazir* 1:2) that if one said: **I am hereby a nazirite *heina*,** or stated a similar expression with other comparable Greek terms, **Sumakhos said** that his status depends on which term he used. If he used the word ***heina*,** he is a nazirite for **one** term of naziriteship, i.e., thirty days; if he said ***digon*,** he is a nazirite for **two** terms of thirty days each; if he said ***terigon*,** he is a nazirite for **three** terms; ***tetrigon*,** for **four** terms; ***pentigon*,** for **five** terms.

Sumchus applies this vocabulary to *nazir* vows. If someone declares *hareini nazir acta*, he accepts one period of *nezirut* (the standard being thirty days). *Digon* means two periods; *trigon*, three; *tetragon*, four; *pentagon*, five. The Greek shape-names function as numerical shorthand within the context of vow-acceptance.

### Four corners needed

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: בַּיִת עָגוֹל; דִּיגוֹן; טְרִיגוֹן; פֶּנְטִיגוֹן – אֵינוֹ מִטַּמֵּא בִּנְגָעִים. טֶטְרִיגוֹן – מִטַּמֵּא בִּנְגָעִים.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught** in another *baraita* (*Tosefta*, *Nega’im* 6:3): **A round house,** or one that is shaped like **a *digon*,** i.e., it has two walls, one straight and one curved, or one that is shaped like **a *terigon*,** i.e., a triangle, or one that is shaped like **a pentagon, does not become susceptible to the ritual impurity of leprous spots.** If it is shaped like **a *tetrigon*,** i.e., a quadrilateral, it **becomes susceptible to the ritual impurity of leprous spots.**

The *gemara* then cites a *baraita* applying the same geometric vocabulary to the laws of house impurity from *nega'im* — leprosy. A round house (*bayit agul*), a *digon* house (two-cornered), a *trigon* house (triangular), or a *pentagon* house (five-cornered) — *eino metamei be-nega'im*: none of these structures can become *tamei* through the leprosy impurity that afflicts houses. Only a *tetragon* — a four-cornered house — *metamei be-nega'im*.

### Four walls source

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי? דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן, לְמַעְלָה אוֹמֵר: ״קִיר–קִירֹת״ – שְׁתַּיִם; לְמַטָּה אוֹמֵר: ״קִיר–קִירֹת״ – שְׁתַּיִם; הֲרֵי כָּאן אַרְבַּע.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **From where are these matters** derived? The Gemara answers: It is **as the Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The Torah **states above:** “If the plague be in the walls of the house” (Leviticus 14:37). The verse did not state: **A wall,** but **“walls,”** indicating that the house in question has at least **two** walls. And where it **states below:** “If the plague has spread in the walls of the house” (Leviticus 14:39), instead of stating: **A wall,** the verse states **“walls,”** indicating another **two** walls. There **are** a total of **four** walls mentioned **here** in order to indicate that a house can become impure through leprous spots only if it has four sides.

The *gemara* asks: what is the scriptural basis for requiring four walls and four corners? The *baraita* derives it from the verses in *Vayikra* governing house-leprosy: one verse refers to *kir* (a wall) and another to *kirot* (walls, plural). Each use of the plural implies at least two walls, and the combination of two such verses yields four walls — and with four walls comes the requirement of four corners. Hence the leprosy impurity applies only to a *tetragon* structure, completing the *gemara*'s excursion into Greek geometric terminology as applied to *halacha*.
