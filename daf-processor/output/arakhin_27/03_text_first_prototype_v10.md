## Son Buys Father's Field



### Scenario Setup

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ הַלּוֹקֵחַ שָׂדֶה מֵאָבִיו, וּמֵת אָבִיו, וְאַחַר כָּךְ הִקְדִּישָׁהּ — הֲרֵי הִיא כִּשְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה. הִקְדִּישָׁהּ וְאַחַר כָּךְ מֵת אָבִיו — הֲרֵי הִיא כְּשָׂדֶה מִקְנָה, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי מֵאִיר.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **One who purchases** an ancestral **field from his father, and his father** subsequently **died and afterward** the son **consecrated it, its** halakhic status **is like** that of **an ancestral field,** as he inherited his father’s ancestral rights prior to the consecration. Consequently, the field’s redemption price is calculated on the basis of fifty *sela* per *beit kor*, and if another redeems it instead of the son, it is given to the priests during the Jubilee Year. But if the son **consecrated** the field **and afterward his father died, its** halakhic status **is like** that of **a purchased field,** whose redemption price is based on its monetary value, and which will return to the ancestral owner, i.e., the son, at the Jubilee; this is **the statement of Rabbi Meir.**

The mishnah presents the following scenario: *halokeach sadeh me-aviv u-meis aviv* — someone purchased a field from his father and then his father died, after which he sanctified the field. The question is how to classify that field's status for purposes of redemption. Is it a *sdei miknah*, a purchased field, because it was originally acquired through purchase? Or is it a *sdei achuza*, an inherited field, because had the son never purchased it, he would have inherited it regardless? The mishnah rules *harei hi k'sdei achuza* — it is treated as an inherited field — and the son therefore enjoys the full rights of an owner to redeem it and retain it when the *yovel* arrives.

Before turning to the more complex case in the mishnah, it is worth noting a fascinating *Rambam* and *Ra'avad* bearing on the earlier portion of the chapter. The mishnah had established that if the original owner did not personally redeem his field from *hekdesh* but instead bought it from a third party who had redeemed it, he loses the field when *yovel* arrives, since he was not the first to redeem it from the Temple treasury. The *Rambam*, however, rules the opposite: if someone else redeemed the field and the original owner subsequently purchased it from that person, the owner retains it when *yovel* comes. The *Ra'avad* objects sharply — this contradicts an explicit mishnah. He then adds, however, that upon consulting various manuscripts, he actually found a version of the mishnah that reads as the *Rambam* does. This exchange illustrates how much of this area of *halacha* remains underdeveloped in the *gemara*, given its practical irrelevance in later generations.

### Father Dies After Sancti

The mishnah then turns to a harder case: *hikdisha ve-achar kach meis aviv* — he purchased the field, sanctified it, and only afterward did his father die. At the moment of sanctification, the son's relationship to the field was that of a purchaser alone; his father was still alive and the son had not yet inherited anything. Later, when *yovel* arrives, the field would naturally fall into the category of an inherited field since his father has since died. The question is: which moment determines the field's classification — the moment of sanctification or the moment of *yovel*?

### Rabbi Meir's Opinion

*Rabbi Meir* rules *harei hi k'sdei miknah* — it is treated as a purchased field. At the moment the son sanctified it, he had not yet inherited it, and therefore the determinative classification is that of a purchased field.

### Rabbi Yehuda & Shimon

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יְהוּדָה וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמְרִים: הֲרֵי הִיא כִּשְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״אִם מִשְּׂדֵה מִקְנָתוֹ אֲשֶׁר לֹא מִשְּׂדֵה אֲחֻזָּתוֹ״, שָׂדֶה שֶׁאֵינָהּ רְאוּיָה לִהְיוֹת שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה, יָצְתָה זוֹ שֶׁהִיא רְאוּיָה לִהְיוֹת שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon say:** Even in a case where the son consecrated the field before his father died, **its** halakhic status **is like** that of **an ancestral field, as it is stated** with regard to a purchased field: “And **if** he will consecrate unto the Lord **a field that he has bought, which is not of his ancestral field”** (Leviticus 27:22), indicating that this *halakha* applies only to **a field that is not due to become** his **ancestral field,** thereby **excluding this** field, **which** at the time of consecration **is due to become** his **ancestral field** in the future, when his father dies.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שְׂדֵה מִקְנָה אֵינָהּ יוֹצְאָה לַכֹּהֲנִים בַּיּוֹבֵל, שֶׁאֵין אָדָם מַקְדִּישׁ דָּבָר שֶׁאֵינוֹ שֶׁלּוֹ. הַכֹּהֲנִים וְהַלְוִיִּם מַקְדִּישִׁין לְעוֹלָם, וְגוֹאֲלִין לְעוֹלָם, בֵּין לִפְנֵי הַיּוֹבֵל בֵּין לְאַחַר הַיּוֹבֵל.
> **Translation:** The mishna continues: **A purchased field** that was consecrated **is not removed** from the possession of the Temple treasury and given **to the priests during the Jubilee** Year, **as** the purchase of the land was valid only until the Jubilee, at which point fields return to their ancestral owners, and **a person cannot consecrate an item that is not his. The priests and the Levites may always consecrate** their ancestral fields **and may always redeem** their ancestral fields, **both before the Jubilee** Year **and after the Jubilee** Year.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מִנַּיִן לְלוֹקֵחַ שָׂדֶה מֵאָבִיו וְהִקְדִּישָׁהּ וְאַחַר כָּךְ מֵת אָבִיו, מִנַּיִן שֶׁתְּהֵא לְפָנָיו כִּשְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וְאִם מִשְּׂדֵה מִקְנָתוֹ אֲשֶׁר לֹא מִשְּׂדֵה אֲחֻזָּתוֹ״ — שָׂדֶה שֶׁאֵינָהּ רְאוּיָה לִהְיוֹת שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה, יָצְתָה זוֹ, שֶׁרְאוּיָה לִהְיוֹת שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: **From where** is it derived that **one who purchases** an ancestral **field from his father and consecrated it, and afterward his father died, from where** is it derived **that it shall be** treated **for him like an ancestral field? The verse states: “And if** he will consecrate unto the Lord **a field that he has bought, which is not of his ancestral field”** (Leviticus 27:22), indicating that this *halakha* applies only to **a field that is not due to become** his **ancestral field,** thereby **excluding this** field that he purchased from his father, **which** at the time of consecration **is due to become** his **ancestral field;** this is **the statement of Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי מֵאִיר אוֹמֵר: מִנַּיִן לְלוֹקֵחַ שָׂדֶה מֵאָבִיו, וּמֵת אָבִיו וְאַחַר כָּךְ הִקְדִּישָׁהּ, מִנַּיִן שֶׁתְּהֵא לְפָנָיו כִּשְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וְאִם מִשְּׂדֵה מִקְנָתוֹ אֲשֶׁר לֹא מִשְּׂדֵה אֲחֻזָּתוֹ״ — שָׂדֶה שֶׁאֵינָהּ שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה, יָצָאת זוֹ שֶׁהִיא שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Meir says: From where** is it derived with regard to **one who purchases** an ancestral **field from his father, and his father died and afterward he consecrated** the field, **from where** is it derived **that it shall be** treated **for him like an ancestral field? The verse states: “And if** he will consecrate unto the Lord **a field that he has bought, which is not of his ancestral field.”** This teaches that **a field that is not an ancestral field** at the time of consecration is deemed a purchased field, thereby **excluding this** field, **which is** at this point **an ancestral field.** But according to Rabbi Meir, a field that at the time of consecration is merely due to become an ancestral field in the future is deemed a purchased field.

*Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon* rule *harei hi k'sdei achuza* — it is treated as an inherited field. They derive this from the verse: *im misdei miknato asher lo misdei achuzato* — "if from a purchased field that is not from his inherited field." The phrase is apparently redundant: of course a purchased field is not an inherited field. The Torah must therefore be pointing to a case where a field could be both purchased *and* inherited. The exclusion — *sdei she-einah re'uya lihyot sdei achuza*, a purchased field that is not in line to become inherited — carves out the scenario before us. Since this son was in line to inherit the field, it is treated as inherited.

A natural question arises: would *Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon* take this reasoning to its logical extreme and rule that even if the *yovel* arrived while the father was still alive, the field would revert to the son as a *sdei achuza*? That cannot be correct. If the son purchased the field from his father, sanctified it, and then *yovel* hit while the father was still living, logic demands the field revert to the father — it is his ancestral portion. To rule otherwise would allow a son to purchase a field from his father, sanctify it, and then reclaim it as his own *sdei achuza* even though he only ever held a lease-like interest for the remaining years until *yovel*. The ruling of *Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon* must therefore be limited to cases where the father has in fact died before *yovel* arrives. The verse's exclusion applies only to that case: the son was in line to inherit and has now actually inherited; we therefore treat the field as *sdei achuza*.

It is also worth considering the practical difference between the two opinions. If the father has died by the time *yovel* arrives, and the field is classified as a *sdei miknah* per *Rabbi Meir*, then it reverts to the original owner — the father — and passes to all of the father's heirs. If the son has brothers, the field will be divided among them all. By contrast, according to *Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon*, since the son is treated as the field's owner by virtue of *sdei achuza*, he retains it entirely at *yovel*. This underscores the real *chiddush* of their position: it is not necessary that the son would have inherited the entire field himself. Even if he was merely one of several heirs, his status as an heir-in-line is sufficient to designate him as the owner for purposes of *sdei achuza*.

### Kinyan Peiros Debate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֵימָא בְּהָא קָא מִיפַּלְגִי, דְּרַבִּי מֵאִיר סָבַר: קִנְיַן פֵּירוֹת כְּקִנְיַן הַגּוּף דָּמֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Let us say** that **they disagree about this: That Rabbi Meir,** who requires a derivation from the verse to teach the *halakha* that if the son consecrates the field after his father’s death it is not treated as a purchased field, **holds** that **acquisition** of an item for **its produce is tantamount** to **acquisition of the item itself.** Accordingly, as the son owned the plot as a purchased field prior to his father’s death, his ownership remained unchanged when his father died. Therefore, the verse is required to teach that upon the father’s death the field’s status changes to that of an ancestral field.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יְהוּדָה וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן סָבְרִי: קִנְיַן פֵּירוֹת לָאו כְּקִנְיַן הַגּוּף דָּמֵי.
> **Translation:** In contrast, let us say that **Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon hold** that **acquisition** of an item for **its produce is not tantamount** to **acquisition of the item itself.** Accordingly, his ownership of the field became absolute only upon his father’s death. Consequently, there is no need for a derivation from the verse for such a case, and the derivation is therefore applied to the case where the son consecrated the field before his father’s death.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: בְּעָלְמָא לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה קִנְיַן פֵּירוֹת כְּקִנְיַן הַגּוּף דָּמֵי,
> **Translation:** **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said: In general, according to Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Yehuda, acquisition** of an item for **its produce is tantamount** to **acquisition of the item itself.** Consequently, they also require the derivation from the verse to teach that if the father died before the son consecrated the field, it is treated as an ancestral field and not as a purchased field.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָכָא קְרָא אַשְׁכֻּחַ וּדְרֻשׁי: לִכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא ״אִם מִשְּׂדֵה מִקְנָתוֹ אֲשֶׁר לֹא שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּתוֹ״, אִי נָמֵי: ״אֲשֶׁר לֹא שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה״, מַאי ״מִשְּׂדֵה אֲחֻזָּתוֹ״? שָׂדֶה שֶׁאֵינָהּ רְאוּיָה לִהְיוֹת שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה, יָצְתָה זוֹ שֶׁרְאוּיָה לִהְיוֹת שְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה.
> **Translation:** **But here,** with regard to their opinion that even if the son consecrated the field before his father’s death it is considered an ancestral field, this is because they **found** another allusion in the **verse and interpreted** it as follows: If the verse meant to indicate only the *halakha* that Rabbi Meir derives from it, **let the Merciful One write:** And **if** he will consecrate unto the Lord **a field that he has bought, which is not his ancestral field, or: Which is not an ancestral field. What** is the meaning of the extra word “of” in the phrase: “Which is not **of his ancestral field”** (Leviticus 27:22)? It teaches that only **a field that is not due to become** his **ancestral field** is treated like a purchased field, thereby **excluding this** field, **which is due to become** his **ancestral field.**

The *gemara* proposes that the underlying dispute between *Rabbi Meir* and *Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon* may reflect the broader legal debate over *kinyan peiros k'kinyan haguf*: whether ownership of produce alone is legally equivalent to full ownership of the corpus. The relevance here is as follows. When the son purchases the field from his father in a period when *yovel* is operative, the purchase is effectively a lease for the remaining years until *yovel* — the son does not acquire outright title, only the right to work the land and benefit from its produce. This is *kinyan peiros*. Now, is that functionally equivalent to owning the land itself?

If *Rabbi Meir* holds *kinyan peiros k'kinyan haguf dami* — that the produce-lease is treated as full ownership — then even before the father dies, the son already has a status equivalent to full title. His first relationship to the land was therefore as a full owner, and when he sanctifies it, you might classify it as a *sdei miknah* based on that full-title-equivalent acquisition. A verse is then needed even for the simpler case — father dies, then son sanctifies — because without the verse one might have said that the prior purchase-as-title precludes *sdei achuza* status.

*Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon*, by contrast, would hold *kinyan peiros lav k'kinyan haguf dami* — the lease-purchase is not equivalent to full title. The son never had real title until his father died; at that point he first acquires genuine ownership, and any subsequent sanctification is obviously that of an inherited field. No verse is needed for the simple case. The verse's work is reserved for the harder case: where the son sanctified the field while still holding only his lease — while the father still lived — and the father died afterward. Only in that case does one need a scriptural basis to say we treat it as *sdei achuza*.

### Hermeneutic Analysis

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַכֹּהֲנִים וְהַלְוִיִּם מַקְדִּישִׁין לְעוֹלָם וְכוּ׳. בִּשְׁלָמָא גּוֹאֲלִין אִצְטְרִיךְ, לְאַפּוֹקֵי מִדְּיִשְׂרָאֵל דְּלָא פָּרְקִי אֶלָּא עַד יוֹבֵל, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן דְּכֹהֲנִים וּלְוִיִּם גּוֹאֲלִין לְעוֹלָם. אֶלָּא מַקְדִּישִׁין, מַאי אִירְיָא כֹּהֲנִים וּלְוִיִּם? אֲפִילּוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל נָמֵי!
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **The priests and the Levites may always consecrate** their ancestral fields and may always redeem their ancestral fields, both before the Jubilee Year and after the Jubilee Year. The Gemara asks: **Granted,** the mishna’s statement that they **may** always **redeem** their ancestral fields **was necessary,** in order **to exclude** them **from** the *halakha* that applies to the ancestral field **of an Israelite, which may be redeemed only until** the **Jubilee** Year, and if not redeemed by then, it is given to the priests. In contrast, the mishna **teaches us that priests and Levites may always redeem** their fields. **But** with regard to the statement that they **may** always **consecrate** their fields, **why** does the mishna **specifically** mention **priests and Levites? Even an Israelite** may always consecrate his field, both before and after the Jubilee Year.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי תֵּימָא בִּשְׁנַת הַיּוֹבֵל עַצְמָהּ, הָנִיחָא לִשְׁמוּאֵל דְּאָמַר: ״בִּשְׁנַת הַיּוֹבֵל עַצְמָהּ לָא קָדְשָׁה״, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן דְּכֹהֲנִים וּלְוִיִּם מַקְדִּישִׁין לְעוֹלָם, אֶלָּא לְרַב, מַאי אִירְיָא כֹּהֲנִים וּלְוִיִּם? אֲפִילּוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל נָמֵי!
> **Translation:** The Gemara continues: **And if you would say** that the mishna means that priests and Levites may consecrate their fields **during the Jubilee Year itself,** whereas an Israelite may not, **this works out well according to** the opinion of **Shmuel, who says** (24a) that if an Israelite consecrated his field **during the Jubilee Year itself it is not consecrated.** Accordingly, the mishna **teaches us that priests and Levites may always consecrate** their fields, even in the Jubilee Year itself. **But according to** the opinion of **Rav, why** does the mishna **specifically** mention **priests and Levites? Even an Israelite** may consecrate his field during the Jubilee.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְטַעְמָיךְ, בֵּין לִפְנֵי הַיּוֹבֵל בֵּין לְאַחַר הַיּוֹבֵל לְמָה לִי? אֶלָּא, אַיְּידֵי דִּתְנָא רֵישָׁא ״לִפְנֵי הַיּוֹבֵל וּלְאַחַר הַיּוֹבֵל״, תְּנָא סֵיפָא נָמֵי ״בֵּין לְאַחַר הַיּוֹבֵל בֵּין לִפְנֵי הַיּוֹבֵל״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **And according to your reasoning,** one may similarly ask: **Why do I** need the statement that priests and Levites may consecrate their fields **both before the Jubilee** Year **and after the Jubilee** Year? Doesn’t the mishna already state that they may always consecrate their fields? **Rather, since** the *tanna* **taught** in the **first** mishna in this chapter (24a) that Israelites may not consecrate their fields less than two years **before the Jubilee and** they may not redeem them less than one year **after the Jubilee,** the *tanna* **also taught** in the **latter** mishna that priests and Levites may consecrate their fields **both after the Jubilee and before the Jubilee,** despite the fact that there is no novelty in this statement.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַיְּידֵי דִּתְנָא רֵישָׁא ״אֵין מַקְדִּישִׁין וְלֹא גּוֹאֲלִין״, תְּנָא נָמֵי סֵיפָא ״מַקְדִּישִׁין וְגוֹאֲלִין״.
> **Translation:** **And** similarly, **since** the *tanna* **taught** in the **first** mishna that Israelites **may neither consecrate** their fields less than two years before the Jubilee **nor redeem** them less than one year after the Jubilee, the *tanna* **also taught** in the **latter** mishna that priests and Levites **may** always **consecrate and redeem** their fields, despite the fact that there is no novelty with regard to consecrating in this statement either, as Israelites may also consecrate their fields both after the Jubilee and before the Jubilee.

*Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak* rejects this framework. In truth, he says, *Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon* may agree that *kinyan peiros k'kinyan haguf dami* in other contexts. The difference here is purely hermeneutic. The verse could have been written simply: *im mi-sdei miknato asher lo sdei achuza* — "if from a purchased field that is not an inherited field." That formulation alone would have excluded the case of father-dies-then-sanctifies, since in that scenario the field is also an inherited field. What is the additional mem in the phrase *mi-sdei achuzato* — "from his inherited field" — teaching? It is teaching *sdei she-einah re'uya lihyot sdei achuza* — not merely a field that is not currently inherited, but a field that is not even in line to become inherited. The additional mem pushes us to the further case: purchased, then sanctified, then father dies. *Mai mi-sdei achuzato* — what is the purpose of the word *mi-*? To exclude a field that, though purchased at the time of sanctification, was in line to become an inherited field. And that is precisely this case.

The *gemara* concludes, then, that the debate between *Rabbi Meir* and *Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon* is driven by hermeneutics — by which case each sage requires the verse to address — rather than by a direct consequence of the *kinyan peiros* debate. What is notable here is that *Rabbi Yehuda* and *Rabbi Shimon*, left to their own devices, would have said that a verse is needed even for the simpler case, precisely because a lease-purchase is like a title and so the field might be classified as a *sdei miknah*. It is the verse's own formulation — the extra mem of *mi-sdei achuzato* — that pushes them to reserve the verse for the more extreme case instead. This is a genuinely hermeneutic result: the sages are not choosing which case the verse addresses on the basis of their legal intuitions; the verse's wording makes that choice for them.

The mishnah closes this section with a statement about purchased fields generally: *sdei miknah einah yotzah la-kohanim ba-Yovel* — a sanctified purchased field does not go out to the *kohanim* at *yovel*, but returns to its original owner. The reason given is *she-ein adam makdish davar she-eino shelo* — a person cannot sanctify something that is not fully his. Since a purchase in the *yovel* era is really only a lease for the years remaining until *yovel*, the buyer never owned the land outright. He cannot therefore cause it to leave the possession of the original owner. At *yovel*, it reverts.

The mishnah then states: *ha-kohanim ve-ha-Levi'im makdishim le-olam ve-go'alim le-olam* — the *kohanim* and *Levi'im* may sanctify and redeem at all times, both before and after *yovel*. This is derived from the verse in *Behar* concerning the cities of the *Levi'im*: *ge'ulat olam tihyeh la-Levi'im* — permanent redemption rights belong to the *Levi'im*. The *gemara* will explore what this means in the context of fields. The *gemara* notes: *bishlama go'alin itztarich* — the ruling that they may redeem is clearly necessary, *le-afukei mi-Yisrael de-lo parkei*, to exclude an ordinary Israelite who does not have that ongoing redemption right. The precise contours of what *go'alin le'olam* entails — whether it refers to redeeming from *hekdesh* directly, or whether it also allows the *kohen* or *Levi* to force a repurchase from a third-party buyer, and whether the *kohen* who sanctified retains priority over other *kohanim* — are left as open questions by the *gemara*.

## Redemption Without Jub.



### No Jubilee Effects

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֲדַרַן עֲלָךְ אֵין מַקְדִּישִׁין.
> **Translation:** 

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ הַמַּקְדִּישׁ אֶת שָׂדֵהוּ בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁאֵין הַיּוֹבֵל, אוֹמֵר לוֹ: ״פְּתַח אַתָּה רִאשׁוֹן״, שֶׁהַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין חוֹמֶשׁ, וְכׇל אָדָם אֵין נוֹתְנִין חוֹמֶשׁ.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: In the case of **one who consecrates his** ancestral **field during a period when the Jubilee** Year **is not** observed, and therefore the field is not redeemed according to a fixed rate of fifty shekels per *beit kor* but according to its value, when the treasurer announces the sale of the field **he says to** the owner: **You open** the bidding **first;** how much do you offer for its redemption? This method is advantageous for the Temple treasury, **as the owner gives** an additional payment of **one-fifth** of the value of the field, **and every** other **person does not give** an additional **one-fifth** payment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַעֲשֶׂה בְּאֶחָד שֶׁהִקְדִּישׁ שָׂדֵהוּ מִפְּנֵי רָעָתָהּ, אָמְרוּ לוֹ: פְּתַח אַתָּה רִאשׁוֹן. אָמַר: הֲרֵי הִיא שֶׁלִּי בְּאִיסָּר. אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹסֵי: לֹא אָמַר זֶה ״בְּאִיסָּר״, אֶלָּא ״בְּבֵיצָה״, שֶׁהֶקְדֵּשׁ נִפְדֶּה בְּכֶסֶף וּבְשָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף, אָמַר לוֹ: ״הִגַּעְתִּיךָ״, נִמְצָא מַפְסִיד אִיסָּר וְשָׂדֵהוּ לְפָנָיו.
> **Translation:** There was **an incident involving one who consecrated his field due to its inferior quality.** The treasurers **said to him: You open** the bidding **first. He said:** It is **hereby mine for an *issar*,** a small sum. **Rabbi Yosei says: That** person **did not say** he would purchase it **for an *issar*; rather,** he said he would purchase it **for an egg, as consecrated** items **may be redeemed with money or with the equivalent** value of **money.** The treasurer **said to him:** The field **has come into your** possession based on your bid. As **a result, he loses an *issar* and his field** remains **before him** in his possession.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ הַמַּקְדִּישׁ שָׂדֵהוּ בְּשָׁעָה כּוּ׳. אוֹמְרִין? וְהָתַנְיָא כּוֹפִין! מַאי ״אוֹמְרִין״ נָמֵי? כּוֹפִין. וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: מֵעִיקָּרָא אוֹמְרִין, אִי צָאֵית — צָאֵית, וְאִי לָא — כּוֹפִין.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The mishna teaches that with regard to **one who consecrates his** ancestral **field during a period** when the Jubilee Year is not observed, the treasurer says to him: You open the bidding first. The Gemara asks: Why does the treasurer merely **say** this to him? **But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita* that the treasurer **forces** him to open the bidding? The Gemara responds: **What is** the meaning of the phrase: The treasurer **says** to him? It means that the treasurer **forces** him to open the bidding. **And if you wish, say** instead that **initially** the treasurer **says** to him that he should open the bidding; **if he complies, he complies; and if not,** the treasurer **forces** him to comply.

When *yovel* is not operative, the fixed-rate valuation chart of the Torah — fifty *shekel* for a given acreage for the full cycle — no longer applies, since that rate is calibrated to the number of years remaining until *yovel*. With no *yovel*, the framework collapses and market value takes its place. The question then becomes how to determine that market value.

When *yovel* is operative, two powerful forces shape the redemption of a sanctified field: the fixed rate locks in the price, and *yovel* itself functions as a deadline — if the owner does not redeem by *yovel*, the field transfers to the *kohanim*. When *yovel* is not operative, neither force applies. The owner retains an indefinite right to redeem the field as long as it remains in *hekdesh*'s possession. If *hekdesh* sells it to a third party, the owner must negotiate with that buyer — there is no *yovel* to force a reversion. This is, as the *gemara* puts it, essentially how we treat property today: if one sanctifies one's field, one may attempt to buy it back before anyone else does, and if *hekdesh* has already sold it to another, one must deal with that buyer directly. The one remaining distinctively Torah-law feature is that if the owner wishes to repurchase from *hekdesh*, he must add a *chomesh* — a fifth. That obligation persists even when *yovel* is not in force.

One might ask: if there is no *yovel*, does the category of *sdei achuza* even apply? The answer is no — without *yovel*, there is no *sdei achuza* framework. Sanctifying a field when *yovel* is not operative is effectively treated as a standard dedication of movable property, except for the *chomesh* obligation.

The *Tosafot* make an important point about the historical setting of this mishnah. If there is no *Beit HaMikdash* at all, then while one can technically sanctify property — since sanctity is a metaphysical status and the Torah's categories do not disappear merely because there is no institution to collect — *Tosafot* notes that *hekdesh* in that case has no practical financial interest, and the entire mechanism of redemption reduces to a purely symbolic act. One would simply redeem on a *perutah* to remove the *kedushah*. There would be no bidding war. The mishnah's scenario — with its competitive auction and financial stakes — therefore must describe a period when there is a *Beit HaMikdash* but no *yovel*. That is precisely the period of the Second Temple, during which *yovel* did not apply because the majority of the Jewish people were not settled on the Land in accordance with their tribal portions.

### Owner's First Bid Right

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁהַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין חוֹמֶשׁ וְכוּ׳. מַאי אִירְיָא שֶׁהַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין חוֹמֶשׁ? תִּיפּוֹק לֵיהּ דְּאַיְּידֵי דַּחֲבִיבָה עֲלֵיהּ טָפֵי וּפָרֵיק לַהּ! וְעוֹד, מִצְוַת גְּאוּלָּה בָּאָדוֹן!
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches that the one who consecrated the field opens the bidding, **as the owner gives** an additional **one-fifth** payment whereas others do not give an additional one-fifth payment. The Gemara asks: **Why** does the mishna state that the reason he opens the bidding is **specifically** because **the owner gives** an additional **one-fifth? Let** the *tanna* **derive** it from the reason **that since** the field **is dear to** the owner **he** would **increase** his bid **and redeem it** for a higher price. **And furthermore,** doesn’t the **mitzva of redemption** begin **with the owner** of the field by Torah law?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חֲדָא וְעוֹד קָאָמַר, חֲדָא: דְּאַיְּידֵי דַּחֲבִיבָה עֲלֵיהּ טָפֵי וּפָרֵיק לַהּ, וְעוֹד: מִצְוַת גְּאוּלָּה בָּאָדוֹן הִיא, וְעוֹד: שֶׁהַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין חוֹמֶשׁ כּוּ׳.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: The *tanna* **states one** reason **and** there are **another** two reasons: **One** reason is **that since it is dear to him he** would **increase** his bid **and redeem it** for a higher price; **another** reason is that the **mitzva of redemption** begins **with the owner; and another** reason is that **the owner gives** an additional **one-fifth** payment and every other person does not give an additional one-fifth payment.

The mishnah establishes: *omer lo petach atah rishon* — we say to the owner, make the first offer. The rationale is that since the owner must add a *chomesh* to whatever he bids, he will ultimately contribute more to the Temple treasury than any other bidder at the same base price. By inviting him to enter the bidding first, *hekdesh* maximizes the likelihood of receiving his augmented contribution.

In practice, a savvy owner will discount his bid to account for the eventual *chomesh* — bidding less than he would otherwise be willing to pay, knowing the *chomesh* will bring it up to his true ceiling. This exposes him to being outbid. The dynamics are thus genuinely those of an auction, not a simple assessment. The *gemara* identifies two reasons to want the owner involved in the bidding. *Chada de-aydi de-chaviva lei tfei hu parik lei* — first, because the field is personally dear to him, he may be willing to pay more than a disinterested third party. *Ve-od mitzvat geulah ba-adon* — and further, the Torah itself establishes that the owner has the first right and obligation to redeem what he has sanctified; the bidding process gives that right procedural expression. Beyond both of these, there is the concrete financial consideration: *she-ha-be'alim notnim chomesh ve-chol adam einam notnim chomesh* — the owner adds a fifth while everyone else does not.

### Poor Quality Field Case

A striking episode is recorded in the mishnah. Someone sanctified a field *mipnei ra'atah* — on account of its poor quality. The field was so unproductive that it was not worth the expense of working it. By sanctifying it, the person made a gesture of piety at essentially no personal cost. When told *petach atah rishon* — make the first offer — he offered a trivial amount. *Rabbi Yose* says that the amount he offered was the value of an egg — *harei sheli be-beitzah* — on the basis that *hekdesh nifdeh be-kesef u-ve-shaveh kesef*, that *hekdesh* may be redeemed with monetary equivalents, not only with coin. The court accepted his offer: *higatanu* — deal. And the consequence: *nimtza mafsid et sadehu lefanav* — he wound up paying for his trivial field and being stuck with it anyway.

The point of the episode is dual. First, it confirms that redemption is a bidding process and not a unilateral assessment by *hekdesh*. Second, and more significantly, it establishes that the court does not merely invite the owner to bid — it pressures him to bid. *Hekdesh* must not become a dumping ground for worthless property.

### Chomesh Requirement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַעֲשֶׂה בְּאֶחָד שֶׁהִקְדִּישׁ שָׂדֵהוּ וְכוּ׳. לֵימָא בְּהָא קָמִיפַּלְגִי, דְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי סָבַר: שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף כְּכֶסֶף, וְרַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף אֵינוֹ כְּכֶסֶף.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: There was **an incident involving one who consecrated his field,** which was causing him a loss due to its low quality. The treasurers said to him: You open the bidding first. He said: It is hereby mine for an *issar*. Rabbi Yosei says: That person did not say he would purchase it for an *issar*; rather, he said he would purchase it for an egg, as consecrated items may be redeemed with money or with the equivalent value of money. The Gemara suggests: **Let us say they disagree about this, that Rabbi Yosei holds** that the **equivalent** value of **money** is the same **as money, and the Rabbis hold** that the **equivalent** value of **money** is **not** the same **as money.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא קַיְימָא לַן, דְּשָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף כְּכֶסֶף! דְּכוּלֵּי עָלְמָא שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף כְּכֶסֶף, וְהָכָא בְּפוֹדִין בְּדָבָר שֶׁאֵין בְּחוּמְשׁוֹ שָׁוֶה פְּרוּטָה קָמִיפַּלְגִי. תַּנָּא קַמָּא סָבַר: אִיסָּר, דְּאִיכָּא בְּחוּמְשׁוֹ שָׁוֶה פְּרוּטָה — פָּרְקִינַן, וְרַבִּי יוֹסֵי: כְּבֵיצָה נָמֵי פָּרְקִינַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But don’t we maintain** as an accepted principle **that** the **equivalent** value of **money** is the same **as money?** The Gemara answers: **Everyone agrees** that the **equivalent** value of **money** is the same **as money, and here they disagree with regard to** whether **one may redeem with an item one-fifth of whose** value is **not worth one *peruta*. The first *tanna* holds** that **one may redeem** with **an *issar*,** which is worth eight *perutot*, **as one-fifth of its** value is greater than **the value of one *peruta*, and Rabbi Yosei** holds that **one may even redeem** with an item **such as an egg,** despite the fact that one-fifth of its value is not worth one *peruta*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לוֹ: ״הִגַּעְתִּיךָ״, נִמְצָא מַפְסִיד אִיסָּר וְשָׂדֵהוּ לְפָנָיו. סְתָמָא כְּרַבָּנַן.
> **Translation:** The mishna relates that after the owner made his bid the treasurer **said to him:** The field **has come into your** possession based on your bid. As **a result, he loses an *issar* and his field** remains **before him** in his possession. The Gemara notes: This statement, whose source is **unspecified,** is **in accordance with** the opinion of **the Rabbis,** who hold that in the incident in the mishna, the man redeemed the field with an *issar*.

The *gemara* explores the precise mechanism of the owner's obligation to add a *chomesh*. The *tanna kamma* of the case holds that the owner must bid at least an *issar*, because an *issar* contains at least four or more *perutot*, meaning that when the *chomesh* — which is technically one quarter of the principal, such that the principal becomes four-fifths of the total — is calculated on an *issar*, the result is at least a *perutah*. One cannot add a *chomesh* of less than a *perutah*, since that is the minimum denomination for a valid halachic transaction. *Rabbi Yose*, however, holds that even if the bid is only a *beitzah* — an egg — whose fifth would fall below a *perutah*, it is still valid: *hekdesh nifdeh be-kesef u-ve-shaveh kesef*, even a non-monetary equivalent of minimal value suffices to redeem *hekdesh*. The *gemara* confirms that the dispute cannot concern whether equivalents are acceptable in principle — that is agreed upon by all. The debate is specifically whether one may redeem *hekdesh* when the *chomesh* that results would be less than a *perutah*.

The case therefore works as follows: *stama de-rabbanan de-amar ke-issar* — the anonymous rabbinic position requires at least an *issar* to ensure a valid *chomesh*. *Rabbi Yose* permits even a *beitzah*. In either case, if the owner makes even that minimal offer and the court accepts it, *nimtza mafsid* — he is left having paid something trivial and having retrieved his worthless field. The *gemara* confirms that the court does not merely suggest: *kofin* — they compel him. Or more precisely, per one reading, *ibe'it eima me-ikara omrim* — if you prefer, say that first they suggest, and if he declines, *ve-ilo kofin* — they coerce. The policy is clear: *hekdesh* refuses to absorb property it cannot use, and the obligation falls back on the owner.

A separate question the *gemara* addresses is why the owner is specifically singled out to bid first, given that the *chomesh* premium alone might not seem a sufficient explanation. The *gemara* offers two reasons that operate together — *chada ve'od ka'amar*, one reason and an additional reason. First, *chada de'aydi dechaviva lei tfei hu parik lei* — because the field is dear to him, he is likely to bid more than a stranger would, since personal attachment inflates what one is willing to pay. Second, *mitzvat geulah ba'adon* — there is an independent Torah obligation for the owner to be the first to redeem his own sanctified property; the bidding protocol reflects not only financial strategy but the fulfillment of that priority. And beyond both of these, *ve'od shebe'alim notnim chamesh* — whatever the owner bids, he must add a fifth on top of it, making his participation additionally valuable to *hekdesh* in purely monetary terms. These three considerations compound one another, and together they explain why pressuring the owner to open the bidding is not merely procedural but carries real substantive weight.

## Auction Mechanics



### Bids Set Price

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ אָמַר אֶחָד: ״הֲרֵי הִיא שֶׁלִּי בְּעֶשֶׂר סְלָעִים״, וְאֶחָד אוֹמֵר: ״בְּעֶשְׂרִים״, וְאֶחָד אוֹמֵר: ״בִּשְׁלֹשִׁים״, וְאֶחָד אוֹמֵר: ״בְּאַרְבָּעִים״, וְאֶחָד אוֹמֵר: ״בַּחֲמִשִּׁים״. חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל חֲמִשִּׁים — מְמַשְׁכְּנִין מִנְּכָסָיו עַד עֶשֶׂר. חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל אַרְבָּעִים — מְמַשְׁכְּנִין מִנְּכָסָיו עַד עֶשֶׂר.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: If **one said:** The field is **hereby mine for ten *sela*, and one** other person **said:** It is mine **for twenty, and one said for thirty, and one said for forty, and one said for fifty;** and then the one who bid **fifty reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela* and the field is redeemed by the one who bid forty. This ensures that the Temple treasury does not lose. If the one who bid **forty** *sela* subsequently **reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela* and the field is redeemed by the one who bid thirty.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל שְׁלֹשִׁים — מְמַשְׁכְּנִין מִנְּכָסָיו עַד עֶשֶׂר, חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל עֶשְׂרִים — מְמַשְׁכְּנִין מִנְּכָסָיו עַד עֶשֶׂר, חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל עֶשֶׂר — מוֹכְרִין אוֹתוֹ בְּשׇׁוְויוֹ, וְנִפְרָעִין מִשֶּׁל עֶשֶׂר אֶת הַמּוֹתָר.
> **Translation:** If the one who bid **thirty** subsequently **reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela* and the field is redeemed by the one who bid twenty. If the one who bid **twenty reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela* and it is redeemed by the one who bid ten. If the one who bid **ten reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **sells** the field **at its value and collects the remainder from** the property **of the one** who bid **ten,** to complete the sum of ten *sela*.

The mishnah now describes the full auction. One bidder offers ten *sela*, another twenty, another thirty, another forty, another fifty. The field is awarded to the highest bidder. But what happens if the winning bidder backs out? The *gemara*'s insight, and that of the mishnah itself, is that each bid functions not merely as an offer but as a *designation of value*. When the fifty-*shekel* bidder makes his offer, he has, in some objective sense, established the value of the field at fifty. If he then backs out, *hekdesh* can only recover forty from the next bidder. The fifty-*shekel* bidder has caused a loss of ten *shekel* and must make up that difference. *Memashkenin minichasav ad eser* — the court seizes assets to collect the ten-*shekel* gap.

### Backing Out Penalties

The principle applies uniformly at every level. If the forty-*shekel* bidder backs out, he pays ten — the difference between his bid and the thirty-*shekel* bid below him. If the thirty-*shekel* bidder backs out, he pays ten. If the twenty-*shekel* bidder backs out, he pays ten. Each backing-out causes a loss of ten, and each is responsible for exactly that gap. This structure serves as a powerful deterrent against frivolous or strategic bidding — entering the auction solely to drive up the price for others. Every bid is binding, and the bidder remains liable for the gap his withdrawal creates even if others above him have already withdrawn and been assessed their own penalties.

### Initial Bidder Backs Out

The case of the initial ten-*shekel* bidder who backs out is handled differently. Since there is no lower bid to serve as the floor, the court sells the field at market value — *mochrin oto be-shavyo* — and the ten-*shekel* bidder pays the difference between market value and ten. *Ve-nifra'in mi-shel eser et ha-motar* — the surplus above ten is collected from him.

This mechanism is worth pausing on. The auction format is always preferred when it can generate more revenue for *hekdesh*, but selling at market value is available as a fallback precisely for situations like this one. What the penalty ultimately means is that the ten-*shekel* bidder is on the hook for the entire gap between his bid and whatever the market bears — which could be substantial. That is a serious deterrent, but it is not aimed at the sincere bidder; someone genuinely committed to their price has nothing to fear. The real target is the bidder who was never interested in buying at all and entered only to drive up the price for others. The rule makes clear that every bid is binding and that liability can reach back to any participant in the chain, regardless of what others above him have done.

## Owner vs. Other Bidders



### Owner's Fifth Advantage

The mishnah turns to competition between the owner and other bidders. When the owner and another bidder both offer twenty, *ha-be'alim kodmim mipnei she-hem mosifim chomesh* — the owner takes precedence because he will add a fifth. His effective payment is twenty-five, whereas the other bidder's payment is twenty. *Hekdesh* receives more from the owner.

### Non-Owner Bids Higher

Things become more complex when a non-owner bids higher than the owner. Say the owner bids twenty and another bidder says twenty-one. The non-owner's higher bid designates the value at twenty-one, yet the owner still wins the auction since his twenty plus his *chomesh* of five yields twenty-five, which is more than twenty-one. But because the non-owner's bid has set the price at twenty-one, the owner does not pay based on his own initial offer of twenty. He pays twenty-one — the price set by the competing bid — and then adds his *chomesh* calculated on his original bid of twenty, yielding a total of twenty-six. *Ha-be'alim notnim esrim ve-shesh.*

This is a striking consequence. The owner entered the auction prepared to pay twenty plus the *chomesh* — twenty-five total. Because another bidder said twenty-one, the owner is now committed to twenty-six. He wins, but at a higher cost than he anticipated. The mechanism at work here is not simply that of a conventional auction, where an offer is meaningless until accepted. Rather, as we have already seen, a bid in this context actively designates the price. So two things are simultaneously true: the owner wins the auction — because his ultimate payout would have been higher — and the non-owner's bid fixes the floor price, binding the owner to it even though it exceeds what he himself offered. The owner pays the non-owner's higher designated price, and then adds his *chomesh* on top of his own original bid. The practical implication is that competing bids can put the owner at a real disadvantage: the higher a non-owner drives the price, the more the owner is forced to pay, even as the owner remains the winner of the auction. The rule is clear before one enters the auction: all bids are binding, bids set prices, and the owner's *chomesh* is calculated on his own bid regardless of what price has been set by another bidder's offer.

### Equivalence Point

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַבְּעָלִים אוֹמְרִים בְּעֶשְׂרִים, וְכׇל אָדָם אוֹמְרִים בְּעֶשְׂרִים — הַבְּעָלִים קוֹדְמִין, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהֵם מוֹסִיפִין חוֹמֶשׁ. אָמַר אֶחָד: ״הֲרֵי הִיא שֶׁלִּי בְּעֶשְׂרִים וְאַחַת״ —
> **Translation:** If **the owner says** he will pay **twenty** *sela* **and any** other **person says** he will pay **twenty** *sela*, the offer of **the owner takes precedence, due to** the fact **that** he **adds one-fifth.** If the owner says he will pay twenty *sela* and **one** other person **said:** The field **is hereby mine for** a payment of **twenty-one** *sela*,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין עֶשְׂרִים וָשֵׁשׁ, ״בְּעֶשְׂרִים וּשְׁתַּיִם״ — הַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין עֶשְׂרִים וָשֶׁבַע,
> **Translation:** **the owner gives twenty-six** *sela* and takes the field. He pays the twenty that he initially offered; plus five *sela*, which is one-fifth of the total future sum, i.e., one-quarter of his initial offer. In addition, he adds one *sela*, the difference between his initial offer and that of the other person, so that the Temple treasury will not receive less than the twenty-one *sela* offer proposed by the other person. If the owner says he will pay twenty *sela* and another person said: The field is hereby mine for a payment **of twenty-two** *sela*, **the owner gives twenty-seven** *sela* and takes the field.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״בְּעֶשְׂרִים וְשָׁלֹשׁ״ — הַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין עֶשְׂרִים וּשְׁמוֹנֶה, ״בְּעֶשְׂרִים וְאַרְבַּע״ — הַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין עֶשְׂרִים וָתֵשַׁע, ״בְּעֶשְׂרִים וְחָמֵשׁ״ — הַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין שְׁלֹשִׁים, שֶׁאֵין מוֹסִיפִין חוֹמֶשׁ עַל עִלּוּיוֹ שֶׁל זֶה.
> **Translation:** If the owner says he will pay twenty *sela* and another said: The field is hereby mine for a payment **of twenty-three** *sela*, **the owner gives twenty-eight** *sela* and takes the field. If the owner says he will pay twenty *sela* and another said: The field is hereby mine for a payment **of twenty-four** *sela*, **the owner gives twenty-nine** *sela* and takes the field. If the owner says he will pay twenty *sela* and another said: The field is hereby mine for a payment **of twenty-five** *sela*, **the owner gives thirty** *sela*, **as** the owner adds one-fifth only to the amount that he bid, and **does not add one-fifth to the addition of that** other person.

The mishnah traces through successive increments. At twenty-two, twenty-three, and twenty-four offered by the non-owner, the owner still wins — paying twenty-seven, twenty-eight, and twenty-nine respectively — since his twenty plus five still outpaces the non-owner's offer. When the non-owner reaches twenty-five, the owner's twenty plus five equals exactly twenty-five — a tie — and the owner still receives the field since the tie goes to him, with his total payment now thirty. At this point the mishnah notes: *she-ein mosifin chomesh al iluyo shel zeh* — the *chomesh* is calculated only on the owner's own bid, not on the non-owner's higher offer. Even though the competing bid has driven the price to twenty-five, the owner still pays a *chomesh* of only four — one fifth of his own bid of twenty — and not a *chomesh* of five calculated on the non-owner's twenty-five. The non-owner's bid sets the floor price the owner must meet, but it does not recalibrate the base on which the owner's penalty addition is assessed. Once the non-owner pushes the bidding to twenty-six, however, the dynamic shifts entirely — a question the next stage of the mishnah addresses directly.

### Owner's Final Bid Option

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אֶחָד: ״הֲרֵי שֶׁלִּי בְּעֶשְׂרִים וָשֵׁשׁ״, אִם רָצוּ הַבְּעָלִים לִיתֵּן שְׁלֹשִׁים וְאֶחָד וְדִינָר — הַבְּעָלִים קוֹדְמִין, וְאִם לָאו — אוֹמֵר לוֹ: ״הִגַּעְתִּיךָ״.
> **Translation:** If the owner said he will pay twenty *sela* and **one** other person **said:** The field is **hereby mine** for a payment **of twenty-six** *sela*, **if the owner wished to pay thirty-one** *sela* **and a dinar the owner takes precedence; and if not,** the treasurer **says to the other person:** The field **has come into your** possession based on your bid, as it is more than the Temple treasury can compel the owner to pay.

When another bidder reaches twenty-six, the owner's twenty plus five is now only twenty-five — strictly less than twenty-six — and the owner can no longer claim the field on the strength of his original bid alone. At this point the mishnah affords the owner an option: *im ratzu ha-be'alim notnim shloshim ve-echad ve-dinar* — if the owner wishes, he may submit a final offer of thirty-one and a fraction, which is equivalent to revising his base bid to approximately twenty-six, yielding a total that exceeds twenty-six. If he elects to do so, *ha-be'alim kodmim* — he takes the field. If not, *omer lo harei hi shelcha* — the field goes to the twenty-six bidder.

The mishnah's structure implies that outside of this final option, the auction is essentially a sealed-bid, single-round process — what one might call a silent auction. The owner is the one party who retains the right to a final counteroffer after the other bids are revealed.

## Auction Mechanics (II)



### Sequence vs Simultaneous

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: לֹא שָׁנוּ אֶלָּא שֶׁבֶּן אַרְבָּעִים עוֹמֵד בִּמְקוֹמוֹ.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The mishna teaches that if one said: The field is mine for ten *sela*, and another said for twenty, and another said thirty, and another said forty, and yet another said fifty; and then the one who bid fifty reneged on his offer, the treasurer repossesses from his property up to ten *sela*, and it is redeemed by the one who bid forty. In this manner, the Temple treasury does not lose. **Rav Ḥisda says:** The mishna **taught** that the treasurer repossesses ten *sela* from the one who initially offered fifty *sela* **only when the one who** offered **forty stands in his place** and intends to purchase the field, as the Temple treasury would have lost only ten *sela*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲבָל אֵין בֶּן אַרְבָּעִים עוֹמֵד בִּמְקוֹמוֹ, מְשַׁלְּשִׁין בֵּינֵיהֶן.
> **Translation:** **But** if **the one who** offered **forty does not stand in his place,** i.e., he too reneged on his offer, then the additional amount they had offered is **divided between** the one who initially offered fifty and the one who offered forty, as follows: The one who offered fifty pays all ten of the difference between fifty and forty, but he must also pay half of the extra ten that is the difference between the offer of forty and the previous offer of thirty. The reason is that the one who bid fifty agreed to that extra ten that raised the price from thirty to forty. In sum, the one who offered fifty pays fifteen, and the one who said forty pays five.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּנַן: חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל אַרְבָּעִים — מְמַשְׁכְּנִין עַד עֶשֶׂר. אַמַּאי? לִיתֵּן בַּר חַמְשִׁין בַּהֲדֵיהּ! דְּלֵיכָּא בֶּן חֲמִשִּׁים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises an objection to the statement of Rav Ḥisda from that which **we learned** in the mishna: If the one who bid **forty reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela*. The Gemara explains the objection: According to Rav Ḥisda, there is a difficulty: **Why** does the mishna rule that his property is repossessed by ten *sela*? **Let the one who** offered **fifty give together with him,** and therefore the one who offered forty should give only five. The Gemara responds: The mishna is referring to a case **where there is no one who** offered **fifty** *sela*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חָזַר בּוֹ בֶּן שְׁלֹשִׁים — מְמַשְׁכְּנִין מִנְּכָסָיו עַד עֶשֶׂר, אַמַּאי? וְלִיתֵּן דְּבֶן אַרְבָּעִים בַּהֲדֵיהּ! דְּלֵיכָּא בֶּן אַרְבָּעִים. חָזַר בּוֹ בֶּן עֶשְׂרִים — מְמַשְׁכְּנִין מִנְּכָסָיו עַד עֶשֶׂר, אַמַּאי? לִיתֵּן דְּבֶן שְׁלֹשִׁים בַּהֲדֵיהּ! דְּלֵיכָּא בֶּן שְׁלֹשִׁים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara further objects: The mishna also teaches that if the one who bid **thirty reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela*. But according to Rav Ḥisda, **why** is only his property repossessed? **Let** the one **who** offered **forty give together with him.** The Gemara again responds: The mishna is referring to a case **where there is no one who** offered **forty** *sela*. The Gemara persists: The mishna states that if the one who bid **twenty reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela*. According to Rav Ḥisda, **why** is only his property repossessed? **Let** the one **who** offered **thirty give together with him.** Once again, the Gemara responds: The mishna is referring to a case **where there is no one who** offered **thirty** *sela*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, אֵימָא סֵיפָא: חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל עֶשֶׂר, מוֹכְרִין אוֹתָהּ בְּשׇׁוְיָהּ וְנִפְרָעִין מִשֶּׁל עֶשֶׂר אֶת הַמּוֹתָר, לִיתֵּב דְּבֶן עֶשְׂרִים בַּהֲדֵיהּ! וְכִי תֵּימָא הָכָא נָמֵי דְּלֵיכָּא בֶּן עֶשְׂרִים, אִי הָכִי, ״נִפְרָעִין מִשֶּׁל עֲשָׂרָה״ — ״נִפְרָעִין מִמֶּנּוּ״ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If so,** then **say the latter clause** of the mishna: If the one who bid **ten reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **sells** the field **at its value and collects the remainder from** the property **of the one** who bid **ten,** to complete the sum of ten *sela*. Why is payment collected only from the one who offered ten? **Let the one who** offered **twenty give together with him. And if you would say that here too, there is no one who** offered **twenty** *sela*, then **if so,** why does the mishna state: And **collects from** the one who bid **ten?** The mishna **should have** stated simply: And **collects from him,** as there is no one else who submitted an offer.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: לָא קַשְׁיָא, כָּאן בְּבַת אַחַת, כָּאן בְּזֶה אַחַר זֶה. תַּנְיָא נָמֵי הָכִי: חָזְרוּ כּוּלָּן כְּאֶחָד — מְשַׁלְּשִׁין בֵּינֵיהֶם. וְהָא אֲנַן תְּנַן: מְמַשְׁכְּנִין מִנְּכָסָיו עַד עֶשֶׂר! אֶלָּא לָאו שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ כִּדְרַב חִסְדָּא, שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rav Ḥisda said:** This is **not difficult. Here,** when the potential loss incurred by the Temple treasury is divided, they reneged on their offers **simultaneously;** whereas **there,** in the mishna, where only one individual must cover the loss, two individuals reneged **one after the other.** The Gemara notes that **this is also taught** in a *baraita*: If **all of them reneged at once they divide** the potential loss incurred by the Temple treasury **between them.** Now **didn’t we learn** in the mishna: The treasurer **repossesses from his property up to ten** *sela*, without any mention of a division? **Rather,** must one **not conclude from** the *baraita* that the *halakha* is **in accordance with** the statement of **Rav Ḥisda,** who holds that when two individuals renege on their offers simultaneously they divide the loss between them? The Gemara comments: Indeed, **conclude from it** that this is the case.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיכָּא דְּרָמֵי לְהוּ מִירְמָא: תְּנַן: חָזַר בּוֹ שֶׁל עֶשֶׂר, מוֹכְרִין אוֹתָהּ בְּשׇׁוְיָהּ וְנִפְרָעִין מִשֶּׁל עֶשֶׂר אֶת הַמּוֹתָר, וְהָתַנְיָא: מְשַׁלְּשִׁין בֵּינֵיהֶם! אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: לָא קַשְׁיָא, כָּאן — בְּבַת אַחַת, כָּאן — בְּזֶה אַחַר זֶה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes that **there are** those **who raise** this *baraita* as a contradiction: **We learned** in the mishna that if the one **who** bid **ten reneged** on his offer, the treasurer **sells** the field **at its value and collects the remainder from** the property **of the one** who bid **ten. But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita* that **they divide** the potential loss to the Temple treasury **between them? Rav Ḥisda said:** This is **not difficult. Here** in the *baraita* they reneged on their offers **simultaneously,** whereas **there,** in the mishna, two individuals reneged **one after the other.**

*Rav Chisda* introduces a critical distinction. The mishnah states that when the fifty-*shekel* bidder backs out, he pays ten; when the forty-*shekel* bidder backs out, he pays ten; and so on down to the twenty-*shekel* bidder, each paying ten. *Rav Chisda* asks: why does the forty-*shekel* bidder pay the full ten between his bid and the thirty-*shekel* bid below him? If the fifty-*shekel* bidder had also backed out, should he not share responsibility for that gap?

*Rav Chisda*'s answer is *lo kashya — kan be-vat achat kan be-zeh achar zeh*: there is no contradiction, because the two scenarios are different. If the fifty-*shekel* bidder backed out first — sequentially — and was assessed his ten-*shekel* penalty and departed, the auction then proceeds as if he were never there. The forty-*shekel* bidder now stands as the high bidder and bears full responsibility for the gap between his bid and the next. His backing out therefore costs him the full ten between forty and thirty. But if both the fifty and forty-*shekel* bidders back out *simultaneously*, their responsibilities overlap. Both bear responsibility for the gap between forty and thirty, since either one of them, had he remained, would have covered that gap. In the zone of overlap, liability is divided equally — *mishalshin beineihem*. The fifty-*shekel* bidder thus pays ten for the exclusive gap between fifty and forty, and then five for his half of the shared gap between forty and thirty — fifteen in total. The forty-*shekel* bidder pays five for his half of the gap between forty and thirty.

The *gemara* initially attempts to read the mishnah as describing separate auctions, each with only one bidder at a given level, in order to avoid the question of shared liability. But this reading is forced and ultimately abandoned. The text *nifra'in mi-shel asarah* — "collected from the ten-*shekel* bidder" — implies there are others in the same auction, not that he is the sole participant. *Rav Ashi* confirms that the resolution is *Rav Chisda*'s: sequential withdrawal and simultaneous withdrawal are governed by different rules, and a *baraita* that states *mishalshin beineihem* is describing the simultaneous case while the mishnah's straight ten-*shekel* penalty describes sequential cases.

One might ask about an intermediate case: what if the forty-*shekel* bidder announced his withdrawal before the fifty-*shekel* bidder did, but both had effectively backed out by the time the dust settled? The *gemara* does not address this directly, but the logic of *Rav Chisda*'s framework suggests it may be a moot point in practice — so long as the fifty-*shekel* bidder is present and active, the auctioneer has no occasion to treat the forty-*shekel* bidder's words as consequential, and it is unclear that his premature withdrawal would even register as a binding act. The question is interesting to contemplate but may never arise in a properly conducted auction.

Having resolved how liability is apportioned when bidders back out, the *gemara* turns to a separate question arising from the mishnah's treatment of the owner's *chomesh* — namely, whether the fifth is truly the decisive factor in awarding the field, and whether that logic holds up against a parallel case in the laws of *ma'aser sheni*.

## Owner vs. Other Bidd (II)



### Ma'aser Sheni Comparison

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַבְּעָלִים אוֹמְרִים בְּעֶשְׂרִים וְכׇל אָדָם בְּעֶשְׂרִים וְכוּ׳. לְמֵימְרָא דְּחוֹמֶשׁ עֲדִיף? וּרְמִינְהוּ: בַּעַל הַבַּיִת אוֹמֵר בְּסֶלַע, וְאֶחָד אוֹמֵר בְּסֶלַע וְאִיסָּר — שֶׁל סֶלַע וְאִיסָּר קוֹדֵם, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁמּוֹסִיף עַל הַקֶּרֶן!
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: If **the owner says** he will pay **twenty** *sela* **and any** other **person says** he will pay **twenty** *sela*, the owner’s offer takes precedence because he adds one-fifth. The Gemara asks: Is this **to say that** an offer of the owner, which perforce includes an additional **one-fifth,** is **preferable** to a higher offer for the principal submitted by another? **And** the Gemara **raises a contradiction** from a mishna (*Ma’aser Sheni* 4:3): If one is unable to transport his second-tithe produce to Jerusalem to consume it there, the produce is redeemed and the redemption money is spent on food in Jerusalem. If **the owner** of second tithe **says** he will redeem it **for one *sela* and one** other **says** he will redeem it **for one *sela* and one *issar*,** the offer **of one *sela* and one *issar* takes precedence, because he adds to the principal.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכָא דְּחוּמְשָׁא רַוְוחָא דְּהֶקְדֵּשׁ הוּא — חוֹמֶשׁ עֲדִיף, הָתָם דְּחוּמְשָׁא רַוְוחָא דְּבַעַל הַבַּיִת — קַרְנָא תִּיפְרוֹק שַׁפִּיר, חוּמְשָׁא לָא אִיכְפַּת לַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Here, where the** additional **one-fifth is a gain of the Temple** treasury, a calculation that includes the **one-fifth** is **preferable.** By contrast, **there, where the** additional **one-fifth** belongs to **the owner,** i.e., the Temple treasury gains nothing from it, as the redemption money for second-tithe produce belongs to the owner and must be taken to Jerusalem for him to spend it there, **let the principal be redeemed** for **a proper** sum, and **we do not care** about the additional **one-fifth.**

The *gemara* raises a challenge from the laws of *ma'aser sheni*. In our mishnah, *hekdesh* gives the field to the owner when the owner and another bidder are tied at the same base amount — precisely because the owner's *chomesh* will result in a higher total payment to the treasury. The fifth, in other words, is treated as a decisive factor. The *gemara* then cites a *baraita* about redeeming *ma'aser sheni*: if the owner offers one *sela* and another person offers one *sela* and an *issar*, the other person's bid wins — *mipnei she-mosif al ha-keren* — because he has added more to the principal. The owner's *chomesh* is not taken into account as a tiebreaker; the higher principal wins outright.

The *gemara* resolves this by pointing to a fundamental structural difference. *Hacha de-chumsha rivcha de-hekdesh hu, chumash adif* — in our case of a sanctified field, the *chomesh* that the owner adds goes to *hekdesh*. *Hekdesh* has a direct financial interest in receiving the *chomesh*, and so naturally it factors the fifth into its calculation of who offers more. By contrast, with *ma'aser sheni*, *karna tefei parik shapir, chumsha lo ichpat lan* — the fifth that the owner adds to redeem his *ma'aser sheni* accrues only to himself; he takes both the redeemed value and the money he spent to Jerusalem to eat. The institution receiving the redemption money has no stake in whether the owner adds more or less. Therefore, in the *ma'aser sheni* context, only the principal matters, and a higher principal wins regardless of what *chomesh* the owner might add.
