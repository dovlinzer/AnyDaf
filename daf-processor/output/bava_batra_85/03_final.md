## Kinyan Kli Overview



### Kli as Chatzer

The sugya on daf 85a opens by continuing a discussion carried over from the final lines of 84b concerning *kinyan kli* — the act of acquisition effected by placing an object into the buyer's vessel. The underlying conceptual basis of this *kinyan* is not that the *kli* constitutes an independent mode of transfer, but rather that the buyer's vessel functions as a miniaturized *chatzer*: just as placing an object in someone's domain can effect acquisition, so too does placing it in his vessel, which stands as a surrogate for his personal domain. In the same way that placing goods on a buyer's donkey might effect acquisition, filling the buyer's bag or basket operates on an analogous principle.

### Kli in Reshut Hamocher

This framework immediately generates a sharp question. If the vessel functions as the buyer's *chatzer*, the mechanism depends on the vessel being meaningfully "his" domain at the relevant moment. This works cleanly when the vessel is located in a *simta* — a side alley — or in a *chatzer shel shneihem*, a space belonging to neither party exclusively, since in those settings the buyer has an uncontested right to have his vessel present. The problem intensifies when the vessel sits in the seller's own domain. The seller has, of course, permitted the buyer to set down his bag in the store while the goods are being loaded into it — so the buyer has some right to have the vessel there. Yet the store remains fundamentally the seller's domain, and the goods have not yet left the seller's sphere of control. Has a genuine transfer of possession occurred? This is the central question the *gemara* now pursues: does *kinyan kli* operate when the buyer's vessel is in *reshut hamocher*, the seller's domain?

## Kli in Reshut Harabim



[DAF:85a]
### Rav and Shmuel

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּלְיוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם קוֹנֶה לוֹ בְּכׇל מָקוֹם, חוּץ מֵרְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים. וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן לָקִישׁ דְּאָמְרִי תַּרְוַיְיהוּ: אֲפִילּוּ בִּרְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים.
> **Translation:** **A person’s vessels effect acquisition** of any item placed inside them **for him, in any place** in which they are situated, **except for the public domain. And Rabbi Yoḥanan and Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish both say: Even in the public domain,** one’s vessels effect acquisition of items placed in them.

The *gemara* opens the formal discussion at the top of 85a with a paired ruling attributed to Rav and Shmuel, who state together: *kelo shel adam koneh lo b'chol makom* — a person's vessel acquires on his behalf anywhere — *chutz mirshut harabim*, except in the public domain. The reason for this exclusion is that in *reshut harabim* one has no right to leave one's vessels on the ground; absent that entitlement, the vessel cannot function as a surrogate domain. Importantly, this formulation of Rav and Shmuel implies that *kinyan kli* would work even in *reshut hamocher*, since the seller does permit the buyer to place his bags in the store — a question the *gemara* will press later.

### Yochanan & Reish Lakish

Against this, Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish — the preeminent *Eretz Yisrael* authorities — state that *kinyan kli* works *afilu birshut harabim*, even in the public domain. On its face, this creates a direct dispute: Rav and Shmuel say it fails in *reshut harabim*; Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish say it succeeds even there. One might speculate whether this *Bavel*–*Eretz Yisrael* divergence reflects genuine differences in how *reshut harabim* functioned practically in those respective societies, though the *gemara* itself does not pursue that line.

### Rav Papa Reconciles

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: לָא פְּלִיגִי; כָּאן בִּרְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים, כָּאן בְּסִימְטָא. וְאַמַּאי קָרוּ לַהּ רְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים? שֶׁאֵין רְשׁוּת הַיָּחִיד.
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa said:** These *amora’im* **do not disagree: Here,** when Rav and Shmuel state that one’s vessel does not effect acquisition for him, they are speaking of a vessel placed **in the public domain; there,** when Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish state that his vessel effects acquisition on his behalf, they are referring to a vessel located **in an alleyway. And why do they call** an alleyway **the public domain?** The reason is **that** an alleyway is **not a private domain.**

Rav Papa attempts to dissolve the apparent conflict. He proposes that the two schools are not actually arguing: when Rav and Shmuel say *kinyan kli* does not work in *reshut harabim*, they mean a literal, full public thoroughfare. When Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish say it does work in *reshut harabim*, they do not mean it literally — they mean a *simta*, a semi-public alley, and they call it *reshut harabim* only in the loose sense of "not a private domain." On Rav Papa's reading, everyone agrees that in a true *reshut harabim* it does not work, and everyone agrees that in a *simta* it does.

### Gemara's Counter-Proof

The *gemara* questions whether this harmonization can be sustained. A *baraita* is cited in which Rabbi Abba reports in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: *kelo shel adam koneh lo b'chol makom sheyesh lo reshut lehanicho* — a person's vessel acquires for him wherever he has the right to place it. The *gemara* reasons from this formulation: if *kinyan kli* operates precisely where one has the *right* to leave one's vessel, then it should fail where one lacks that right — i.e., in *reshut harabim* — and this principle, stated in Rabbi Yochanan's own name, supports the Babylonian reading of his position rather than undermining it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכִי נָמֵי מִסְתַּבְּרָא, דְּאָמַר רַבִּי אֲבָהוּ אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: כִּלְיוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם קוֹנֶה לוֹ בְּכׇל מָקוֹם שֶׁיֵּשׁ לוֹ רְשׁוּת לְהַנִּיחוֹ. יֵשׁ לוֹ רְשׁוּת – אִין, אֵין לוֹ רְשׁוּת – לָא. שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara points out: **So, too, it is reasonable** to interpret Rabbi Yoḥanan’s statement in this manner, **as Rabbi Abbahu says** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: A person’s vessels effect acquisition for him in any place where he has permission to keep them.** It can be inferred from here: In a location where **he has permission** to keep them, **yes,** his vessels effect acquisition for him. But in a place where **he does not have permission** to keep his vessels, they do **not** effect acquisition for him, and one has permission to keep his vessels in an alleyway but not in the public domain. The Gemara affirms: **Conclude from** this statement that when Rabbi Yoḥanan referred to the public domain he meant an alleyway.

### No Kli in True RH


The *gemara* therefore concludes — *shema mina* — that *kinyan kli* does not work in a true *reshut harabim* because one lacks the right to deposit vessels there. The question of *hagbahah* is briefly noted as a separate matter: if a person is actually holding or carrying the bag rather than resting it on the ground, that operates as *hagbahah* — lifting — or perhaps as something the *acharonim* would call *kinyan yad*, since the object is directly on one's person. The underlying logic of *kinyan yad* is worth pausing over: according to some opinions, *kinyan chatzer* works only *mishum yad* — because one's *chatzer* is treated as an extension of one's person. If that is the theoretical basis, then acquisition should work with even greater force when an object is resting on the body itself, where one's control is most immediate and direct. This raises the question of whether a carried bag effects acquisition through *hagbahah* proper — which may require an intentional act of lifting to a certain height — or through this separate principle that what is on one's person is already within one's domain regardless of elevation. But a bag sitting on the ground in the public domain, unconnected to the buyer's body, cannot effect acquisition.

## Four Categories



### Measure Filling Rule

The *gemara* now introduces a *Tosefta* — introduced with the formula *ta shema* — that enumerates four categories (*arba middos*) relevant to commercial transactions. The first category concerns the measuring process itself. Imagine a buyer purchasing three cups of flour from a seller, who fills a measuring vessel to the three-cup mark. The *Tosefta* states: *ad shelo nismaila hamiddah lamocher* — before the measure is filled to the purchased amount, the goods belong to the seller; *mishenismaila hamiddah lalokeach* — once the measure is filled to that amount, they belong to the buyer.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע, אַרְבַּע מִדּוֹת בַּמּוֹכְרִין: עַד שֶׁלֹּא נִתְמַלְּאָה מִדָּה – לַמּוֹכֵר, מִשֶּׁנִּתְמַלְּאָה מִדָּה – לַלּוֹקֵחַ. בַּמֶּה דְּבָרִים אֲמוּרִים – בְּמִדָּה שֶׁאֵינָהּ שֶׁל שְׁנֵיהֶן, אֲבָל אִם הָיְתָה מִדָּה שֶׁל אֶחָד מֵהֶן – רִאשׁוֹן רִאשׁוֹן – קָנָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Come** and **hear** a difficulty from a *baraita*: There are **four cases with regard to sellers,** i.e., four methods through which merchandise is acquired. When the seller measures merchandise for the buyer, **before the measuring** vessel **has been filled** the merchandise in the vessel still belongs **to the seller** and he can change his mind and cancel the sale. **Once the measuring** vessel **has been filled** the merchandise belongs **to the buyer. In what** case **is this statement said?** It is said when the seller measures **with a measuring** vessel **that does not belong to either of them. But if the measuring** vessel **belonged to one of them,** the buyer **acquires** the items of sale **one** by **one** as they are placed in the measuring vessel.

### Third Party Vessel

The *Tosefta* immediately qualifies this rule: *bameh devarim amurim bemiddah she'einah shel shneihem* — in what circumstances does this apply? When the measuring vessel belongs to neither party, i.e., it was borrowed from a third party. The *gemara* supplies the logic. Since neither the seller nor the buyer owns the vessel, the third party's loan operates sequentially: until the seller completes measuring out the purchased quantity, he is the current user of the borrowed vessel and it functions as his *kli*; once the measure is full, the buyer becomes the current user, and at that moment he acquires the goods by *kinyan kli*. This explains why the transfer occurs precisely at the moment the measure is filled.

### Rishon Rishon Kanah


The *Tosefta* continues: *aval im hayetah middah shel echad mehem — rishon rishon kanah*. But if the measuring vessel belongs to one of the parties, then *rishon rishon kanah* — each successive portion acquires immediately as it is poured in. The Rashbam clarifies the meaning of *kanah* here: it does not signal the completion of a transaction but describes the state of possession. If the vessel belongs to the seller, each increment poured remains in his possession — *kanah* means it stays his — and no *kinyan* has been effected on behalf of the buyer even once the measure is full, since the vessel throughout has been the seller's. If the vessel belongs to the buyer, then each successive portion poured in is immediately acquired by the buyer, since it is entering his vessel. Thus *rishon rishon kanah* captures the buyer's continuous, incremental acquisition as the goods flow in.

This generates a curious question: what is the practical difference if the vessel belongs to the seller versus a third party? If the vessel is the seller's, nothing transfers until the buyer physically removes the goods or performs *hagbahah*. If borrowed from a third party, the transfer occurs automatically at the moment the measure is filled, by *kinyan kli*. This discussion is all preliminary to the central spatial question: where exactly is this transaction taking place?

### Tosefta Domains

The *Tosefta* now becomes directly relevant to the *gemara*'s central inquiry. It specifies the domains in which the above rules operate: *bameh devarim amurim — birshut harabim uvechatzer she'einah shel shneihem*. In what circumstances do these rules apply? In a *reshut harabim* and in a *chatzer* belonging to neither party. *Aval birshut mocher lo kanah* — but in the seller's domain, the buyer does not acquire — *ad she'yagbihennah*, until he lifts it, *o ad she'yotzienu mereshuto*, or takes it out of the seller's domain.

This is a striking *gemara*. The *Tosefta* takes a position that is almost the precise inverse of what the Babylonian *gemara* has been assuming. The Babylonian *gemara* treats *reshut harabim* as the paradigm case where *kinyan kli* fails — precisely because one has no right to leave vessels there. Yet the *Tosefta* says *kinyan kli* works in *reshut harabim*, while it is *reshut hamocher* where it fails. The *Tosefta*'s reasoning can be understood on its own terms: perhaps what matters is not the buyer's formal right to deposit the vessel but whether the goods have left the seller's sphere of control. Once the goods are in the buyer's vessel and no longer under the seller's hand, they are the buyer's — even if the buyer's vessel is technically sitting somewhere it should not be. Conversely, even if the buyer has permission to set his bag down in the seller's store, as long as they remain in the seller's domain they remain fundamentally under the seller's control.

### Reshut Lokeach

The *Tosefta* then addresses *reshut lokeach* — the buyer's own domain. Here, *keivan she'kibel alav mocher — kanah loke'ach*: once the seller has accepted the terms of the sale, the buyer acquires immediately. This is because the goods are already in the buyer's domain and *kinyan chatzer* operates automatically. One might find this somewhat counterintuitive: the usual value of a formal *kinyan* is to identify a concrete, unambiguous moment of transfer, and a simple statement of agreement seems less concrete than a physical act. Yet when the goods are already in the buyer's house — whether because the seller is a traveling merchant who has come to the buyer, or for some other reason — the act of agreement itself, *kibel alav*, is sufficient to trigger *kinyan chatzer*. The *chatzer* of the buyer is already in place; it only awaits the finalization of terms.

A related question arises whether this changes if the goods remain in the seller's own vessels within the buyer's domain. The *gemara* treats the two configurations — buyer's vessel in seller's domain, and seller's vessel in buyer's domain — as parallel and symmetrical. Whatever principle determines acquisition in one case must apply in the other.

### Third Party Domain

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בַּמֶּה דְּבָרִים אֲמוּרִים – בִּרְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים וּבְחָצֵר שֶׁאֵינָהּ שֶׁל שְׁנֵיהֶן, אֲבָל בִּרְשׁוּת מוֹכֵר – לֹא קָנָה עַד שֶׁיַּגְבִּיהֶנָּה אוֹ עַד שֶׁיּוֹצִיאֶנָּה מֵרְשׁוּתוֹ. בִּרְשׁוּת לוֹקֵחַ – כֵּיוָן שֶׁקִּבֵּל עָלָיו מוֹכֵר, קָנָה לוֹקֵחַ. בִּרְשׁוּת הַלָּה הַמּוּפְקָדִים אֶצְלוֹ – לֹא קָנָה עַד שֶׁיְּקַבֵּל עָלָיו, אוֹ עַד שֶׁיִּשְׂכּוֹר אֶת מְקוֹמָן.
> **Translation:** **In what** case **is this statement said?** It is said when the seller measures the items **in the public domain or in a courtyard that does not belong to either of them. But** if it happens **in the domain of** the **seller,** the buyer **does not acquire** the merchandise **until he lifts** the measuring vessel **or until he moves it out of the domain** of the seller. If it is **in the domain of** the **buyer, once** the **seller accepts upon himself** to sell, the **buyer acquires** it. If the merchandise is located **in the domain of this** individual **with whom it had been deposited,** the buyer **does not acquire** it **until** the bailee **accepts upon himself** to designate a place where the merchandise is to be stored for the buyer, **or until** the buyer **rents** from the bailee **the place** where the merchandise is situated.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָתָנֵי מִיהָא, בִּרְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים וּבְחָצֵר שֶׁאֵינָהּ שֶׁל שְׁנֵיהֶן;
> **Translation:** **In any event,** this *baraita* **teaches with regard to** a transaction in **the public domain or in a courtyard that does not belong to either of them** that if the measuring vessel belonged to one of them, the buyer acquires the items of sale one by one as they are placed in the vessel.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי, לָאו בִּרְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים מַמָּשׁ? לָא; סִימְטָא. וְהָא דּוּמְיָא דְּחָצֵר שֶׁאֵינָהּ שֶׁל שְׁנֵיהֶם קָתָנֵי!
> **Translation:** **What,** is it **not** stating that a buyer’s vessels effect acquisition of items on his behalf even **in the actual public domain,** which contradicts Rav Pappa’s explanation? The Gemara answers: **No,** the *baraita* is referring to **an alleyway,** not the actual public domain. The Gemara asks: How can this be what the *baraita* means? **But** the *baraita* **teaches** that it is **similar to a courtyard that does not belong to either of them,** i.e., a location to which neither of them have any rights, whereas they both have some rights in an alleyway. Consequently, this must be referring to the actual public domain, not an alleyway.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״חָצֵר שֶׁאֵינָהּ שֶׁל שְׁנֵיהֶם״ נָמֵי – דְּלָא דְּהַאי כּוּלָּהּ וְלָא דְּהַאי כּוּלָּהּ; אֶלָּא דְּתַרְוַיְיהוּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **What** is the meaning of the phrase: **A courtyard that does not belong to either of them?** It **also** refers to shared property, specifically a courtyard shared by partners, **which does not** belong to **this** one **entirely and does not** belong to **that** one **entirely, but rather** it is the property **of both of them.** Consequently, this courtyard is comparable to an alleyway.

The fourth domain addressed in the *Tosefta* is that of a third party — for example, the seller's goods are being stored in David's warehouse, and the buyer wishes to acquire them while they remain there. The *Tosefta* rules: *lo kanah* — the buyer does not acquire — *ad she'kibel alav*, until David accepts upon himself to hold the goods on behalf of the buyer, *o ad she'yischar mekoman* — or until the buyer rents the space from David.

The underlying principle here is essential. If the goods are in the seller's own domain, the seller cannot simply declare "I will now hold these on your behalf" and thereby effect a transfer: one cannot simultaneously be the party relinquishing possession and the party receiving it on behalf of the other side. But if a neutral third party is holding the goods, David can declare that he now holds them for the buyer. This functions on the principle of *zachin l'adam shelo befanav* — one may act to confer benefit on another in his absence. David, as an independent third party, can use his *chatzer* or his *yad* to be *zocheh* on the buyer's behalf, effecting acquisition. This is precisely why *kibel alav* by a third party works, whereas the same declaration by the seller would be meaningless. The buyer could alternatively rent the space from the seller himself; once he has done so, it becomes his own *yad*, and the seller's involvement is no longer acting as both sides of the transaction.

The question is raised whether David could, at the outset, make a blanket agreement to hold goods for whichever buyer the seller designates at the time of sale, without needing to know the buyer's identity in advance. This would resemble a form of *breira*, though not quite, since the *kinyan* would not operate retroactively — David would become the buyer's representative only at the moment the seller makes the designation. Such an arrangement might well be valid.

Tosafos and the Rashbam disagree about how exactly to count the "four categories" (*arba middos*). The Rashbam objects that the rules applicable in the seller's domain and in the third party's domain are in practice the same — *hagbahah* and removal work in both — and so they do not constitute genuinely distinct categories. Tosafos responds that there is indeed a difference: in the third party's domain, *kibel alav* by the third party suffices to effect acquisition without any rental or physical act, whereas in the seller's domain no such declaration by the seller can work. It is this difference that justifies treating them as four distinct categories.

The *gemara* returns to the *Tosefta*'s most significant ruling for the current discussion: *kinyan kli* works in *reshut harabim* and in a *chatzer she'einah shel shneihem*, but not in *reshut hamocher*. The *gemara* attempts to reread *reshut harabim* here as meaning merely "not a fully private domain," i.e., a *simta* shared by both parties — *she'einah shel echad mehem ella shel shneihem* — rather than a literal public thoroughfare. Similarly, *chatzer she'einah shel shneihem* is reread to mean *chatzer shel shneihem*, a jointly held space. This is a frankly forced reading. The *Tosefta* compares *reshut harabim* and *chatzer she'einah shel shneihem* in a way that implies they share the quality of not being either party's private domain — which would suggest a true *reshut harabim* is meant. Nevertheless, the Babylonian *gemara* insists on the reread, maintaining its position that *kinyan kli* does not work in a genuine *reshut harabim*. The *Eretz Yisrael* tradition — represented by Rabbi Yochanan, Reish Lakish, and apparently this *Tosefta* — seems to hold the opposite view, that once goods are in the buyer's vessel and outside the seller's control, the buyer acquires them regardless of whether he had a formal right to leave his vessel in that location. The Babylonian *gemara* rejects this position and rereads all the supporting evidence to conform to its own view.

## Gittin Case Analysis



### Get in Basket

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּעָא מִינֵּיהּ רַב שֵׁשֶׁת מֵרַב הוּנָא: כִּלְיוֹ שֶׁל לוֹקֵחַ בִּרְשׁוּת מוֹכֵר – קָנָה לוֹקֵחַ, אוֹ לָא? אֲמַר לֵיהּ, תְּנֵיתוּהָ: זְרָקוֹ לָהּ לְתוֹךְ חֵיקָהּ אוֹ לְתוֹךְ קַלְתָּהּ – הֲרֵי זוֹ מְגוֹרֶשֶׁת.
> **Translation:** **Rav Sheshet raises a dilemma before Rav Huna:** If the **vessels of the buyer** are **in the domain of the seller,** does the **buyer acquire** the merchandise once it is placed in his vessels **or not?** Rav Huna **said to him: You learned** the answer already in a mishna (*Gittin* 77a): A wife is divorced when her husband hands her a bill of divorce or places it in a manner that is considered equivalent to handing it to her, e.g., placing in her courtyard. Accordingly, if the husband **threw** the bill of divorce **to her into her lap or into her basket [*kaltah*], this** woman **is divorced** even if she was in her husband’s domain at that time. By the same token, even if the buyer’s vessels are in the domain of the seller, they effect acquisition of the sold items on his behalf.

Having set aside the *Tosefta* evidence, the *gemara* turns to a case from *Gittin* as a potential proof for the question of *kinyan kli* in *reshut hamocher*. The case: a husband throws a *get* into his wife's *cheikah* — her lap — or into her *kaltah* (basket; the word appears to be of Greek origin), while she is standing in his house. The ruling is *harei zu megureshset* — she is validly divorced. The implicit logic is that if the *get* lands in the wife's basket and takes effect as a valid delivery, then the wife's vessel must be considered her domain — her *reshut* — even while it sits in the husband's house. And if *kinyan kli* works in this way for *gittin*, it should likewise work for commercial transactions: the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain should be considered the buyer's domain, and the goods placed into it should be acquired.

The connection between *gittin* and *kinyanim* rests on a shared underlying question: whose domain is the object considered to be in? The *pasuk* instructs *venatan beyadah* — he shall place it in her hand — and the *gemara* on *gittin* extends this to include her *chatzer*, her roof, her enclosure, and her basket. One might object that the act of delivering a *get* need not be governed by the laws of *kinyanim* at all — the *pasuk* says place it in her hand, and perhaps placing it on her person or in her possession is sufficient on its own terms, without satisfying a formal *ma'aseh kinyan*. The *gemara* here, however, proceeds on the assumption that the two are parallel: if the wife's basket functions as her domain for purposes of receiving the *get*, it must be because the basket is treated as her *chatzer*, her property — which is precisely the same analysis that governs *kinyan kli* in commercial law. The mechanism in both *gittin* and commercial *kinyanim* thus reduces to the same inquiry: does the wife's basket in the husband's house constitute *her* domain for these purposes?

### Suspension and Tie

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב נַחְמָן: מַאי טַעְמָא פָּשְׁטַתְּ לֵיהּ מֵהַהִיא, דְּמָחוּ לַהּ מְאָה עוּכְלֵי בְּעוּכְלָא?
> **Translation:** **Rav Naḥman said to** Rav Huna: **What is the reason** that **you resolved** Rav Sheshet’s dilemma **from that** mishna, **which** has already been **struck** with **one hundred strikes of a hammer [*uklei be’ukela*]?** In their analysis of this mishna, the Sages have already inserted so many qualifications that it cannot be understood in a straightforward manner.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּאָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה אָמַר שְׁמוּאֵל: וְהוּא שֶׁהָיְתָה קַלְתָּהּ תְּלוּיָה בָּהּ. וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ אָמַר: קְשׁוּרָה, וְאַף עַל פִּי שֶׁאֵינָהּ תְּלוּיָה בַּהּ. רַב אַדָּא בַּר אַהֲבָה אָמַר: כְּגוֹן שֶׁהָיְתָה קַלְתָּהּ מוּנַּחַת לָהּ בֵּין יַרְכוֹתֶיהָ. רַב מְשַׁרְשְׁיָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי אַמֵּי אָמַר: כְּגוֹן שֶׁהָיָה בַּעְלָהּ מוֹכֵר קְלָתוֹת.
> **Translation:** **As Rav Yehuda says** that **Shmuel says: This** *halakha* of the mishna in *Gittin* applies only if **her basket was hanging from her** body, so that it is considered on her or in her hand. **And Reish Lakish says:** It is sufficient if **it** was **tied** to her, **even though it is not hanging from her,** but resting on the ground. **Rav Adda bar Ahava says:** The mishna is referring to a case **where her basket was placed between her thighs.** Although it is not hanging from her, since it is placed on her body it serves to acquire the bill of divorce on her behalf. **Rav Mesharshiyya, son of Rabbi Ami, says:** This is referring to a case **where her husband** was **a basket seller.** Since he is not particular about the place where the basket into which he placed the bill of divorce is located, as his entire courtyard is full of baskets, it is considered as though he expressly granted her the right to make use of its location.

Rav Nachman immediately deflates this proof, noting that this question was raised and thoroughly addressed in the tractate of *Gittin* itself — so thoroughly that the expression *demachla me'ah*, "they knocked it down a hundred times," is used. Several answers were proposed there, each of which explains the *gittin* ruling on grounds that do not extend to the general case of *kinyan kli* in *reshut hamocher*.

The first explanation, attributed to Rav Yehuda in the name of Shmuel: *pashita kaltah teluyah bah* — the case is obvious if the basket is suspended from her body. If the basket hangs from her person rather than resting on the ground, it is effectively an extension of her body, analogous to *hagbahah* or to *kinyan yad*. In that circumstance it is not a case of *kinyan kli* in the ordinary sense at all — it is more like the object being placed directly on her person. Reish Lakish adds: *keshurah* — even if the basket is merely tied to her body without being lifted off the ground, it is considered sufficiently connected to her person to effect the transfer. The question of whether being tied to a person while resting on the ground constitutes *hagbahah* or a distinct form of *kinyan yad* is left open, but either way these cases are distinguishable from the general question of a free-standing *kli* in someone else's domain.

### Mekom Cheika Kanuy

Rabbi Yochanan offers his own formulation: *mekom cheika kanuy lah, mekom kaltah kanuy lah* — the place of her lap is acquired by her, the place of her basket is acquired by her. This is a more expansive principle, going beyond the technical question of suspension or attachment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: מְקוֹם חֵיקָהּ קָנוּי לָהּ, מְקוֹם קַלְתָּהּ קָנוּי לָהּ. אָמַר רָבָא: מַאי טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן? לְפִי שֶׁאֵין אָדָם מַקְפִּיד לֹא עַל מְקוֹם חֵיקָהּ וְלֹא עַל מְקוֹם קַלְתָּהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: The place of her lap,** i.e., the place within her husband’s property where she stands or sits, **belongs to her,** and **the place of her basket is acquired to her. Rava said: What is the reason** behind the statement **of Rabbi Yoḥanan?** It is **because a person,** including a husband, **is not particular neither about the place of her lap nor about the place of her basket,** as she requires these areas and they do not take up much space. It is evident from all of these qualifications that one cannot infer a halakhic principle from here with regard to a buyer’s vessels in a seller’s domain.

### Rava's Explanation


Rava explains the rationale behind Rabbi Yochanan's principle: *ein adam makpid lo al mekom cheikah velo al mekom kaltah* — a husband is simply not particular about where his wife sits or where she places her basket. This non-particularity amounts to an ongoing, de facto grant of rights to that space. The wife therefore has a genuine, continuous entitlement to the space her lap and basket occupy within the household — not a momentary permission like that extended to a customer in a store, but something more like an enduring proprietary right.

This distinction is critical for understanding why the *gittin* case cannot simply be transplanted into the commercial setting. And it is worth pausing here, because on the surface the two situations might seem analogous: in neither case is the property owner actively asserting control over the precise spot where the other party's receptacle rests. In a store, a seller is also not exactly protesting that the customer has set a bag down — and yet the *gemara* treats the cases differently. The answer lies in the character of the permission granted. In a store, a seller permits a customer to set down a bag for the brief duration of a transaction, but this is a purely temporary and instrumental accommodation. The seller remains fundamentally in control of the space and the goods; the customer's bag is there for a moment. By contrast, a wife in her husband's home has an ongoing, ambient right to the space she occupies, so much so that the husband is not *makpid* — not even mildly asserting his ownership over that particular patch of floor. Because of this ongoing entitlement, her basket can be considered to be in her domain in a substantive sense.

### Gittin Distinction

The *gemara* therefore concludes that the *gittin* case cannot prove *kinyan kli* in *reshut hamocher* for commercial transactions. The circumstances are categorically different: either the basket in *gittin* is suspended or tied to her body (making it effectively *hagbahah* or *kinyan yad*), or she has an ongoing proprietary right to the space (not merely a customer's transient permission), or both. None of these conditions obtain in the typical commercial scenario, so no proof can be drawn.

It is worth pausing to note where this leaves the overall inquiry. The *gemara* has now worked through two attempted proofs for *kinyan kli* in settings other than *reshut hakoneh*: the case of *keilav b'reshut harabbim* — the buyer's vessel in the public domain — was examined first, and that proof was rejected. Now the attempt to establish *keilav b'reshut mocher* — the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain — from the *gittin* case has likewise been set aside. The question of whether *kinyan kli* functions in *reshut hamocher* therefore remains open, and it is worth noting that the *gemara* has been raising and dismantling these proofs independently, using the *baraita* as evidence rather than simply deferring to what the *baraita* explicitly rules. That explicit ruling — that acquisition in the seller's domain does not work — is the next thing to be addressed directly.

## Tosefta Evidence



### Tosefta Simple Read

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא פְּשׁוֹט לָהּ מֵהָא: בִּרְשׁוּת מוֹכֵר – לֹא קָנָה עַד שֶׁיַּגְבִּיהֶנָּה אוֹ עַד שֶׁיּוֹצִיאֶנָּה מֵרְשׁוּתוֹ. מַאי, לָאו בְּכִלְיוֹ דְלוֹקֵחַ? לֹא, בְּכִלְיוֹ דְמוֹכֵר.
> **Translation:** **Rather, resolve** the dilemma **from that** which was taught in a *baraita*: If the merchandise was **in the domain of a seller,** the buyer **does not acquire** the merchandise **until he lifts** it **or until he removes it from the domain** of the seller. **What,** is it **not** referring to merchandise placed **in the vessels of the buyer,** which proves that the buyer’s vessels do not effect acquisition of the merchandise on his behalf when they are in the seller’s domain? The Gemara answers: **No,** this does not serve as proof, as it is referring to merchandise placed **in the vessels of the seller.** That is why the buyer must lift or pull the merchandise to acquire it.

The *gemara* returns to what seems the most straightforward source: the *Tosefta* itself, which states explicitly — *birshut mocher lo kanah ad she'yagbihenu o ad she'yotzienu mereshuto* — that in the seller's domain the buyer does not acquire until the goods are lifted or removed from that domain. The simple reading of this *Tosefta* is transparent: it does not matter that the goods may be sitting in the buyer's vessel; as long as they remain in the seller's domain, the buyer has not acquired them. Only *hagbahah* or physical removal from the seller's domain suffices to complete the transaction. This apparently settles the question of *kinyan kli* in *reshut hamocher* — it simply does not work.

But the *gemara* immediately interrogates the *Tosefta*'s assumed backdrop. The *Tosefta* says the buyer does not acquire in the seller's domain until *hagbahah* or removal — but in whose vessel are the goods sitting? The *gemara* proposes: *mai lav be'kelav de'loke'ach* — is this not speaking of a case where the goods are already in the buyer's own bags? If so, the *Tosefta* would be establishing that even *kinyan kli* in the buyer's own vessels fails to effect acquisition when those vessels are located in the seller's domain, which is precisely the strong proof the *gemara* was seeking. The *gemara* deflects this reading: *lo be'kelav de'mocher* — no, the *Tosefta* is describing a case where the goods remain in the seller's bags. In that scenario, it is unsurprising that no acquisition occurs; of course the buyer does not acquire goods sitting in the seller's own vessels in the seller's own domain. The *Tosefta* would then say nothing at all about *kinyan kli* in the buyer's vessels within the seller's domain.

Yet this deflection generates its own difficulty. If the *Tosefta*'s default assumption throughout is that the goods are always in the seller's bags, then its ruling about *reshut loke'ach* — the buyer's domain — becomes incoherent, a tension the *gemara* will pursue in the next step. The underlying issue the *gemara* is pressing here is that the two scenarios — the seller's bags in the buyer's domain, and the buyer's bags in the seller's domain — must be treated symmetrically. Whatever principle determines acquisition when *kli* and *chatzer* belong to different parties in one configuration must apply equally to the mirror configuration. If location in the larger domain governs, it governs in both cases; if ownership of the containing vessel governs, it governs in both cases. The *gemara* does not yet allow that the two factors can operate differently depending on which party's vessel or domain is at stake — a tension it will press directly against the *Tosefta*'s internal consistency.

### Gemara Challenge

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִדְּרֵישָׁא בְּכִלְיוֹ דְמוֹכֵר – סֵיפָא נָמֵי בְּכִלְיוֹ דְמוֹכֵר; אֵימָא סֵיפָא: בִּרְשׁוּת לוֹקֵחַ, כֵּיוָן שֶׁקִּיבֵּל עָלָיו מוֹכֵר – קָנָה לוֹקֵחַ. וְאִי בְּכִלְיוֹ דְמוֹכֵר, אַמַּאי קָנָה לוֹקֵחַ? סֵיפָא אֲתָאן לְכִלְיוֹ דְלוֹקֵחַ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But from** the fact **that the first clause** is referring **to the vessels of the seller,** as currently understood, **the latter clause** must **also** be referring **to the vessels of the seller. Say the latter clause:** If the merchandise was **in the domain of the buyer, once the seller accepts upon himself** to sell an item, **the buyer acquires** it. **And if** this is referring to merchandise **in the vessels of the seller,** as in the earlier clause, **why does the buyer acquire** it? The Gemara answers: In **the latter clause, we come to** a different scenario, which involves **the vessels of the buyer.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַאי פַּסְקָא? סְתָמָא דְמִילְּתָא, בֵּי מוֹכֵר – מָאנֵי דְמוֹכֵר שְׁכִיחִי, בֵּי לוֹקֵחַ – מָאנֵי דְלוֹקֵחַ שְׁכִיחִי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** if the clauses of the *baraita* are addressing different cases, **why** was it stated **without qualification?** The Gemara answers: **The normal** way **of things** is that **in the house of the seller the vessels of the seller are** commonly **found, and in the house of the buyer the vessels of the buyer are** commonly **found.** In sum, the dilemma cannot be resolved from the *baraita*.

But the *gemara* resists this conclusion and probes the *Tosefta* for internal consistency. The challenge arises from the *Tosefta*'s ruling on *reshut lokeach*: in the buyer's domain, once the seller accepts the terms (*kibel alav mocher*), the buyer acquires. If the *Tosefta* is uniformly discussing cases where the goods are in the seller's vessels, then why does *kinyan chatzer* of the buyer work in his domain — after all, the goods are still in the seller's bags? If the reasoning is that it is the seller's *kli* that matters and not the domain, then the buyer's *chatzer* should be irrelevant. If the reasoning is that the domain determines acquisition, then being in the seller's *kli* within the buyer's domain should not undermine *kinyan chatzer*. The *gemara* is pressing the point that the two principles — domain-based acquisition and vessel-based acquisition — cannot both be operative simultaneously without contradiction.

### Goes by Reshut

The *gemara* resolves this by stepping back and concluding that the *Tosefta* is in fact entirely domain-based (*reshut*), not *kli*-based. The reason the *Tosefta* produces consistent results across all four cases is not that we are tracking whose *kli* the goods are in, but simply whose domain they are in. The *Tosefta* simply assumes, as a matter of practical reality, that goods in the seller's store are typically in the seller's vessels, and goods in the buyer's home are typically in the buyer's vessels — *stama demilta, bei mocher mani democher shechichei, bei loke'ach mani delokeach shechichei* — in the seller's premises, the available containers are the seller's; in the buyer's premises, the available containers are the buyer's. The whole rule goes by *reshut*, and the mention of *kelim* is incidental to the typical circumstance in each location. This is a notably forced reading, and the *gemara* is quite consciously working against the plain meaning (*pshat*) of the *Tosefta*. The *Tosefta* very clearly teaches that it does not matter whose bags the goods are in — what matters is whose domain. If one is in the seller's store, one cannot acquire even if the goods are already in the buyer's bags, not until they leave the store; if the seller comes to the buyer's home, the buyer acquires once the goods are in the buyer's domain even if they remain in the seller's bags. The *gemara*, by contrast, insists that domain is not the operative principle at all — that what the *Tosefta* really meant was whose *kelim* the goods are in, and that it simply assumed *kelim* and domain would align as a matter of course. This is, as the *gemara*'s own framing of *umai paska* — meaning, on what basis do you simply assume that? — implicitly concedes, not a self-evident reading. But the *gemara* maintains it nonetheless: the *Tosefta*'s principle is domain-based, and the question of *kinyan kli* independent of domain remains open.

## Donkey Proof



### Pirkan Setup

Rava introduces a new attempt to resolve the question, drawing on a *baraita* concerning a seller's donkeys loaded with goods. The case: the buyer leads the seller's donkey-drivers and their donkeys — laden with bags of apples — into the buyer's house. On its face, this might seem to constitute *meshichah* — the pulling or drawing that effects *kinyan* on movable goods — but the *gemara* notes that the buyer did not perform *meshichah* directly on the apples; he pulled the donkeys, not the apples themselves, and perhaps only guided the donkey-drivers rather than the donkeys. Moreover, even if one argued for *meshichah* on the donkeys, the goods on the donkeys' backs may not be affected. Similarly, while the goods have now entered the buyer's domain, they remain on the backs of the seller's donkeys — not resting in the buyer's space independently — and so *kinyan chatzer* may not apply either. The *baraita* then addresses the case of *pirkan* — where the donkeys have been unloaded and the bags now sit in the buyer's house.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא, תָּא שְׁמַע: מָשַׁךְ חֲמָרָיו וּפוֹעֲלָיו, וְהִכְנִיסָן לְתוֹךְ בֵּיתוֹ; בֵּין פָּסַק עַד שֶׁלֹּא מָדַד, וּבֵין מָדַד עַד שֶׁלֹּא פָּסַק – שְׁנֵיהֶן יְכוֹלִין לַחֲזוֹר בָּהֶן.
> **Translation:** **Rava said: Come** and **hear** a resolution from a *baraita*: If **one pulled his donkey drivers,** thereby dragging along with them the donkeys laden with goods, **and** likewise, if he pulled **his laborers,** who were carrying merchandise he wished to purchase, **and he brought them into his house, whether he fixed** a price **before he measured** the merchandise **or whether he measured before fixing** a price, **both** parties **can renege on** the sale, provided that the merchandise has not been unloaded from the laborers or the donkeys.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פֵּירְקָן וְהִכְנִיסָן לְתוֹךְ בֵּיתוֹ; פָּסַק עַד שֶׁלֹּא מָדַד – אֵין שְׁנֵיהֶן יְכוֹלִין לַחֲזוֹר בָּהֶן, מָדַד עַד שֶׁלֹּא פָּסַק – שְׁנֵיהֶן יְכוֹלִין לַחֲזוֹר בָּהֶן. וּמִדְּכִלְיוֹ דְמוֹכֵר בִּרְשׁוּת לוֹקֵחַ – לֹא קָנָה, כִּלְיוֹ דְלוֹקֵחַ נָמֵי – בִּרְשׁוּת מוֹכֵר לֹא קָנָה!
> **Translation:** But in a case where **he unloaded** the merchandise from them **and brought it into his house,** if **he fixed** a price **before he measured** the merchandise, **both** parties **are no** longer **able to renege on** the sale. If **he measured** the merchandise **before fixing** a price, **both of them are able to renege on** the sale. The Gemara comments: **And from** the fact **that the vessels of the seller** when **in the domain of** the **buyer do not effect acquisition** of the merchandise for the seller, i.e., they do not prevent the buyer from acquiring the merchandise, one can derive that **the vessels of the buyer in the domain of** the **seller do not effect acquisition** of the merchandise on his behalf **as well.**

### Pesak and Madad


The *baraita* introduces a distinction based on *gemirut da'at* — finality of intent. If the price has been established (*pasak*) but the goods have not yet been measured (*madad*), or if the goods have been measured but no price established, then even after the donkeys are unloaded into the buyer's house, *shneihem yecholim lachzor bahen* — either party may retract, because there is no *gemirut da'at*. However, if both a price has been agreed upon and the goods have been brought into the buyer's domain via *pirkan*, then *ein shneihem yecholim lachzor bahen* — neither party may retract. The *kinyan chatzer* is complete once there is both *gemirut da'at* (established price) and physical entry into the buyer's domain. The measurement need not be complete for the *kinyan* to take effect; what matters is that both parties have committed to the transaction with sufficient finality.

### Chatzer Proof

The crucial implication of the *pirkan* case is that once the goods have been unloaded into the buyer's house — even though they remain in the seller's bags — the sale becomes final. This appears to be a direct proof that *kinyan chatzer* operates according to the domain, not the vessel: the goods are in the seller's bags (*kelav delokeach* in the buyer's *reshut*), and yet the buyer acquires. If *kinyan kli* in the buyer's domain could be undermined by the fact that the goods remain in the seller's vessel, then the *baraita* would not rule that the buyer acquires at *pirkan*. The case thus appears to demonstrate symmetrically that it is the domain that controls, not the *kli*.

### Gemara Defenses

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: בְּשֶׁשְּׁפָכָן. אִיקְּפַד רָבָא – מִידֵּי ״שְׁפָכָן״ קָתָנֵי?! ״פֵּירְקָן״ קָתָנֵי! אֶלָּא אָמַר מָר בַּר רַב אָשֵׁי: בִּמְתַאכְּלֵי דְתוּמֵי.
> **Translation:** **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said:** The merchandise was not placed in the domain of the buyer in vessels belonging to the seller. Rather, the *baraita* is referring to a case **where he emptied** the vessels onto the ground. Conversely, if the merchandise remains in the seller’s vessels, the buyer does not acquire it. **Rava became angry** with Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak at his rejection of Rava’s proof, and retorted: **Does** the *baraita* **teach: He emptied them?** No; **it teaches: He unloaded them,** i.e., he kept the goods in vessels belonging to the seller. **Rather, Mar bar Rav Ashi says:** This proof can be rejected by means of a different interpretation, as the *halakha* of the *baraita* does not refer to vessels full of goods but is stated **with regard to bundles of garlic** that were tied together. Therefore, they are unloaded straight onto the floor in the buyer’s domain.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּמָר זוּטְרָא לְרָבִינָא: מִכְּדֵי ״פֵּירְקָן״ קָתָנֵי; מָה לִי פָּסַק וּמָה לִי לֹא פָּסַק? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: פָּסַק – סָמְכָא דַּעְתֵּיהּ, לֹא פָּסַק – לָא סָמְכָא דַּעְתֵּיהּ.
> **Translation:** With regard to the matter itself, **Huna, son of Mar Zutra, said to Ravina: Since** the *baraita* **teaches: He unloaded them,** which indicates that unloading the merchandise constitutes the act of acquisition, **what** difference is there **to me** if **he fixed** a price, **and what** difference is there **to me** if **he did not fix** a price? Ravina **said to him:** If **he fixed** a price **he has made up his mind** to sell, and therefore the transaction can take place. If **he did not fix** a price, **he has not made up his mind** to sell and the transaction does not occur. In any event, no convincing proof has been found with regard to the *halakha* in a case where the vessels of the buyer are in the domain of the seller.

The *gemara* refuses to accept this proof and offers two defensive readings. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak proposes that *pirkan* — "he unloaded" — means the seller literally poured out the apples, emptying them directly onto the floor of the buyer's house, so there are no seller's bags involved at all. Rava objects sharply: the *baraita* uses the word *pirkan*, not *shafchan* — it says he unloaded, not that he dumped or poured out. *Pirkan* implies removing the bags from the donkeys while the goods remain inside them; the bags are still present. The *gemara* then offers a second defense, attributed to Mar bar Rav Ashi: the case involves *matachli detuma* — strings or braids of garlic, which were typically not packed in bags at all but carried loosely or braided together directly on the donkey's back. Since garlic was sold and transported in this form without bags, *pirkan* in this context means simply unloading the garlic strands onto the buyer's floor — and since there were never any bags, the proof says nothing about what happens when the seller's vessels are present. The question of *kinyan kli* in *reshut hamocher* thus remains unresolved, and the *gemara* leaves it open for the continuation of the sugya.
