## Single Witness Testimony



### Mishna Setup

The perek of *Ha-Ishah Rabbah* is one of the most perplexing and challenging *perakim* in all of Shas — not only analytically, but in terms of *rabbinic* justice. The central difficulty is understanding how a woman who appears to have done everything right ends up in such severe legal jeopardy: she must leave both husbands, and she loses her rights from each of them.

The *Talmud* begins by identifying the precise situation described in the *Mishnah*. A single witness came and told the woman that her husband was dead. She went to the *beit din*, received permission to remarry, and did so. Because the remarriage was predicated on the testimony of a single witness, all of these harsh consequences follow. Had two witnesses come and told her that her husband was dead, the picture would be entirely different — she could return to her first husband, the second marriage would be treated as a nullity, and so on. What the *Mishnah*'s structure implies, then, is that a single witness is in fact *believed* in such a case.

### Korban Chatas Source

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מְנָא לַן? דְּתַנְיָא: ״אוֹ הוֹדַע אֵלָיו חַטָּאתוֹ״. וְלֹא שֶׁיּוֹדִיעוּהוּ אֲחֵרִים. יָכוֹל אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁאֵינוֹ מַכְחִישׁוֹ יְהֵא פָּטוּר? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״אוֹ הוֹדַע אֵלָיו״ — מִכׇּל מָקוֹם.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks: **From where do we** derive this? The Gemara answers: **As it is taught** in a *baraita* that the verse states: **“Or if his sin be known to him”** (Leviticus 4:23, 28). This indicates that he himself must be aware of his sin, **and not if it was made known to him by others.** In other words, one is not obligated to bring an offering due to the testimony of others, even if they testify that he had transgressed. I **might** have thought **he should be exempt even though he does not contradict** the witness’s claim. Therefore, **the verse states: If his sin be known to him,** which indicates that **in any case,** however he comes by this knowledge, he is liable.

This leads the *Gemara* to a further implication. It is established that if one witness comes and says *achalta chelev* — "you ate forbidden fat" — and the accused responds *lo achalti chelev*, he is *patur* from bringing a *korban*. But if he remains silent, he would be *chayav*. This means that on the basis of a single witness, one is obligated to bring a *korban chatas* — not merely an *asham talui*, the offering brought in cases of genuine doubt, but the *chatas* itself, which implies that the sin is treated as established. This in turn implies that the single witness has been believed *m'doraisa* — a striking conclusion.

The *Gemara* asks: *minalan* — from where do we derive that a single witness can be believed at the Torah level? The source is a *baraita* interpreting the verse *o hoda eilav chataso*: his sin must become known *to him*, and not through others informing him. The *baraita* raises the possibility that even if he fails to contradict someone who accuses him, he might still be *patur* — since another person informed him, perhaps that does not satisfy the requirement of *o hoda eilav*. The verse is therefore read broadly: *mikol makom*, from any situation, he can come to know of his sin.

### Ne'emanut vs. Shtika

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֵיכִי דָמֵי? אִילֵּימָא דַּאֲתוֹ תְּרֵי וְלָא קָא מַכְחִישׁ לְהוּ, קְרָא לְמָה לִי? אֶלָּא לָאו — חַד, וְכִי לָא קָא מַכְחִישׁ לֵיהּ — מְהֵימַן, שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ עֵד אֶחָד נֶאֱמָן. וּמִמַּאי דְּמִשּׁוּם דִּמְהֵימַן? דִּלְמָא מִשּׁוּם דְּקָא שָׁתֵיק, וּשְׁתִיקָה כְּהוֹדָאָה דָמְיָא?
> **Translation:** The Gemara clarifies this *halakha*. **What are the circumstances? If we say that two** witnesses **came** and informed him **and** he **does not contradict them, why do I** need a **verse** to teach this ruling? After all, the testimony of two witnesses is always accepted. **Rather, is it not** referring to **one** witness, **and** yet **if he does not contradict** the sole witness, that witness is **deemed credible?** One can **learn from this** that **one witness is deemed credible** with regard to prohibitions. The Gemara refutes this claim: **And from where** do you infer **that** the reason is **due to** the fact that the one witness **is deemed credible? Perhaps** the accused must bring an offering **because** he remains **silent, as** there is a principle that **silence is considered like an admission.**

The *Gemara* now asks: *heichi dami* — what exactly is this case? If two witnesses came and the accused failed to contradict them, why would we need a verse at all? Two witnesses establish a fact; the *korban* obligation would be obvious. It must therefore be that the *baraita* is discussing a single witness — *chad* — and the inference is that if one fails to contradict a single witness, one is *chayav*. This would mean that the single witness is actually believed, *shema mina eid echad ne'eman*.

The *Gemara* immediately pushes back: *umimai demishum de'meiman*? Perhaps this has nothing to do with the reliability of the witness at all. Perhaps the reason for the *chiyuv* is *mishum de'kashtik* — because the accused was silent. And *shtika ke'hoda'ah damya* — silence functions as a form of admission. This is confirmed by the *seifa* of the *baraita*.

### Two Witnesses Problem

The end of the *baraita* states: if two witnesses say *achalta chelev* and the accused says *lo achalti*, he is *patur* — even against two witnesses. *Rabbi Meir* disagrees and holds him *chayav*. This is a striking passage. Our initial assumption was that two witnesses obviously should be believed — *lama li kra*, why would we need a verse to establish what is self-evident? But the *seifa* reveals that the *Rabbanan* do not convict even on the basis of two witnesses if the accused denies it. This suggests that the *Rabbanan* are not operating in the realm of *ne'emanut* — of witness reliability — at all.

### Shtika K'Hoda'ah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תֵּדַע, דְּקָתָנֵי סֵיפָא: אָמְרוּ שְׁנַיִם ״אָכַלְתָּ חֵלֶב״, וְהוּא אוֹמֵר ״לֹא אָכַלְתִּי״ — פָּטוּר. רַבִּי מֵאִיר מְחַיֵּיב. אָמַר רַבִּי מֵאִיר: קַל וָחוֹמֶר, אִם הֱבִיאוּהוּ שְׁנַיִם לִידֵי מִיתָה חֲמוּרָה — לֹא יְבִיאוּהוּ לִידֵי קׇרְבָּן הַקַּל?
> **Translation:** And you should **know** that this is the reason, **as the latter clause** of **that** same *baraita* **teaches** that if **two** witnesses **said** to him: **You ate forbidden fat, and he says: I did not eat** it, he is **exempt,** and **Rabbi Meir obligates** him to bring an offering. **Rabbi Meir said** that this is **an *a fortiori*** inference: **If two** witnesses **can bring him to** the **severe** penalty of **death** by testifying that he had committed a transgression for which one is liable to receive the death penalty, should they **not bring him to** the **more lenient** obligation of **an offering?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרוּ לוֹ: מָה אִם יִרְצֶה לוֹמַר ״מֵזִיד הָיִיתִי״! רֵישָׁא
> **Translation:** The Rabbis **said to him:** There is a difference between the two cases, as with regard to an offering, **what** is the *halakha* **if he would choose to say: I was an intentional** sinner? One who sins intentionally is not liable to bring an offering. Since the accused in the latter clause of the *baraita* can negate the testimony that would have rendered him liable to bring an offering, he can likewise deny the act itself, whereas if witnesses testify that he performed an action that incurs the death penalty, his denial has no bearing on the case. The Gemara clarifies: In **the first clause**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי טַעְמָא קָא מְחַיְּיבִי רַבָּנַן? אִילֵּימָא מִשּׁוּם דִּמְהֵימַן — וְהָא תְּרֵי בְּעָלְמָא, דְּאַף עַל גַּב דְּקָא מַכְחִישׁ לְהוּ אִינְהוּ — מְהֵימְנִי, וְקָא פָּטְרִי רַבָּנַן. אֶלָּא לָאו, מִשּׁוּם דְּאִישְׁתִּיק — וּשְׁתִיקָה כְּהוֹדָאָה דָמְיָא.
> **Translation:** of the mishna, **what is the reason that** when he remains silent, **the Rabbis obligate** him to bring to an offering based on the testimony of one witness? **If we say** it is **because** the witness **is deemed credible, but** there is the case of **an ordinary pair** of witnesses, **where even though he contradicts their** claim **they are deemed credible, and** yet **the Rabbis exempt** him from bringing an offering. If so, they would certainly not obligate him to bring an offering due to the testimony of a lone witness. **Rather, is it not because he** remained **silent, and silence is considered like an admission?** If this is the reason why he brings an offering, there is no proof from here that the testimony of one witness is accepted.

*Rabbi Meir* argues by way of a *kal vachomer*: if two witnesses can bring a person to the death penalty, surely they should be sufficient to obligate a *korban*, which is far less severe. The *Rabbanan* respond: the accused is not without recourse even against two witnesses, because he could always say *mezid hayiti* — I acted intentionally — in which case no *korban* would be due. This is a form of *migo*: since he has available to him a more extreme claim that would exempt him, we credit his denial as well. The deeper point is that a *korban chatas* depends not on whether a person sinned, but on whether they *know* they sinned. No external testimony can substitute for a person's own internal certainty about what occurred and what their intentions were.

### Rabbi Meir's Kal Vachomer

Returning to the opening of the *baraita*, the *Gemara* asks: *reisha mai ta'ama kemachayvei Rabbanan* — why do the *Rabbanan* hold that silence in the face of a single witness creates *chiyuv*? If it were because the single witness is *ne'eman*, then we would be back to a contradiction — here are two witnesses who are *ne'eman* in all circumstances, yet the *Rabbanan* exempt the accused even against their testimony. The answer must be that the *Rabbanan* are not working within the framework of *ne'emanut* at all. Rather, *ela lav mishum de'ishtik* — it is entirely because the accused was silent. *Shtika ke'hoda'ah damya*: silence is treated as an admission.

### Internal Knowledge Model

The *Gemara* therefore proposes to abandon the verse entirely and seek a *sevara* — a logical basis. It cites the known case of *safek chelev safek shuman*: a piece of meat that might be *chelev* or might be permitted fat, where a single witness comes and declares *bari li d'shuman hu* — I am certain it is permitted — and is believed. A brief aside is warranted here: it is striking that the *Talmud* reaches for the analogy of the piece of meat to describe the legal status of a wife. Feminist scholars of the *Talmud* have noted this kind of comparison, as the *din* of the woman in this case is treated as formally identical to the *din* of a cut of meat.

### Chelev Analogy

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא סְבָרָא הִיא, מִידֵּי דְּהָוֵה אַחֲתִיכָה סָפֵק שֶׁל חֵלֶב סָפֵק שֶׁל שׁוּמָּן, וַאֲתָא עֵד אֶחָד וְאָמַר: בָּרִי לִי, דְּשׁוּמָּן הוּא — דִּמְהֵימַן.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** this **is** evidently based on **logical reasoning: Just as it is in** the case of **a piece** of meat about which it is **uncertain** if it is **forbidden fat** and **uncertain** if it is **of** permitted **fat,** and there is no way of clarifying which it is, **and one witness comes and says: It is clear to me that it is** permitted **fat,** the *halakha* is **that he is deemed credible.** Here too, the testimony of a single witness can resolve the uncertainty.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִי דָּמֵי? הָתָם לָא אִיתַּחְזַק אִיסּוּרָא, הָכָא אִיתַּחְזַק אִיסּוּרָא דְּאֵשֶׁת אִישׁ, וְאֵין דָּבָר שֶׁבְּעֶרְוָה פָּחוֹת מִשְּׁנַיִם!
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **Is it comparable? There, the presumption of a prohibition has not** been established, as there is no proof that the piece was ever forbidden, and one can therefore rely on the witness who permits it, whereas **here, the presumption of** the **prohibition** with regard **to a married woman is** established, **and** there is a principle that **nothing involving** those **with whom relations are forbidden** can be determined by **fewer than two** witnesses.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא לָא דָּמְיָא אֶלָּא לַחֲתִיכָה דְּוַדַּאי חֵלֶב, וַאֲתָא עֵד אֶחָד וְאָמַר: בָּרִי לִי דְּשׁוּמָּן הֲוָה — דְּלָא מְהֵימַן! מִי דָּמֵי? הָתָם, אֲפִילּוּ אָתוּ בֵּי מְאָה לָא מְהֵימְנִי. הָכָא, כֵּיוָן דְּכִי אָתוּ בֵּי תְרֵי מְהֵימְנִי — חַד נָמֵי לְהֵימְנֵיהּ, מִידֵּי דְּהָוֵה אַטֶּבֶל, הֶקְדֵּשׁ, וְקֻוֽנָּמוֹת.
> **Translation:** In fact, **this is comparable only to** a case involving **a piece** of meat **that** is **definitely** forbidden **fat, and one witness comes and says: It is clear to me that it is** permitted **fat,** as the *halakha* is **that he is not deemed credible.** The Gemara refutes this claim: **Is it comparable? There,** when it is established as forbidden fat, **even** if **one hundred** witnesses **come they are not deemed credible. Here, since if two** witnesses **come** and say the husband is dead **they** would be **deemed credible, let us also deem one** witness **credible.** This is **just as it is in** the case of **untithed produce,** i.e., produce from which neither *teruma* nor tithe has been separated, **consecrated** property, **and *konamot*,** an alternative term for offerings [*korbanot*] used in vows creating prohibitions. Such vows are called by the generic term: *Konamot*.

Can this analogy actually sustain the case of the woman? The *Gemara* objects: *hatam lo itchazek issura* — in the meat case, the prohibition was never established; we simply did not know what category the piece fell into. *Hacha itchazek issura d'eshet ish* — here we know for certain that this woman was married. Furthermore, *ein davar she-be'erva pachot mishnayim* — in cases that touch on sexual prohibitions, we require no fewer than two witnesses. These two objections are related: once we say *itchazek issura d'eshet ish*, we are in the domain of *arayot*, and therefore we cannot rely on a single witness.

The objection can be sharpened further. Our case does not resemble *safek chelev safek shuman* at all. It more closely resembles a case of *vadai chelev* — a piece of fat that is definitely forbidden — where a single witness comes and says it is actually permitted. In that case, *d'lo meheiman* — the single witness is not believed.

One might ask: is it not the case that even where *itchazek issura* applies, a single witness would be ineffective? Yes — and this is precisely the point. Even the case of *safek chelev* allows a single witness only because there is no established prohibition. The moment something is *vadai chelev* or *itchazek issura*, no number of witnesses can overturn the established fact through testimony alone. The only exception would be personal knowledge — if you yourself saw a piece of meat cut from the animal's kidney, no hundred witnesses can tell you otherwise, because it is your direct knowledge, not testimony at all, that establishes the *issur*.

This leads to a further observation from the *Gemara*: *hatam afilu bei mea lo meheimni* — in the case of *vadai chelev*, even a hundred witnesses cannot permit it. But *hacha keivan d'ki atu bei trei meheimni* — in the case of the woman, if two witnesses came and said her husband is dead, we would believe them. Therefore, *chad nami meheiman* — perhaps we should believe a single witness as well, just as we do in the analogous cases of *tevel*, *hekdesh*, and *konamot*.

### Tevel, Hekdesh, Konamot

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַאי טֶבֶל, הֵיכִי דָמֵי? אִי דִּידֵיהּ — מִשּׁוּם דִּבְיָדוֹ לְתַקְּנוֹ. אֶלָּא דְּאַחֵר, מַאי קָסָבַר:
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: With regard to **this** case of **untithed produce, what are the circumstances? If** it **is his,** and he testifies that *terumot* and tithes have been separated from it, he should be deemed credible **because it is within his power to prepare** the produce for consumption by separating tithes whenever he wishes. **Rather,** you must say that he testifies with regard to untithed produce **of another,** but if so, **what does** the anonymous Sage who cited this example **hold** in this case?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי קָא סָבַר: תּוֹרֵם מִשֶּׁלּוֹ עַל שֶׁל חֲבֵרוֹ אֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ דַּעַת בְּעָלִים — מִשּׁוּם דִּבְיָדוֹ לְתַקְּנוֹ, וְאִי קָסָבַר: צָרִיךְ דַּעַת בְּעָלִים, וְאָמַר: אֲנָא יָדַעְנָא בֵּיהּ דִּמְתַקַּן, הִיא גּוּפַהּ מְנָלַן?
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates: **If** he **holds** that **one who separates** tithes **from his** produce **for** that **of another does not require the owner’s knowledge,** and he can prepare his friend’s produce for consumption whenever he chooses, in this case too his testimony is deemed credible **because it is within his power to prepare it. And if he holds** that **the owner’s knowledge is required** before someone else can separate the gifts, and this is referring to a situation where the witness comes **and says: I know with regard to it that it is prepared,** in that case, **it itself,** this very *halakha*, **from where do we** derive it? Why is the case of untithed produce more obvious than the testimony with regard to a missing husband?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֶקְדֵּשׁ נָמֵי, אִי קְדוּשַּׁת דָּמִים — מִשּׁוּם דִּבְיָדוֹ לִפְדּוֹתוֹ. אִי קְדוּשַּׁת הַגּוּף, אִי דִּידֵיהּ — מִשּׁוּם דִּבְיָדוֹ לְאִיתְּשׁוֹלֵי עֲלֵיהּ. אֶלָּא דְּאַחֵר, וְאָמַר: יָדַעְנָא בֵּיהּ דְּאִיתְּשִׁיל מָרֵיהּ עֲלֵיהּ, הִיא גּוּפַהּ מְנָלַן?
> **Translation:** Similarly, with regard to **consecrated** property **too,** if it is merely **sanctity** that inheres in its **value,** i.e., it is not an actual offering but an item that has been dedicated to the Temple upkeep, then the reason why the testimony of one witness who says it is not consecrated is accepted is **due to** the fact that **it is within his power to redeem it.** And **if** this is referring to **inherent sanctity,** the matter still remains to be clarified: **If** it is **his** offering, then the reason is **due to** the fact that **it is within his power to request** from a Sage **that** the vow be dissolved, like any other vow. **Rather,** you must say that it is referring to the offering **of another, and he said: I know with regard to it that its owner requested** from a Sage **that** his vow be dissolved. However, here too, in this case **itself, from where do we** derive that he is deemed credible?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קֻוֽנָּמוֹת נָמֵי, אִי קָסָבַר: יֵשׁ מְעִילָה בְּקֻוֽנָּמוֹת, וּקְדוּשַּׁת דָּמִים נָחֲתָא לְהוּ — מִשּׁוּם דִּבְיָדוֹ לִפְדּוֹתוֹ, וְאִי קָסָבַר: אֵין מְעִילָה בְּקֻוֽנָּמוֹת, וְאִיסּוּר בְּעָלְמָא הוּא דְּרָכֵיב לְהוּ אַכַּתְפֵּיהּ, אִי דִּידֵיהּ — מִשּׁוּם דִּבְיָדוֹ לְאִיתְּשׁוֹלֵי עֲלֵיהּ,
> **Translation:** In the case of ***konamot* too, if** he **holds** that **there is misuse** of consecrated objects **with regard to *konamot*,** i.e., he holds that articles sanctified by a *konam* have the status of consecrated property, **and** that the **sanctity** that inheres in its **value applies to them,** then his claim is accepted **because it is within his power to redeem it. And if** he **maintains** that **there is no misuse** of consecrated objects **in** the case of ***konamot*, and it is an ordinary prohibition that rides on its shoulders,** i.e., it is forbidden due to its similarity to consecrated property despite the fact that is not fully sacred, even in this case the above argument applies: **If** the property in question **is his,** it is permitted **because it is within his power to request** from a Sage **that** his vow be dissolved.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא דְּאַחֵר, וְאָמַר: אֲנָא יָדַעְנָא דְּאִיתְּשִׁיל מָרֵיהּ עֲלֵיהּ — הִיא גּוּפַהּ מְנָלַן?
> **Translation:** **Rather,** you will say that the *konam* must belong **to another, and he said: I know with regard to it that its owner requested** from a Sage **that** his vow be dissolved. However, with regard to this *halakha* **itself,** that one witness is deemed credible in this case, **from where do we** derive it? Consequently, after the Gemara has refuted these attempts to explain why one witness should be deemed credible, the question remains: Why is the testimony of a single witness accepted in the case of a missing husband?

The *Gemara* now examines each of these three parallel cases in detail to see if any of them can actually serve as the source or model for the woman's case.

Regarding *tevel* — grain from which tithes have not yet been separated — suppose we have a basket that may not have been tithed, and a single witness comes and says it has been properly separated. The *Gemara* asks: *ei dideih* — if the witness is the owner himself, then we understand why he is believed: *mishum d'beyado l'takno* — it is within his power to fix it at any moment, and since he could always tithe it, we treat his assertion that he already did so as credible. But the case that actually resembles ours must involve a witness speaking about *someone else's* property. In that case, the analysis depends on a *machloket*: can one tithe on behalf of another person using one's own grain, without the owner's consent? If so, then *mishum d'beyado l'takno* — this witness himself could effect the remedy at any moment, and so he has a degree of standing analogous to ownership. But if the owner's knowledge is required, then the witness is simply making an assertion about a fact he did not himself create — and we are back to the question of where we get the authority to believe him.

The same difficulty recurs with *hekdesh*. If something has been consecrated to the Temple and a witness says it has been redeemed, we can explain the case of *kedushat damim* — monetary consecration — by saying *mishum deyadei liftoto*: the owner can always effect a redemption. And even for *kedushat haguf* — where the animal itself has been consecrated for a *korban* — if it is the owner's own animal, *mishum deyadei leishalei alei*, he can always go to a *talmid chacham* and have the *hekdesh* annulled. But once again, the case that resembles ours is where a third party comes and says the owner went to a *talmid chacham* and cleared the animal of its consecrated status. And *hi gufa minlan* — from where do we derive that such a witness is believed?

The same pattern plays out with *konamot*. A *konam* can operate in two ways: it can render something forbidden to oneself, or it can render one's own property forbidden to others. In either case, if it is one's own *konam*, the owner can seek annulment at any time, which explains why a single witness who happens to be the owner is credited. But the moment we move to a witness testifying about someone else's *konam* — that the owner has already sought annulment — we once again reach *hi gufa minlan*. Each time the analysis is brought to the point that actually resembles our case, the underlying logic collapses.

It is notable that all of this reasoning has been *stama d'Talmuda* — anonymous throughout. Every one of these exchanges represents the back-and-forth of the *Gemara*'s own anonymous layer of analysis, and no solution has been found.

### Agunah Prevention

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי זֵירָא: מִתּוֹךְ חוֹמֶר שֶׁהֶחְמַרְתָּ עָלֶיהָ בְּסוֹפָהּ — הֵקַלְתָּ עָלֶיהָ בַּתְּחִלָּה. לָא לַיחְמַיר וְלָא לַיקֵּיל!
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira said: Due to the stringency that you were stringent with her,** the woman who married on the basis of a single witness, **at the end,** i.e., if it turns out that the testimony was incorrect and the husband is still alive, the *halakha* is very severe with her and she loses out in all regards, **you are lenient with her at the beginning,** by accepting the testimony of a single witness to enable the woman to marry. The Gemara suggests: If so, let us **not be stringent** at the end **and not be lenient** at the beginning.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִשּׁוּם עִיגּוּנָא אַקִּילוּ בַּהּ רַבָּנַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Due to** the case of **a deserted wife, the Sages were lenient with her.** Since it is not always easy to find two witnesses to attest to a husband’s death, the Sages realized that if the testimony of one witness were not accepted, the woman would be likely to remain a deserted wife, unable to remarry. However, to prevent this leniency from causing mistakes and licentiousness, they were very stringent with her in a case where the testimony is found to be erroneous, to ensure that she is very careful not to accept untrustworthy accounts.

Now, for the first time, a named *amora* steps forward with a direct answer. *Amar Rabbi Zeira: mitoch chomer she'hichmarta aleha besofah hekaltah aleha betechilah.* Because the consequences at the end are so severe — because she must leave both husbands, forfeit her *ketubah*, and face all the attendant penalties — the rabbis did not need to make life so difficult for her at the beginning. Knowing full well that she stands to lose everything if she acts precipitously, she can be trusted to wait until she is genuinely certain that her husband is dead before proceeding to remarry. Therefore the rabbis permitted reliance on a single witness.

The *Gemara* responds tartly: *lo tachmir velo takal* — do not make it harder at the end and thereby justify making it easier at the beginning. Leave well enough alone; the cost is too high.

But the final answer is: *mishum aguna ikilu bah rabanan*. Ultimately, the rabbis were lenient with her out of concern for the *agunah*. Without this leniency, a woman whose husband disappears and is presumed dead would have no legal recourse and would remain chained for the rest of her life. It is important to note that this type of *igun* — where a husband has actually died or disappeared — is categorically different from the *agunah* crisis of the modern era, which involves husbands who are known to be alive yet refuse to deliver a *get*. The halakhic solutions that have been developed for the former have far less application to the latter.

## Husband Returns



### Rav's Position

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תֵּצֵא מִזֶּה וּמִזֶּה וְכוּ׳. אָמַר רַב: לֹא שָׁנוּ אֶלָּא שֶׁנִּיסֵּת בְּעֵד אֶחָד, אֲבָל נִיסֵּת עַל פִּי שְׁנֵי עֵדִים — לֹא תֵּצֵא. מַחֲכוּ עֲלֵיהּ בְּמַעְרְבָא: אֲתָא גַּבְרָא וְקָאֵי, וְאַתְּ אָמְרַתְּ לֹא תֵּצֵא? לָא צְרִיכָא, דְּלָא יָדְעִינַן לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that if she was informed that her husband was dead and she married another man, and her husband later returned, **she must leave this one and this one. Rav said: They taught** this *halakha* **only if she married by** virtue of the testimony of **one witness, but** if **she married on the basis of** the testimony of **two witnesses, she does not** have to **leave** him. **They laughed at him in the West,** Eretz Yisrael: **The man,** the first husband, **has come and stands** before us, **and** yet **you say she does not** have to **leave** her second husband. The Gemara explains: **No,** it is **necessary** in a situation **when we do not know** the man who comes before us claiming to be the first husband.

The *Mishnah* continues: if the husband returns after she has remarried, *tetze mizeh umizeh* — she must leave both the second husband and the first. At this point, *Rav* makes a striking statement: *lo shanu ela she'niset be'ed echad, aval niset al pi shenei edim lo tetze.* Rav holds that the rule of *tzet mizeh umizeh* applies only when she remarried on the basis of a single witness. But if two witnesses came and testified that her husband was dead, and she married on that basis — she does not have to leave her second husband.

In *Eretz Yisrael*, they laughed at this: *machku aleha bema'arava.* The man is standing right in front of you, and you are telling her she may remain with the second husband?

### Gemara's Objection

The *Gemara* formulates the objection directly: *ata gavra veka'i* — the man has come and is standing before you. *Ve'at amart lo tetze?* And you are saying she does not need to leave?

### Identity Recognition

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי דְּלָא יָדְעִינַן לֵיהּ, בְּעֵד אֶחָד אַמַּאי תֵּצֵא? לָא צְרִיכָא, דַּאֲתוֹ בֵּי תְרֵי וְאָמְרִי: אֲנַן הֲוֵינַן בַּהֲדֵיהּ מִכִּי נְפַק וְעַד הַשְׁתָּא, וְאַתּוּן הוּא דְּלָא יָדְעִיתוּ לֵיהּ, דִּכְתִיב: ״וַיַּכֵּר יוֹסֵף אֶת אֶחָיו וְהֵם לֹא הִכִּירוּהוּ״, וְאָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁיָּצָא בְּלֹא חֲתִימַת זָקָן, וּבָא בַּחֲתִימַת זָקָן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If we do not know him,** even if she married **by one witness, why** should she **leave?** The testimony of the witness who says the husband is dead should be accepted. The Gemara answers: **No,** it is **necessary** for a case **when two** others **came and said: We were with him from when he left until now, and it is you who do not recognize him,** as his appearance has changed over the course of time. This is **as it is written: “And Joseph recognized his brothers but they did not recognize him”** (Genesis 42:8), **and Rav Ḥisda said** that this verse **teaches that** Joseph **left** his brothers **without a full beard, and he came with a full beard,** which is why they failed to recognize him. This shows that one’s appearance can change so much over time that even his own family members are unable to identify him.

The *Gemara* answers: *lo tzericha* — Rav's ruling is still necessary. The case is one of *d'la yad'ina leh* — we do not recognize this person. Someone is claiming to be the long-lost husband, but there is no positive identification. The *Gemara* presses: if we do not recognize him at all, why would she even need to leave her second husband in the case where she married on the basis of a single witness? It answers: the ruling is needed for the case where two witnesses come and say, *anan havainan bahadei mikai nafak v'ad hashta* — "We have been with him continuously from the time he left until this very moment; it is you who fail to recognize him."

The *Gemara* supports this with the verse describing Yosef's reunion with his brothers: *vayaker Yosef et echav ve-hem lo hikiruhu* — Yosef recognized his brothers but they did not recognize him. *Rav Hisda* explains: he left before the seal of a beard had formed on his face, and he returned with it. The broader point is clear: it is entirely plausible that a person could return after a long absence and not be recognized, whether because of physical changes, the passage of time, or the disappearance of those who originally knew him.

### Conflicting Testimony

The *Gemara* then raises a further difficulty. Even granting all of this — even accepting that the two witnesses who accompany the returning husband are credible — we now have *trei v'trei ninhu*: two witnesses who said the husband was dead against two witnesses who say he is alive and present. This creates a *safek*, and anyone who now sleeps with this woman would be obligated in an *asham talui* — the conditional guilt offering for uncertain sin. How then can Rav say *lo tetze*, that she remains with the second husband, when every act of marital relations generates a *safek* requiring an *asham talui*?

[DAF:88b]
### Rav Sheshet's Solution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סוֹף סוֹף תְּרֵי וּתְרֵי נִינְהוּ,
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: Even in this case, **ultimately they are two** against **two.** Initially, two witnesses testified that the man was dead, and now another pair arrives saying he is alive. Why should the testimony of the witnesses who say he is dead be accepted, allowing her to remain with the second husband, while other witnesses claim he is still alive?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַבָּא עָלֶיהָ בְּאָשָׁם תָּלוּי קָאֵי! אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: כְּגוֹן שֶׁנִּיסֵּת לְאֶחָד מֵעֵדֶיהָ.
> **Translation:** **And** as this is an uncertain case, **one who has intercourse with her stands** obligated to bring **an uncertain guilt-**offering. Since before she remarried some witnesses say she is a married woman while others claim she is now a widow, her relations with her second husband involve a possible prohibition that entails *karet*, and whoever mistakenly performs an action of this kind is liable to bring an uncertain guilt-offering. If so, this second husband must certainly divorce her. **Rav Sheshet said:** We are dealing with a case **where** that woman **married one of her witnesses,** who testified that her husband had died. Since the witness himself has no doubt as to the truth, he is not liable to bring an uncertain guilt-offering.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הִיא גּוּפַהּ בְּאָשָׁם תָּלוּי קָיְימָא! בְּאוֹמֶרֶת ״בָּרִי לִי״. אִי הָכִי, מַאי לְמֵימְרָא? אֲפִילּוּ רַבִּי מְנַחֵם בְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי לָא קָאָמַר אֶלָּא כְּשֶׁבָּאוּ עֵדִים וְאַחַר כָּךְ נִיסֵּת, אֲבָל נִיסֵּת וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּאוּ עֵדִים — לָא אָמַר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: Even so, **she herself stands** obligated to bring **a uncertain guilt-**offering, as she has no personal knowledge of the matter and relied on the witness. The Gemara answers: It is referring to a case **where she says: It is clear to me.** For whatever reason, she is certain that this is not her husband and that he is dead, and therefore she too is not liable to bring an uncertain guilt-offering. The Gemara asks: **If so, what** is the purpose **of stating** this? That is, if Rav is referring only to this particular case, he has not taught anything new, as **even Rabbi Menaḥem, son of Rabbi Yosei, stated** his opinion **only** with regard to a case **where witnesses came and afterward** she **married, but** in the case where she **married and afterward witnesses came, he did not state** his *halakha* with regard to this case.

*Rav Sheshet* offers a solution: *kegon she-nisset l'achad me-adeha* — the case is one where the woman married one of the original witnesses who testified to her husband's death. This is relevant because an *asham talui* is not an offering that comes from external legal pressure; it reflects a person's own internal doubt about whether they have sinned. If she married the very witness who established the fact of the husband's death, that witness himself has no doubt whatsoever — he was there. He does not feel any uncertainty, and therefore an *asham talui* simply does not apply to him.

The *Gemara* pushes further: even if the man does not have a doubt, *hi gufa be'asham talui* — what about her? Does she not still feel uncertain? The answer is: *be'omeret bari li* — she herself says, "I am certain that he is dead." Rashi explains that the witnesses produced convincing signs — an item that only the husband would have had, for example — that left her with no personal doubt.

### Temporal Sequence

The *Gemara* then raises the question of *novelty*: if the case requires both that she married one of her witnesses and that she herself is certain the husband is dead, what is the *chiddush* of Rav's ruling? Even *Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi* — who takes the strictest position — confines his ruling to a specific scenario. *Afilu Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi lo ka'amar ela sheba'u eidim ve'achar kach niset* — even he only rules strictly when both sets of witnesses appeared *before* she married. But if she married first and only then did contradicting witnesses emerge, *Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi* would also not require her to leave.

### Baraita Dispute

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא: שְׁנַיִם אוֹמְרִים ״מֵת״ וּשְׁנַיִם אוֹמְרִים ״לָא מֵת״, שְׁנַיִם אוֹמְרִים ״נִתְגָּרְשָׁה״ וּשְׁנַיִם אוֹמְרִים ״לֹא נִתְגָּרְשָׁה״ — הֲרֵי זוֹ לֹא תִּנָּשֵׂא, וְאִם נִיסֵּת — לֹא תֵּצֵא. רַבִּי מְנַחֵם בְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי אָמַר: תֵּצֵא. אָמַר רַבִּי מְנַחֵם בְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי: אֵימַת אֲנִי אוֹמֵר תֵּצֵא? בִּזְמַן שֶׁבָּאוּ עֵדִים וְאַחַר כָּךְ נִיסֵּת. אֲבָל נִיסֵּת וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּאוּ עֵדִים — הֲרֵי זוֹ לֹא תֵּצֵא!
> **Translation:** To what ruling is the Gemara referring? **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: If **two** witnesses **say** that the husband is **dead and two say** he is **not dead,** or if **two say** that this woman **was divorced and two say** she **was not divorced, this** woman **may not marry; and if she married** regardless, she need **not leave** her new husband, as there is no uncontroverted testimony that she is forbidden to him. **Rabbi Menaḥem, son of Rabbi Yosei, said:** She must **leave** him. **Rabbi Menaḥem, son of Rabbi Yosei,** further **said: When** do **I say** she must **leave** him? **When the witnesses** who contradicted the first pair by claiming she is still married **came and afterward she married,** despite their testimony. **However,** if **she married and afterward** the second pair of **witnesses came, this** woman need **not leave** her second husband.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי קָאָמַר רַב נָמֵי, בִּזְמַן שֶׁבָּאוּ עֵדִים וְאַחַר כָּךְ נִיסֵּת. לְאַפּוֹקֵי מִדְּרַבִּי מְנַחֵם בְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי. וְאִיכָּא דְּאָמַר: טַעְמָא דְּנִיסֵּת וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּאוּ עֵדִים, אֲבָל בָּאוּ עֵדִים וְאַחַר כָּךְ נִיסֵּת — תֵּצֵא. כְּמַאן — כְּרַבִּי מְנַחֵם בְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **When Rav spoke too,** he was referring to a case **when** the second pair of **witnesses came** and testified that this is her husband, **and afterward she married.** Rav claims that even in that case she need not leave her second husband. His ruling serves **to exclude** the opinion of **Rabbi Menaḥem, son of Rabbi Yosei,** in favor of that of the first *tanna*. **And some say** that Rav’s teaching should be understood as follows: **The reason is that she married and afterward witnesses came; however,** if **witnesses came and afterward she married,** she must **leave** him. **According to whose** opinion is this ruling of Rav? It is **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Menaḥem, son of Rabbi Yosei.**


The *Gemara* cites a *baraita* to clarify this. Two witnesses say the husband is dead; two witnesses say he is not. Two witnesses say she was divorced; two witnesses say she was not. *Harei zo lo tinasei* — she should not marry, since she is a *safek*. *Ve'im niset, lo tetze* — but if she did marry, she need not leave, say the *Rabbanan*. *Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi omer tetzei* — Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi says she must leave.

*Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi* clarifies his own position: *eimatai ani omer tetzei? Bizman sheba'u eidim ve'achar kach niset.* He requires her to leave only when both sets of contradicting witnesses appeared *before* she married. If she knew going in that the situation was a *safek*, and she married anyway, she cannot now claim the protection of *lo tetze*. But if she married first and only afterward did contradicting witnesses arrive, even he would agree she need not leave.

It is worth pausing here to note how surprising it is that the case we are dealing with — in which the woman marries one of her own witnesses — is treated as legally viable at all. The question arises naturally: does this not resemble the principle that a man who causes rumors about a married woman, and whose conduct leads to her divorce, must then give her up even if he subsequently married her? The concern there is that the whole situation has the appearance of a scheme — and something similar seems to apply here, where it looks as though the witness fabricated his testimony precisely in order to marry her. That the *Gemara* nonetheless treats her continued marriage as a live question is, on reflection, quite striking.

Rav's ruling is therefore not merely a restatement of what all *Tannaim* agree on. There are two ways to read Rav. According to the first reading (*yesh omrim*), Rav's case is one where she married *after* the contradicting witnesses appeared, and he rules *lo tetze* — unlike *Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi*. On this reading, Rav is announcing that the *halacha* does not follow *Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi*. According to the second reading, Rav's ruling applies to the case where she married *before* the contradicting witnesses appeared, meaning Rav actually holds *like* Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi — his *chiddush* being simply that the *halacha* follows that position.

### Kohen Case Challenge

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵתִיב רָבָא: מִנַּיִן שֶׁאִם לֹא רָצָה — דׇּפְנוֹ, תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וְקִדַּשְׁתּוֹ״, בְּעַל כׇּרְחוֹ.
> **Translation:** **Rava raised an objection** from a *baraita*: **From where** is it derived **that if** a priest **does not want** to observe the strictures against disqualified women, that the court **forces him [*dafno*]** by flogging him, and it sanctifies him despite his wishes? **The verse states,** at the end of the chapter that deals with the prohibitions of the priesthood: **“And you shall sanctify him**…he shall be sacred for you” (Leviticus 21:8), which indicates that this is performed even **against his will.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֵיכִי דָמֵי, אִילֵּימָא דְּלָא נִיסֵּת לְאֶחָד מֵעֵדֶיהָ וְלֹא קָאָמְרָה ״בָּרִי לִי״, צְרִיכָא לְמֵימַר דְּדׇפְנוֹ? אֶלָּא לָאו, דְּנִיסֵּת לְאֶחָד מֵעֵדֶיהָ, וְקָאָמְרָה ״בָּרִי לִי״, וְקָתָנֵי דׇּפְנוֹ. אַלְמָא, מַפְּקִינַן לַהּ מִינֵּיהּ!
> **Translation:** Rava analyzes this *baraita*: **What are the circumstances?** Assuming that this is referring to a priest who married a woman whose status as a divorced woman or a *zona* is uncertain, **if we say that she did not marry one of her witnesses and did not say: It is clear to me,** that the opposing witnesses were speaking the truth, **need this be said** that the court **forces him?** Since both of them are clearly in violation of a transgression, it is obvious that they must be separated. **Rather, is it not** referring to a case **when she married one of her witnesses and she says: It is clear to me, and** even so the *baraita* **is teaching** that the court **forces him? Apparently,** the *halakha* is that **they remove her from him,** which contradicts Rav’s opinion that one who married based on the testimony of two witnesses need not leave her second husband.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיסּוּר כְּהוּנָּה שָׁאנֵי. וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: מַאי דׇּפְנוֹ — דׇּפְנוֹ בְּעֵדִים. וְאִבָּעֵית אֵימָא: כְּשֶׁבָּאוּ עֵדִים וְאַחַר כָּךְ נִיסֵּת, וְרַבִּי מְנַחֵם בְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי הִיא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **A prohibition** with regard **to the priesthood is different,** as uncertainties with regard to priests are treated stringently, as though they were definite. **And if you wish, say: What is** the meaning of the phrase: The court **forces him?** It means that it **forces him by witnesses.** In other words, only if another pair of witnesses comes and clarifies the matter does the court prevent the marriage *ab initio*. However, if she has already married she need not leave her husband. **And if you wish, say** instead that it is referring to a situation **when witnesses came and afterward she married, and** this *baraita*, which claims that the court forces him to divorce her in that case, **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Menaḥem, son of Rabbi Yosei.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אָשֵׁי אוֹמֵר: מַאי ״לֹא תֵּצֵא״ דְּקָאָמַר רַב — לֹא תֵּצֵא מֵהֶיתֵּירָהּ הָרִאשׁוֹן.
> **Translation:** **Rav Ashi said: What is** the meaning of the phrase: She need **not leave** him, **that Rav said?** It means that she need **not leave her** state of **being permitted** to her **first** husband. Since she married according to *halakha*, on the basis of witness testimony, she is considered to have acted under duress. Like any other woman not married to a priest who was unfaithful against her will, she may return to her first husband upon his return.

*Rava* raises an objection to Rav from a different *baraita*. The case is a *kohen* who has married a woman forbidden to him — a *gerushah*, for example. The *baraita* asks: from where do we know that if he refuses to divorce her, we compel him? The verse says *ve-kidashto* — "you shall sanctify him": he is made holy whether he wishes it or not. Now, *heichi dami* — what is the scenario of this *baraita*? If it is the simple case without the complications of contradicting witnesses and personal certainty, the *kohen*'s obligation to divorce is obvious; no *baraita* is needed. It must therefore be referring to the more complex case — one where she did marry one of her witnesses and she herself declared certainty about her husband's death. And yet the *baraita* says we compel him. This seems to be a direct challenge to Rav, who said *lo tetze*.

### Kohen Exception

The *Gemara* offers several possible resolutions. First: *issur kehunah shani* — the *kohen* case is categorically different. A *kohen* faces additional prohibitions that do not apply to a regular *Yisrael*, and therefore even a doubt that would not require dissolution of an ordinary marriage is sufficient to require dissolution of a *kohen*'s marriage.

Second: *mai dafnu? Dafnu be-eidim.* What does it mean to "compel" him in the *baraita*? Rashi explains that this refers not to forcing him to give a *get* after the fact, but rather to preventing the marriage in the first place — by producing witnesses who will complicate the situation and demonstrate that she may in fact still be married, thereby deterring him from marrying her at all. But once the marriage has occurred, we would not necessarily dissolve it.

Third: *kasheba'u eidim ve'achar kach niset ve-Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi hi* — perhaps this *baraita* represents the position of *Rabbi Menachem Berabbi Yosi* specifically, in the scenario where the contradicting witnesses appeared before she married. Rav may simply hold differently.

## Rav Ashi & Shmuel



### Rav Ashi's Reinterpret

*Rav Ashi* now offers a fundamentally different reading of Rav's original ruling. *Mai lo tetze deka'amar Rav? Lo tetze meheterah harishon.* When Rav said *lo tetze*, he did not mean she stays with the second husband. He meant she does not lose her permitted status with respect to the *first* husband. In other words, when two witnesses testified that her husband was dead and she married on that basis, and the husband subsequently returns — she is not *asurah* to the first husband. She may go back to him. She is *asurah* to the second husband, whose marriage has been nullified, but her original marriage is very much intact.

### Mishna's Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא אַמְרַהּ רַב חֲדָא זִימְנָא, דִּתְנַן: נִיסֵּת שֶׁלֹּא בִּרְשׁוּת — מוּתֶּרֶת לַחְזוֹר לוֹ. וְאָמַר רַב הוּנָא אֲמַר רַב: הָכִי הִלְכְתָא! חֲדָא מִכְּלָלָא דַּחֲבֶרְתַּהּ אִיתְּמַר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: If that is what he meant, **Rav** already **said it once. As we learned** in the mishna: If she **married without** the **consent** of the court, but rather by witnesses’ testimony, it is **permitted** for **her to return to him. And Rav Huna said** that **Rav said: That is** the ***halakha*.** Evidently, Rav already ruled that she does not forfeit her original permitted status. The Gemara answers: **One** ruling **was stated by inference from the other.** In other words, Rav did not state both *halakhot* explicitly, but only one of them, from which his other statement was inferred.

The *Gemara* then asks: but Rav already said this once — why does he need to say it a second time? *Detnan: niset shelo birshut muteret lachzor lo* — the *Mishnah* states that if she married *shelo bi-reshut* (understood here to mean on the basis of two witnesses, without needing the permission of *beit din*), she is permitted to return to the first husband. And *Rav Huna* reported that Rav ruled that the *halacha* follows this *Mishnah*. So Rav has already told us she can return to the first husband. Why restate it?

The answer is: *chada miklala de-chavreita itmar* — one ruling was derived from the other. Once we know the *halacha* follows the end of the *Mishnah* — that she can return to her first husband — it follows as a corollary that she does not forfeit her original *heter*.

### Shmuel's Condition

*Shmuel* now enters with his own formulation: *lo shanu ela shelo machchishto, aval machchishto lo tetze.* What we have been discussing applies only when the woman does *not* contradict the returning man. If the husband returns and she contradicts him — she says, "You are not my husband; I do not know you" — then she does not have to leave her second husband and may remain with him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר שְׁמוּאֵל: לֹא שָׁנוּ אֶלָּא שֶׁלֹּא מַכְחַשְׁתּוֹ, אֲבָל מַכְחַשְׁתּוֹ — לֹא תֵּצֵא. בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן? אִילֵימָא בְּבֵי תְרֵי, כִּי מַכְחֲשָׁא לֵיהּ מַאי הָוֵי! אֶלָּא בְּחַד, טַעְמָא דְּמַכְחַשְׁתּוֹ, הָא שָׁתְקָה — תֵּצֵא.
> **Translation:** **Shmuel said: They taught** that she must leave her second husband **only if she does not contradict** the witness who claims her first husband is alive. **However,** if **she contradicts him,** she need **not leave** her second husband. The Gemara inquires: **With what are we dealing** here? **If we say** we are dealing **with two** witnesses who testified that her husband is still alive, even **if she contradicts him, what of it?** The testimony of the two witnesses is fully accepted. **Rather,** it must be referring **to one** witness, from which it may be inferred that **the reason** that she need not leave her second husband **is that she contradicts him,** which indicates that **if she** remains **silent** and does not contradict his testimony, she must **leave** him.

### One vs. One Witness


The *Gemara* asks: *bemai askinan* — what is the scenario? If two witnesses come and say the husband is alive, then her denial is irrelevant; two witnesses cannot be contradicted by one person's claim. It must therefore be that a *single* witness comes and says the husband is alive. So the *Gemara* concludes: *ta'ama demachishatei* — the reason she stays with the second husband is that she contradicted the single witness. *Ha shetaka teitzei* — but if she had been silent, she would have had to leave, even on the basis of this single witness.

### Ulla's Principle

This prompts the *Gemara* to invoke *Ulla*'s principle: *kol makom she-he'emina Torah ed echad harei kan shnayim, ve-ein devarav shel echad bimkom shnayim.* Anywhere that the Torah has granted the reliability of a single witness, the Torah has not simply lowered the bar — it has elevated that witness to the status of two. So when the original single witness said the husband was dead and the woman was permitted to remarry on that basis, that witness now carries the weight of two. A single witness who subsequently says the husband is alive cannot override that, because one witness cannot stand against two.

One might ask: the *Gemara* earlier concluded that it was the *rabbis* who permitted reliance on a single witness, out of concern for the *agunah* — not the Torah itself. How then can *Ulla* invoke the language of *he'emina Torah*? The force of the objection is real, and the *Gemara* does not resolve it fully here. What seems to be operating is the understanding that even the rabbinic permission was grounded in a Torah-level framework, and that the leniency — once granted — confers the full status that the Torah accords to a single witness in contexts where it is permitted.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָאָמַר עוּלָּא: כׇּל מָקוֹם שֶׁהֶאֱמִינָה תּוֹרָה עֵד אֶחָד, הֲרֵי כָּאן שְׁנַיִם, וְאֵין דְּבָרָיו שֶׁל אֶחָד בִּמְקוֹם שְׁנַיִם! הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן — בִּפְסוּלֵי עֵדוּת, וְכִדְרַבִּי נְחֶמְיָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But didn’t Ulla say** that **wherever** you find **that the Torah relies on one witness, this is** a full testimony equal to that of **two** witnesses, **and the statement of one** witness **has no** bearing **in a place where** it is contradicted by **two** witnesses? If so, there is no difference between one witness and two witnesses in this case. The Gemara answers: **With what are we dealing here?** With a case where the pair who said he was alive were **disqualified** from giving **testimony, and** this is **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Neḥemya.**

### Disqualified Witnesses


The *Gemara* then asks: what exactly is the case where someone comes after the fact and says the husband is alive? If it is a *kosher* single witness against the original *kosher* single witness who now has the status of two — the later witness can obviously not override. So the *Gemara* answers: *bipsulei edut* — the case is where two disqualified witnesses, such as two women or two relatives, come and say the husband is alive. *Ucherabbi Nechemiah* — and this is in accordance with the view of *Rabbi Nechemia*.

### Rabbi Nechemia's Rule

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי נְחֶמְיָה אוֹמֵר: כׇּל מָקוֹם שֶׁהֶאֱמִינָה תּוֹרָה עֵד אֶחָד — הַלֵּךְ אַחַר רוֹב דֵּעוֹת. וְעָשׂוּ שְׁתֵּי נָשִׁים בְּאִישׁ אֶחָד כִּשְׁנֵי אֲנָשִׁים בְּאִישׁ אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** **As it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Neḥemya says: Wherever** you find that **the Torah relies on one witness, follow the majority of opinions.** If the testimony is valid, the account of two witnesses is the same as one hundred, as no greater credence is granted to the larger number. However, when the testimony is invalid the majority opinion is accepted. **And** in these cases **they established** the testimony of **two women against one man like** the testimony of **two men against one man,** whose claim is not considered equivalent to two. In this case, if the wife also contradicts their account she joins the single witness, and therefore the testimony of the disqualified witnesses is not accepted.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: כֹּל הֵיכָא דַּאֲתָא עֵד אֶחָד כָּשֵׁר מֵעִיקָּרָא, אֲפִילּוּ מֵאָה נָשִׁים — כְּעֵד אֶחָד דָּמְיָין. הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן, כְּגוֹן דְּאָתְיָא אִשָּׁה מֵעִיקָּרָא.
> **Translation:** **And if you wish, say** instead that **anywhere that one valid witness came initially** and testified that the husband was dead, **even one hundred women** claiming that he is alive **are considered like one** witness, and their contrary account is not accepted. And **with what are we dealing here?** In a case **where a woman came** and testified **initially.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתָרְצַהּ לִדְרַבִּי נְחֶמְיָה הָכִי: רַבִּי נְחֶמְיָה אוֹמֵר: כׇּל מָקוֹם שֶׁהֶאֱמִינָה תּוֹרָה עֵד אֶחָד — הַלֵּךְ אַחַר רוֹב דֵּעוֹת, וְעָשׂוּ שְׁתֵּי נָשִׁים בְּאִשָּׁה אַחַת — כִּשְׁנֵי אֲנָשִׁים בְּאִישׁ אֶחָד אֲבָל שְׁתֵּי נָשִׁים בְּאִישׁ אֶחָד — כְּפַלְגָא וּפַלְגָא דָּמֵי.
> **Translation:** **And** you must accordingly **amend** the statement **of Rabbi Neḥemya** so that it reads **as follows: Rabbi Neḥemya says: Wherever** you find that **the Torah relies on one witness, follow the majority of opinions, and they established two women against one woman like two men against one man. However,** in a case involving **two women against one man** who is a valid witness, this **is like half against half,** i.e., they are equal. With regard to Shmuel’s statement, if the wife herself remains silent, the testimony of the first woman that the husband is dead has been negated, as her account was contradicted by two women. But if the claim of the wife is joined to that of the first woman she need not leave her second husband.

*Rabbi Nechemia* holds: *kol makom she-he'emina Torah ed echad halach achar rov de'ot* — in all cases where the Torah accepts a single witness, one follows the majority of opinions. Since the original single witness has the status of two, and the two disqualified witnesses also count as two opinions, one follows the larger number. *Ve-asu shtei nashim ke-ish echad* — and by this principle, two women count like one man in terms of testimony.

But this rule applies symmetrically only when the original witness was also a woman. *Aval shtei nashim be-ish echad — ke-palga u-palga dami* — if two women testify against a single male witness (who has the Torah-granted status of two), the result is half against half — a standoff. On the alternative reading proposed by the *Gemara*, the case is that the original witness was a woman from the outset, and the principle of *Rabbi Nechemia* is applied accordingly: two women testifying against one woman is treated like two men against one man.
