## Shinui Makom Debate



### Rav/Shmuel vs. Yochanan

The *daf* under discussion is 102, and the *sugya* continues its examination of *shinui makom* — whether changing one's location requires a new *bracha rishona*. The question arose specifically in the context of wine: if one recited *kiddush* over wine in one place, said *borei pri hagafen*, drank, even in the context of a meal (*bimkom seudah*), and then wished to continue drinking somewhere else — must a new *bracha* be recited? This is fundamentally a question in the laws of *brachot* generally, not specific to *Pesachim*.

The positions are as follows. *Rav* and *Shmuel* both ruled that a new *borei pri hagafen* is required. *Rabbi Yochanan* ruled that no new *bracha* is required. A *baraita* was previously cited as a challenge to *Rabbi Yochanan*, apparently demonstrating that *shinui makom* does require a new *bracha*. The *Gemara* then introduced a distinction, presenting a dispute between *Rav Sheshet* and *Rav Huna*.

### Rav Huna Position

*Rav Huna* held that a new *bracha* is required only when, in the original location, one was not eating a meal that would require a *bracha acharona* — such as *birkat hamazon* or *al hamichya* — to be recited in that same place. If one was indeed engaged in such a fixed meal, then even if one moved to a different location and continued eating or drinking there, it is not considered an entirely new experience. Since one is permitted — and indeed required — to return to the original location to recite *birkat hamazon*, the continuation is regarded as an extension of the primary eating done in the first place. In that scenario, no new *bracha* is required. *Rav Sheshet*, by contrast, held that whenever one switches locations, a new *bracha* is always required, regardless of the nature of the original meal.

### Leaving & Returning

The *Gemara* then examined a *baraita* involving a scenario not identical to the basic case — namely, people who left a meal and then returned. One might have assumed that everyone would agree that in such a case no new *bracha* is needed, yet the *Gemara* treated it as falling within the same framework of analysis.

The case presented in the *baraita*: people were seated, drinking wine and apparently eating a meal, and then they arose to go to the synagogue or to attend a wedding celebration (*chatan* and *kallah*). When they return, must they make a new *bracha*? The *baraita* rules that they do not need a new *bracha* — provided they left some people behind. From this the *Gemara* inferred: it appears that they had uprooted themselves (*akru ragleihem*) from what was clearly a substantial meal, likely bread, and yet the only reason they are exempted from a new *bracha* upon return is that they left people behind. This implies that under other circumstances, one would always require a new *bracha* upon changing location — even when having a fixed meal, and even when returning to the original place. This appeared to be a challenge to *Rav Huna*, whose position was precisely that a *seudah* exempts one from a new *bracha*.

### Hesach Hadaat

At this point it is worth noting how *tosafot* introduces the category of *hesach hadaat* into this discussion. The *Gemara* itself does not use the term *hesach hadaat* in this context — the analysis is framed in terms of the objective reality of place. The more precise way to understand the relationship is that *shinui makom* functions as an objective counterpart to *hesach hadaat*. Just as *hesach hadaat* — a subjective loss of focus or mental disengagement from one's eating — creates discontinuity that may require a new *bracha*, so too *shinui makom* creates an objective form of discontinuity. The two operate on parallel tracks: one is subjective (you fell asleep, or walked away to watch something entirely unrelated and forgot about your meal entirely), and the other is objective (you moved to a different location).

This distinction matters because one could be physically eating continuously — walking from room to room while still consuming food — and yet *shinui makom* might still, according to some views, require a new *bracha*. It is not that the *halacha* is inferring actual subjective disengagement from the fact of movement; rather, the change of place is treated as an independent objective criterion for discontinuity. And this is why more formal, objective criteria — such as whether one was *kovea seudah*, establishing a fixed meal in the original location — can enter the analysis: such a meal more firmly anchors the eating to the original place and makes subsequent eating more naturally viewed as an extension of that original experience.

The intersection between subjective intent and objective reality also bears on *shinui makom* more broadly. *Tosafot* elsewhere, in the context of *kiddush bimkom seudah*, discusses an intermediate case — such as the roof and floor of the same building — where *tosafot* holds that if one has the location in mind, it counts as a single place, but that moving from inside to outside cannot be overcome merely by intention. Different spaces within the same room require no special intention at all. So intent can assist in intermediate cases but cannot override a clear change of domain. How far positive subjective intent can help when an objective *shinui makom* has occurred is a genuine open question that runs through these *rishonim*.

### Chachamim Dispute

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יְהוּדָה, דְּתַנְיָא: חֲבֵרִים שֶׁהָיוּ מְסוּבִּין, וְעָקְרוּ רַגְלֵיהֶם לֵילֵךְ לְבֵית הַכְּנֶסֶת אוֹ לְבֵית הַמִּדְרָשׁ, כְּשֶׁהֵן יוֹצְאִין — אֵין טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְמַפְרֵעַ, וּכְשֶׁהֵן חוֹזְרִין — אֵין טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְכַתְּחִלָּה. אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: בַּמֶּה דְּבָרִים אֲמוּרִים — בִּזְמַן שֶׁהִנִּיחוּ שָׁם מִקְצָת חֲבֵרִים, אֲבָל לֹא הִנִּיחוּ שָׁם מִקְצָת חֲבֵרִים, כְּשֶׁהֵן יוֹצְאִין — טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְמַפְרֵעַ, וּכְשֶׁהֵן חוֹזְרִין — טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְכַתְּחִלָּה.
> **Translation:** **It is Rabbi Yehuda, as it was taught** in a *baraita*: With regard to **friends who were reclining** and eating a meal **and uprooted themselves to go to the synagogue or to the study hall, when they exit,** these foods **do not require a blessing** to be recited **afterward, and when they return, these foods do not require an introductory blessing** before resuming eating. **Rabbi Yehuda said: In what** case **is this statement said? When they left some of** the **friends there,** at the meal. **However, if they did not leave some of the friends there, when they exit,** these foods **require a blessing** to be recited **afterward, and when they return,** these foods **require an introductory blessing.** According to Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak, the *baraita* that served as the basis of the Gemara’s objection to the explanation of Rav Ḥisda actually represents the minority opinion of Rabbi Yehuda, whereas Rav Ḥisda holds in accordance with the majority opinion of the Rabbis.

At this point, *Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak* intervenes. He proposes that the *baraita* presenting the position that leaving without anyone behind requires a new *bracha* — represents the view of *Rabbi Yehuda* alone. There is an opposing Rabbinic majority, the *Chachamim*, who disagree. The *Gemara* introduces a *baraita* to support this:

*Tanu Rabbanan* — the Rabbis taught: *Chaveirim she-hayu mesubin* — a group of companions who were reclining and eating. The context involves *devarim ha-te'unim bracha acharona bimkomam* — foods that require a concluding *bracha* in the same location, i.e., a meal over which one would be obligated to recite *birkat hamazon* in that place, not incidentally (this is not the context of the *Seder*, for example, but an ordinary meal). *Ve-akru ragleihem leilech le-veit ha-knesset o le-veit ha-midrash* — and they uprooted themselves to go to the synagogue or the study hall. *Keshe-hein yotzin ein te'unim bracha le-mafrea* — when they leave, they are not required to recite a *bracha* on what they have already eaten, i.e., they need not recite *birkat hamazon* before departing. *U-keshe-hen chozrin ein te'unim bracha le-chatchila* — and when they return, they are not required to make a new *bracha rishona* before continuing their meal. They simply resume eating and recite *birkat hamazon* when they are done.

It is worth noting, as some *rishonim* point out, that part of the novelty (*chiddush*) of *ein te'unim bracha le-mafrea* is that we are not concerned they might forget to return. One might have thought that even if returning obviates the need for a new *bracha*, we would nevertheless require them to bench before leaving as a precaution against forgetting to come back. This *baraita* teaches that we do not impose that precaution.

### Seuda Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא טַעְמָא דְּבִדְבָרִים הַטְּעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְאַחֲרֵיהֶן בִּמְקוֹמָן, דִּכְשֶׁהֵן יוֹצְאִין — אֵין טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְמַפְרֵעַ, וּכְשֶׁהֵן חוֹזְרִין — אֵין טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְכַתְּחִלָּה. אֲבָל דְּבָרִים שֶׁאֵין טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְאַחֲרֵיהֶן בִּמְקוֹמָן, אֲפִילּוּ לְרַבָּנַן, כְּשֶׁהֵן יוֹצְאִין — טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְמַפְרֵעַ, וּכְשֶׁהֵן חוֹזְרִין — טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְכַתְּחִילָּה,
> **Translation:** The Gemara infers from the above *baraita*: **The reason** for this *halakha* is **that** it is only with regard to **items** of food **that require a blessing after them in their place, that when** the people eating them **exit,** these foods **do not require a blessing** to be recited **afterward, and when they return,** these foods **do not require an introductory blessing. However,** if they ate **items** of food **that do not require a blessing** that must be recited specifically **in their place, even according to** the opinion of **the Rabbis,** who disagree with Rabbi Yehuda, **when** these people **exit,** these foods **require a blessing** to be recited **afterward, and when they return** these foods **require an introductory blessing.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֵימָא תֶּיהְוֵי תְּיוּבְתָּא דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן! וְלָאו מִי אוֹתְבִינֵּיהּ חֲדָא זִימְנָא! נֵימָא מֵהָא נָמֵי תֶּיהְוֵי תְּיוּבְתָּא!
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Shall we say that this is a conclusive refutation of** the opinion of **Rabbi Yoḥanan,** who holds that a change of location never obligates one to recite a new blessing? The Gemara expresses surprise at this proposition: **But didn’t we** already **refute** Rabbi Yoḥanan’s opinion **once?** Why is it necessary to refute his ruling yet again? The Gemara admits that this is true but adds: Nevertheless, **let us say that this** *baraita* **is also a conclusive refutation** of the opinion of Rabbi Yoḥanan.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לָךְ רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: הוּא הַדִּין דַּאֲפִילּוּ דְּבָרִים שֶׁאֵין טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְאַחֲרֵיהֶם בִּמְקוֹמָן נָמֵי אֵין צְרִיכִין לְבָרֵךְ, וְהָא דְּקָתָנֵי ״עָקְרוּ רַגְלֵיהֶן״, לְהוֹדִיעֲךָ כֹּחוֹ דְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה,
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds that Rabbi Yoḥanan’s ruling cannot be definitively refuted from this *baraita*, as **Rabbi Yoḥanan** could have **said to you: The same is true that even** with regard to **items** of food **that do not require a blessing afterward in their place,** the people who ate them **are also not required to recite** a new **blessing. And** with regard to **that which** the *baraita* **teaches: They uprooted themselves,** from which it was inferred that the people were eating foods that require a blessing afterward in the place of eating, this phrase serves **to convey the far-reaching** nature **of Rabbi Yehuda’s** stringent opinion.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דַּאֲפִילּוּ דְּבָרִים שֶׁטְּעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְאַחֲרֵיהֶן בִּמְקוֹמָן, טַעְמָא דְּהִנִּיחוּ מִקְצָת חֲבֵרִים, אֲבָל לֹא הִנִּיחוּ מִקְצָת חֲבֵרִים, כְּשֶׁהֵן יוֹצְאִין — טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְמַפְרֵעַ, וּכְשֶׁהֵן חוֹזְרִין — טְעוּנִין בְּרָכָה לְכַתְּחִלָּה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the previous statement: According to Rabbi Yehuda, **even** if they were eating **items** of food **that require a blessing after them in their place,** and they will definitely return to the meal, **the reason** that these foods do not require a new blessing is **that they left some of** their **friends** at the meal. **However, if they did not leave some of** their **friends, when they exit,** these foods **require a blessing** to be recited **afterward, and when they return,** these foods **require an introductory blessing.** Nevertheless, it is possible that the Rabbis are lenient and do not obligate them to recite a new blessing, even if they are eating food that does not require a blessing afterward in the place in which they ate.

*Amar Rabbi Yehuda* — said *Rabbi Yehuda*: *Bameh devarim amurim?* — When does the above apply? *Bizman she-hinnichu sham mikzat chaveireihem* — when they left some of their group behind. *Aval lo hinnichu sham mikzat chaveireihem* — but if they left no one behind, then *keshe-hen yotzin te'unim bracha le-mafrea* — when they leave, they must recite *birkat hamazon*, and *u-keshe-hen chozrin te'unim bracha le-chatchila* — when they return, they must make a new *hamotzi*. Thus *Rabbi Yehuda* holds that the exemption from a new *bracha* depends on having left some companions at the table; without that, both a concluding *bracha* upon departure and a new *bracha rishona* upon return are required.

The *Chachamim*, however, say that even if nobody was left behind, no new *bracha* is required either way. This is consistent with *Rav Chisda*'s position — and more broadly with *Rav Huna*'s view — that when one had established a proper *seudah* in the original location, *shinui makom* does not require a new *bracha*. It is only *Rabbi Yehuda* who insists on it.

The *Gemara* now unpacks the implications of this *baraita*. The *Gemara* observes: *ella ta'ima di-bedvarim ha-te'unim bracha leachareihem bimkomam* — the reason the *Chachamim* who disagree with *Rabbi Yehuda* do not require a new *bracha* is that the eating in question involved foods that require a *bracha acharona* in that place, i.e., a meal with bread requiring *birkat hamazon* in that location. That is why *keshe-hein yotzin ein te'unim bracha le-mafrea u-keshe-hen chozrin ein te'unim bracha le-chatchila* — because they are returning to their *kvi'ut kamma*, the place where they originally established their meal, and the *shinui makom* does not sever that continuity.

The implication, however, would be: *aval devarim she-ein te'unim bracha leachareihem bimkomam* — if the eating was not of the type that establishes a fixed meal requiring a concluding *bracha* in that place — if they were merely drinking wine casually, or eating meat without bread, something that would not obligate *birkat hamazon* in the original location — then even according to the *Chachamim*, even in the scenario of departing and returning, presumably a new *bracha* would be required. The *baraita* thus implies that *shinui makom* generally requires a new *bracha*, and the only reason it does not here is the special case of a *seudah*. This would be a challenge to *Rabbi Yochanan*, who holds that *shinui makom* never requires a new *bracha*.

*Leima tehevay tiyuvta d'Rabbi Yochanan* — should this then stand as a contradiction to *Rabbi Yochanan*? The *Gemara* immediately asks: *u-mi de-ka tiyuvta d'Rabbi Yochanan ve-lav motvinen chad zimna?* — Did we not already contradict *Rabbi Yochanan* once? The *Gemara* had already declared *tiyuvta d'Rabbi Yochanan tiyuvta* from a previous *baraita*. So why do we need to do so again? The *Gemara* responds: *neima mehai nami tehevay tiyuvta* — if once is good, twice is better. Having multiple independent proofs against a position makes the refutation more definitive, since one might find a way around a single proof — perhaps the text is corrupt, perhaps there is a forced interpretation — but multiple proofs converge to establish the ruling firmly that we reject *Rabbi Yochanan's* view. The more evidence, the stronger the case.

### Yochanan Response

The *Gemara* considers whether *Rabbi Yochanan* has any response to this *baraita*. *Amar lecha Rabbi Yochanan* — *Rabbi Yochanan* can respond: *hu hadin afilu devarim she'ein te'unim bracha l'achareihem bimkomam nami ein tzrichin le'varech* — the same rule applies even to foods that do not require a concluding *bracha* in the original place; even in those cases, one never needs to make a new *bracha* on account of *shinui makom*. *Ve'had d'katani akru ragleihem* — and the reason the *baraita* specifically used the phrase "they uprooted themselves," which implies they were in a case of *kevi'at seudah* with a fixed meal — *le'hodia kocha d'Rebbe Yehuda* — was to tell you how strict *Rabbi Yehuda* is. Even in a case involving foods that require a concluding *bracha* in the same place, a case where the *kevi'ut* of the meal is most pronounced — even there, *Rabbi Yehuda* requires a new *bracha* unless some companions were left behind. *D'afilu devarim she'te'unim bracha l'achareihem bimkomam*, even the most favorable scenario for the *Chachamim*'s leniency — a full *seudah* — is not enough for *Rabbi Yehuda* without leaving people behind: *keshe-hen yotzin te'unim bracha l'mafriya u'keshe'hen chozrin te'unim bracha l'chatchila*.

In other words, *Rabbi Yochanan* argues: do not infer from the *Chachamim*'s ruling that they hold the *seudah* is the operative factor. Perhaps the *Chachamim* hold — in line with *Rabbi Yochanan* — that one never needs a new *bracha* for *shinui makom* regardless of whether a *seudah* was established. The *baraita* chose the *seudah* scenario not to define the boundary of the *Chachamim*'s leniency but simply to highlight the extreme stringency of *Rabbi Yehuda* — who demands a new *bracha* even in the most favorable scenario.

To summarize the full range of positions: *Rabbi Yochanan* holds that *shinui makom* never requires a new *bracha*. *Rav Sheshet* holds that it always does. *Rav Huna* occupies a middle position: a *seudah* — an established meal requiring *birkat hamazon* in the original location — exempts one from a new *bracha*; other situations do not.

There is also something worth noting about the structure of this *baraita* that one might find counterintuitive. It is the *seudah* scenario — the more serious and weighty eating, with bread and *birkat hamazon* — that enjoys the lenient ruling (no new *bracha*). Conversely, casual eating such as wine or snacks would apparently require a new *bracha* under *Rav Huna*'s view. One might expect the opposite: that the more fixed and solemn meal would more clearly constitute a separate experience in a new place, and that casual eating would more easily be seen as a single continuous experience. But the logic runs the other direction: precisely because the *seudah* creates a strong *kevi'ut* — an anchoring — to the original location, it causes even the eating in a new place to be seen as an extension of that primary meal. The *seudah* framework subsumes the later eating, keeping it linked to the first place. Moreover, there is a connection here to *hesach hadaat*: the very gravitas and intentionality of a *seudah* psychologically maintains one's connection to the meal even across different locations, so that a *shinui makom* does not constitute a true break.

### Rav Chisda Final

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תַּנְיָא כְּווֹתֵיהּ דְּרַב חִסְדָּא: חֲבֵרִים שֶׁהָיוּ מְסוּבִּין לִשְׁתּוֹת יַיִן, וְעָקְרוּ רַגְלֵיהֶן וְחָזְרוּ — אֵין צְרִיכִין לְבָרֵךְ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara points out that it **was taught** in a *baraita* **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rav Ḥisda:** With regard to **friends who were reclining to drink wine** together **and uprooted themselves** from their place **and** subsequently **returned** to their original location, they **need not recite** a new **blessing.** Wine is considered an important beverage that requires a concluding blessing in the place where it was consumed. This *baraita* explicitly supports the opinion of Rav Ḥisda, who rules with regard to items of this kind that if one left the place where he was drinking and later returned, no new blessing is necessary.

The *Gemara* concludes: *tanya kevasi d'Rav Chisda* — a *baraita* was taught that supports *Rav Chisda*: *Chaveirim she'hayu mesubin lishtot yayin ve'akeru ragleihem ve'chazaru, ein tzrichim levarech* — a group of companions who were reclining to drink wine, and they uprooted themselves and returned, are not required to make a new *bracha*. This ruling is stated without qualification and without reference to whether anyone was left behind, and without mentioning *Rabbi Yehuda*'s restriction. *Rabbi Yochanan* would maintain that this is always true. *Rav Chisda* would emphasize the *akru ragleihem* — that the reason no new *bracha* is required is that they had been *kovea seudah* and were therefore returning to their established meal.

There is a significant debate among the *rishonim* about the final *psak*. The *Gemara* appears to lean in the end toward *Rav Huna*'s position, yet the *Rif* ruled like *Rav Sheshet* — that *shinui makom* always requires a new *bracha*. We certainly do not rule like *Rabbi Yochanan*, but the question of whether the *seudah* creates an exemption is disputed among *rishonim*.

One further point about the *baraita*'s language — *akru ragleihem leilech le-veit ha-knesset o le-veit ha-midrash* — they uprooted themselves to go to the synagogue or the study hall. The *Ba'al HaMa'or* and the *Ra'avad* both note that this language conveys a practical rule: one should not ordinarily interrupt a meal for its own sake, since doing so raises issues of *brachot* — one might not return, might forget to recite the concluding *bracha*, and so on. Nevertheless, interrupting the meal is permitted — and indeed appropriate — for the sake of a *dvar mitzvah*. It is specifically because going to the synagogue or accompanying a *chatan* and *kallah* are obligatory acts that the *baraita* permits and validates the interruption.

*Tosafot* adds a fascinating application of this same principle. From the *baraita*'s phrase *akru ragleihem leilech le-veit ha-knesset* — they uprooted themselves to go to the synagogue — *tosafot* infers: *mikan mashma she'im shachach lehispalel venizkar be'emtza seudah, ve'yispaleil she'ein tzarich bracha afilu lechatchila* — if one forgot to pray and remembered in the middle of a meal, and one prays at home (rather than going to the synagogue), the act of prayer itself does not constitute a *hefsek* (interruption) in the meal. It is only because the *baraita*'s case involves going to the synagogue — a *shinui makom* — that the issue of a new *bracha* arises at all. Prayer per se, performed in one's current location, is not an interruption. This has practical relevance: if one is eating and suddenly realizes that *mincha* time is about to end and one prays at home, one need not make a new *bracha* when one resumes eating, since no *shinui makom* has occurred. *Tosafot* does acknowledge another *Gemara* that might seem to contradict this conclusion but argues it is not a proof.

## Chaveirim Interrupt



### Friends at Meal

The *Gemara* now introduces a new *baraita*: *Tanu Rabbanan* — the Rabbis taught: *Bnei chaburah she-hayu mesubin* — members of a group who were reclining at a meal. Here the terminology *bnei chaburah* and *chaveirim* may carry a technical resonance. In Tannaitic literature, the *chaburah* refers to a defined group of people who were punctilious about eating their ordinary food (*chullin*) in a state of ritual purity (*tahara*), and who were correspondingly careful about not admitting outsiders to their table. The concerns of the *chaburah* loom much larger in Tannaitic sources than in the later *Gemara*, and it is worth considering whether these *baraitas* are using the term technically or more loosely to denote any cohesive group eating together. There is reason to think that the intentionality underlying the *chaburah* model — the deliberate creation of a shared sacred space around eating, defined by *tumah* and *tahara* — may be the conceptual precursor to the *halachic* framework of *brachot* creating similar boundaries of continuity and discontinuity around a meal. One might even speculate that the very concerns of *shinui makom* are rooted in this earlier framework, where moving from one place to another was laden with *tumah* and *tahara* implications. Whatever the historical connection, the *baraita* presents the case of a group reclining together at a meal who then uprooted themselves to go to the synagogue or to attend a wedding (*chatan* and *kallah*).

## Shabbat Interruption



### Shabbat Arrives

A separate *baraita* addresses an analogous but distinct scenario: *Bnei chaburah she-hayu mesubin* — a group of companions reclining at a meal — *ve-kadesh aleihem ha-yom* — and then the day became sanctified, meaning it was Friday afternoon and *Shabbat* arrived during their meal. This raises the question not only of whether they must interrupt, but how they are to handle the *brachot* attendant on that interruption.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: בְּנֵי חֲבוּרָה שֶׁהָיוּ מְסוּבִּין וְקִדֵּשׁ עֲלֵיהֶן הַיּוֹם — מְבִיאִין לוֹ כּוֹס שֶׁל יַיִן וְאוֹמֵר עָלָיו קְדוּשַּׁת הַיּוֹם, וְשֵׁנִי אוֹמֵר עָלָיו בִּרְכַּת הַמָּזוֹן, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה. רַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹמֵר: אוֹכֵל וְהוֹלֵךְ עַד שֶׁתֶּחְשַׁךְ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara returns to the subject of interrupting one’s meal to recite *kiddush*. **The Sages taught:** With regard to **members of a group who were reclining** and eating a meal, **and the day** of Shabbat **was sanctified, they bring** one of the diners **a cup of wine and he recites over it the sanctification of the day,** i.e., *kiddush*, **and a second** cup **over which he recites Grace after Meals;** this is **the statement of Rabbi Yehuda. Rabbi Yosei says: One** may **continue eating** the rest of his meal, even **until dark.**

## Chaveirim Interrupt (II)



### Yehuda Strict View

*Rabbi Yehuda* holds that when none of the companions were left behind — that is, they all departed — a new *bracha* is required. The *Chachamim* disagree and rule leniently, holding that no new *bracha* is required even when no one remains at the table.

### Foods & Bracha

The underlying principle that enables the *Chachamim*'s leniency is that *devarim ha-te'unim bracha leachareihem bimkomam* — foods that require a concluding *bracha* to be recited in the same place where they were eaten — create a bond of continuity between the person and the original location. Since one must return to that place to bench, the departure is never a true severance from the original meal. The *shinui makom* is absorbed into the larger framework of the *seudah*, and the continuity of the *seudah* persists despite the physical change of place.

## Shabbat Meal Interruption



### Yehuda Interrupts

In the case where *Shabbat* arrives during a meal, *Rabbi Yehuda* requires the group to interrupt immediately: *mevi'im lo kos shel yayin v'omer alav kedushat hayom v'hasheni omer alav birkat hamazon* — they bring a cup of wine, say *kiddush* on it, and say *birkat hamazon* on a second cup. This is the ruling of *Rabbi Yehuda*. There is, however, a question of textual variants (*girsaot*) in the *baraita*. Some versions have *birkat hamazon* first and then *kiddush*; our version has *kiddush* first and then *birkat hamazon*. What is the reasoning for *kiddush* preceding *birkat hamazon*?

*Tosafot* explains: if one were to recite *birkat hamazon al ha-kos* first — which the *Gemara* throughout assumes is standard practice, even for an individual — one encounters a technical problem. Once it has become Friday night and *Shabbat* has begun, one cannot simply drink from the cup of *birkat hamazon* before having made *kiddush*. One is prohibited from drinking wine on *Shabbat* night before *kiddush*. Therefore, *kiddush* must be recited first, notwithstanding the irony that one is using *kiddush* to inaugurate *Shabbat* in the middle of what is still technically a weekday meal. Only after *kiddush* can one proceed to *birkat hamazon al ha-kos* and drink from that cup. The *Ba'al HaMa'or* and another authority suggest alternatively that since it was *kiddush* — the arrival of the new day — that created the need to interrupt in the first place, it naturally takes precedence and is followed by *birkat hamazon*. But *tosafot*'s explanation is the more straightforward one.

Now of course, *Rabbi Yehuda*'s entire purpose was to create an interruption and a clear demarcation. *Tosafot* therefore notes that before reciting *kiddush*, one removes the table (*mesalek et ha-shulchan*) — the *siluk ha-shulchan* that was discussed in an earlier context — which creates the necessary physical break. Then, for the technical reason just described, *kiddush* follows, and then *birkat hamazon al ha-kos*.

### Yossi Lenient


*Rabbi Yossi* takes a more lenient approach: *ochel v'holech ad she-techshach* — one continues eating until it gets dark. Once the meal concludes naturally, then: *gamru, kos rishon mevarech alav birkat hamazon v'hasheni omer alav kedushat hayom* — on the first cup one recites *birkat hamazon*, and on the second cup one says *kedushat ha-yom*. The order here is intuitive: one finishes eating, benches to close the meal, and then recites *kiddush* for *Shabbat*.

There is, however, a question implicit in *Rabbi Yossi*'s position. Even though he permitted continuing to eat after *Shabbat* arrived — presumably because it is a continuation of a meal that began before *Shabbat* and is therefore permitted — once one has finished the meal and is now benching *al ha-kos*, one faces precisely the same problem *Rabbi Yehuda* identified: how can one drink from the cup of *birkat hamazon* without first making *kiddush*? The fact that continuing to eat was permitted as a continuation of the earlier meal does not obviously mean that the cup of *birkat hamazon* shares that same status. Yet *Rabbi Yossi* apparently holds that even the wine of *birkat hamazon* in this case is considered a continuation of the original meal, and the natural order — bench first, then *kiddush* — is retained.

### Purim Friday Case

It is worth noting here a related and fascinating *halacha*: the case of *pores mappah u'mekadesh*, which can arise when *Purim* falls on a Friday. One is eating the *Purim seudah* and wine has been consumed throughout the meal. One must now make *Shabbat kiddush*. In this case, everyone agrees that one does not recite a new *borei pri hagafen*. One begins *kiddush* not with *borei pri hagafen* but directly with *asher kidshanu be-mitzvotav ve-ratza banu* — since *borei pri hagafen* is not technically a *bracha* of *kiddush* itself; it is incorporated into *kiddush* because one needs it to be permitted to drink the wine and to discharge the obligation of *kiddush al ha-yayin*. But since one has been drinking wine throughout the meal and the *kiddush* follows immediately without any interruption, a new *borei pri hagafen* is unnecessary. This is the *halacha*.

The more contested question is whether one must recite a new *hamotzi*. Some *rishonim* hold that the act of *kiddush* — even when recited without interruption at the same table with the food still present — transforms the meal into a *Shabbat* meal. As a new *Shabbat* meal, it requires a fresh *hamotzi*, analogous in some ways to the principle of *shinui makom* creating a new situation. Others disagree: since there was no interruption and one is still seated at the same table with the same food, no new *hamotzi* is required. The *Shulchan Aruch* records both opinions. The *Mishnah Berurah* rules based on the principle that where there is a doubt in *brachot*, the lenient ruling prevails (*safek brachot le-hakel*), and one does not recite a new *hamotzi*. The practical result on a Friday *Purim* would be remarkable: one recites *kiddush* without *borei pri hagafen*, uncovers the two loaves (*lechem mishneh*), and eats the bread without *hamotzi*.

### No Interruption

The principle underlying the ruling on *borei pri hagafen* in the *pores mappah u'mekadesh* case is that when there is genuine continuation — no interruption of any kind — a *bracha* already recited continues to cover subsequent drinking. This flows from the broader principle of *safek brachot le-hakel*: wherever a doubt exists as to whether a new *bracha* is required, the lenient ruling prevails and no new *bracha* is recited.

## Two Cups Principle



[DAF:102b]
### Why Two Cups

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גָּמְרוּ, כּוֹס רִאשׁוֹן מְבָרֵךְ עָלָיו בִּרְכַּת הַמָּזוֹן וְהַשֵּׁנִי אוֹמֵר עָלָיו קְדוּשַּׁת הַיּוֹם. אַמַּאי? וְנֵימְרִינְהוּ לְתַרְוַיְיהוּ אַחֲדָא כָּסָא!
> **Translation:** Rabbi Yosei maintains that once they have **finished** their meal, they bring out two cups; **over the first cup one recites the Grace after Meals, and over the second** cup he **recites the sanctification of the day.** The Gemara asks: **Why** do they need two cups? **And let them say both of them,** Grace after Meals and *kiddush*, **over one cup.**

The *Gemara* now asks a pointed question arising from the *baraita* about *Rabbi Yossi*'s arrangement: *amai* — why is it necessary to use two separate cups, one for *birkat hamazon* and one for *kiddush*? *Ve'nimreinhu letarvaihu achada kasa* — why not say both on a single cup?

*Tosafot* notes that this question applies specifically to the position of *Rabbi Yossi*, and here is why. According to *Rabbi Yehuda*, two cups are inherent to the structure of the situation: the first cup is used to close the weekday meal and the second to inaugurate *Shabbat*, and the entire point of the arrangement is to create a demarcation between them. Combining them on one cup would defeat the purpose of the interruption entirely. But for *Rabbi Yossi*, who allows the meal to continue naturally through nightfall and regards the subsequent *kiddush* as a concluding act tied to the meal, combining *birkat hamazon* and *kiddush* on one cup might seem not only permissible but even logical. *Tosafot* raises a further consideration: according to *Rabbi Yossi*, there is actually a question of *kiddush bemakom seudah* — how does this *kiddush* constitute *kiddush* in the place of a meal if all the eating is already done? *Tosafot* resolves this by explaining that according to *Rabbi Yossi*, once *kiddush* is recited after the *seudah*, it retroactively defines what was eaten as a *Shabbat seudah* and itself constitutes *kiddush bemakom seudah*. This integration of *kiddush* with the meal makes the suggestion of a single cup even more intuitive. So why use two?

### Bundling Rule

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב הוּנָא אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: אֵין אוֹמְרִים שְׁתֵּי קְדוּשּׁוֹת עַל כּוֹס אֶחָד. מַאי טַעְמָא? אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: לְפִי שֶׁאֵין עוֹשִׂין מִצְוֹת חֲבִילוֹת חֲבִילוֹת.
> **Translation:** **Rav Huna said** that **Rav Sheshet said: One does not recite two sanctifications,** i.e., for two mitzvot such as Grace after Meals and *kiddush*, **over one cup. What is the reason** for this *halakha*? **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said: Because one does not perform mitzvot in bundles.** If someone performs multiple mitzvot all in one go, he gives the impression that they are a burdensome obligation that he wants to complete as fast as possible.

*Amar Rav Huna amar Rav Sheshet* — *Rav Huna* said in the name of *Rav Sheshet*: *Ein omrim shtei kedushot al kos echad* — one does not recite two sanctifications over a single cup. This is the principle of *ein osin mitzvot chavilot chavilot* — one does not bundle *mitzvot* together.

The *Rashbam* explains the concern as a problem of appearance: using one cup for two purposes looks as though one is treating the *mitzvot* as a burden, reusing a vessel to economize. But beyond the aesthetics, there is a deeper point. The entire institution of reciting *kiddush* or *birkat hamazon al ha-kos* is designed to lend weight, significance, and ritual identity to these acts. A cup of wine elevates the *bracha* and transforms it from a mere verbal formula into a formal rite. When two distinct *brachot* — each with its own separate identity — are performed over the same cup, each loses something of that identity. One no longer has a *kos* of *birkat hamazon* and a *kos* of *kiddush*; one has a single cup whose identity is unclear, muddy, and diminished. Particularly in the context of *kiddush al ha-kos*, where the cup is constitutive of the ritual character of the act, this conflation is especially problematic.

### Havdalah One Cup

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלָא? וְהָא תַּנְיָא: הַנִּכְנָס לְבֵיתוֹ בְּמוֹצָאֵי שַׁבָּת מְבָרֵךְ עַל הַיַּיִן וְעַל הַמָּאוֹר וְעַל הַבְּשָׂמִים, וְאַחַר כָּךְ אוֹמֵר הַבְדָּלָה עַל הַכּוֹס. וְאִם אֵין לוֹ אֶלָּא כּוֹס אֶחָד — מַנִּיחוֹ לְאַחַר הַמָּזוֹן וּמְשַׁלְשְׁלָן כּוּלָּן לְאַחֲרָיו. אֵין לוֹ שָׁאנֵי.
> **Translation:** **And** does one **not** perform multiple mitzvot together? **But wasn’t** it **taught** in a *baraita*: **One who enters his home at the conclusion of Shabbat recites the blessing over the wine, and** then **over the light, and** then **over the spices, and thereafter he recites *havdala* over the cup** of wine. **And if he has only one cup** of wine, **he leaves it for after** he eats his **food,** and uses it for Grace after Meals, **and arranges all of the** other blessings together **thereafter.** This *baraita* indicates that one may use the same cup both for Grace after Meals and *havdala*. The Gemara answers: We cannot prove anything from here, as a case where one **does not have** an additional cup **is different.** One who has two cups of wine is required to recite Grace after Meals over one of the cups and *havdala* over the other one.

The *Gemara* objects to this principle by citing a *baraita*: *Hanichnas le-beito be-motzei Shabbat* — one who enters his home on *motzei Shabbat* — *mevarech al ha-yayin ve-al ha-maor ve-al ha-besamim* — recites *brachot* over wine, light (*ner*), and spices (*besamim*). Note the order — wine, then light, then spices (we recite wine, spices, then light — that order will be addressed shortly). *Ve-achar kach omer havdalah* — and afterwards recites *havdalah* — all on one cup. This is the regular *havdalah*.

Now *veim ein lo ela kos echad* — if one has only a single cup of wine available, and one also needs that cup for *birkat hamazon* because one has been eating — *mianicho le-achar ha-mazon* — one sets the cup aside until after the meal. *U-meshalshlan kulan le-acharav* — and one strings all the *brachot* together afterwards on that single cup. The word *meshalshal* evokes a chain (*shalshelet*) — the *brachot* are daisy-chained in sequence on a single cup, covering *birkat hamazon* and all the components of *havdalah* together.

This seems to be a direct challenge to the principle just stated: here one is explicitly using a single cup for both *birkat hamazon* and *havdalah*, which are two distinct *mitzvot* — exactly what *ein osin mitzvot chavilot chavilot* appeared to prohibit.

### One Cup Exception

The *Gemara* resolves the contradiction: *ein lo shani* — when one has only one cup, the situation is different. When resources are genuinely limited, the principle of *ein osin mitzvot chavilot chavilot* yields, and one may string together multiple *brachot* on a single cup. It is only when two cups are available that one must use separate cups for separate *mitzvot* to preserve each one's distinct identity and significance.

It is worth being precise about what *kos echad* means in this context. The issue is not the physical cup itself but the quantity of wine — one *kos* worth of wine is all one has. The scenario the *baraita* envisions is therefore someone who has been eating, perhaps at *shalosh seudot*, and faces a genuine dilemma: does he use his single measure of wine for *benching* — *birkat hamazon al ha-kos* — or does he reserve it for *havdalah*? The *baraita*'s answer is that he uses it for *birkat hamazon* first, sets it aside, and then strings all the *havdalah brachot* together on that same cup afterwards — a question of how *tosafot* extends this ruling is taken up shortly.

### Tosafot Exception

*Tosafot* takes this further and draws a remarkable practical conclusion. The *baraita* mentions the scenario of coming home (*hanichnas le-beito*) and having only one cup. *Tosafot* understands this as follows: the person came home from the synagogue on *motzei Shabbat* and has only one cup of wine. Normally one is forbidden to eat before *havdalah*. But this person intends to eat a meal later that evening — presumably bread, the main food available to someone of limited means — and he wants to recite *birkat hamazon al ha-kos* at the end of that meal. If he uses his single cup for *havdalah* now, he will have no cup left for *birkat hamazon al ha-kos*. *Tosafot* therefore permits him to eat his meal before *havdalah*, despite the general prohibition, in order to enable him to use that single cup for both *birkat hamazon* and *havdalah* together, stringing them *al ha-kos*.

This ruling reveals something significant: the *Gemara* throughout these cases takes completely for granted that *birkat hamazon* is recited *al ha-kos* — over a cup of wine — even by an individual dining alone. The assumption is so pervasive and unquestioned that *tosafot* is willing to permit eating before *havdalah* — an ordinarily prohibited act — in order to allow this practice to be maintained. That is a remarkable statement about the centrality of *birkat hamazon al ha-kos* in the practice of the *Gemara*'s world.

## YomTov After Shabbat



### Scenario Setup

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא יוֹם טוֹב שֶׁחָל לִהְיוֹת אַחַר הַשַּׁבָּת, דְּאִית לֵיהּ, וְאָמַר רַב יַקְנֶ״ה!
> **Translation:** The Gemara continues its line of questioning: **But** there is the case of **a Festival that occurs after Shabbat,** when presumably **one has** enough wine. **And** nevertheless, **Rav said** that the proper order of the blessings is according to the Hebrew acronym ***yod*, *kuf*, *nun*, *heh*:** The blessing over the wine [*yayin*], *kiddush*, the blessing over the candle [*ner*], and *havdala*. This ruling shows that one recites *kiddush* and *havdala* over the same cup of wine.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרִי: מִדְּלָא אָמַר זְמַן, מִכְּלָל דְּבִשְׁבִיעִי שֶׁל פֶּסַח עָסְקִינַן, דְּכׇל מַאי דַּהֲוָה לֵיהּ אָכֵיל לֵיהּ, וְלֵית לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **They say** in answer to this question: **From** the fact that Rav **did not say** that one recites the blessing for **time,** Who has given us life [*sheheḥiyanu*], sustained us, and brought us to this time, this proves **by inference that we are dealing with the seventh** day **of Passover,** which is the only Festival day on which one does not recite the blessing for time. If so, it is possible that **whatever** wine this person **had, he** has already **consumed** over the course of the Festival, **and he does not have** enough wine left for two separate cups.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא יוֹם טוֹב רִאשׁוֹן דְּאִית לֵיהּ, וְאָמַר אַבָּיֵי: יַקְזְנָ״ה, וְרָבָא אָמַר: יַקְנְהָ״ז.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** there is the case of **the first Festival** night that occurs after Shabbat, **when one has** wine, **and** nevertheless **Abaye said** that the order of the blessings in this instance follows the Hebrew acronym ***yod*, *kuf*, *zayin*, *nun*, *heh*:** The blessing over wine [*yayin*]; *kiddush*; the blessing for time [*zeman*]; the blessing over the candle [*ner*]; and *havdala*. **And Rava said** that the order of the blessings is in accordance with the acronym ***yod*, *kuf*, *nun*, *heh*, *zayin*:** Wine [*yayin*]; *kiddush*; candle [*ner*]; *havdala*; and the blessing for time [*zeman*]. Although Abaye and Rava dispute the correct order of the blessings, they agree that one recites multiple blessings over a single cup of wine.

The *Gemara* now introduces a new scenario: *Yom Tov she-chal lihyot achar Shabbat* — *Yom Tov* that falls immediately after *Shabbat*. In such a case, one must recite both *havdalah* (transitioning from the greater holiness of *Shabbat* to the lesser holiness of *Yom Tov*) and *kiddush* (inaugurating the new *Yom Tov*). Note that *havdalah* from *Shabbat* to *Yom Tov* is recited only in this specific direction: from *Shabbat* into *Yom Tov*. *Havdalah* always moves from a greater sanctity to a lesser one; since *Yom Tov* is a lesser sanctity than *Shabbat*, one makes *havdalah* at the transition from *Shabbat* to *Yom Tov*, but never from *Yom Tov* into *Shabbat*, which would be moving in the opposite direction.

*Tosafot* notes that the *Gemara* presumes that in this scenario one *vedai* — certainly — has two cups of wine. This is because at the beginning of *Yom Tov*, when it is a time of *simcha* and celebration, one's wine supply is at its fullest. The end-of-*Shabbat* scenario, by contrast, is when one might genuinely be left with a single cup; by *havdalah* time, the wine may indeed be depleted. But at the start of *Yom Tov* one has stock in abundance.

*Ve-amar Rav* — and *Rav* says: *Yakneh* — the mnemonic for the order: *yayin, kiddush, ner, havdalah* — wine, then *kiddush*, then the blessing over the candle, then *havdalah*. This means *kiddush* is recited before *havdalah*, which is counterintuitive since chronologically one is first ending *Shabbat* and then beginning *Yom Tov*.

The *Gemara* attempts to determine which specific scenario *Rav* is addressing by noting that he does not mention *zman* — the blessing of *she-hecheyanu*. The only *kiddush* of *Yom Tov* that omits *she-hecheyanu* is that of *Shevi'i shel Pesach*, which is considered a continuation of *Pesach* rather than a new festival. The *Gemara* infers: *miklal de-beShevi'i shel Pesach iskinan* — we must be discussing *Shevi'i shel Pesach*, when by the last day of *Pesach* one's wine supply has been largely consumed — *dekol mai de-hava lei achlei, ve-leit lei* — one has eaten through all one's *Pesach*-food and drunk all one's wine, leaving at most one cup. This would explain why *Rav* is addressing the situation on one cup.

But this is a rather narrow answer. *Rav* is surely issuing a general ruling. The *Gemara* therefore presses: what about *Yom Tov Rishon*, the first day of *Yom Tov*, when one obviously has ample wine? On that day one also needs *she-hecheyanu*, and *Abaye* there discusses an order incorporating it. *Abaye* says *Yakzneh* — *yayin, kiddush, zman* (i.e., *she-hecheyanu*), *ner, havdalah* — doing the full *kiddush* of *Yom Tov* complete with *she-hecheyanu*, and then the components of *havdalah*. And *Rav* himself says *Yaknehaz* — *yayin, kiddush, ner, havdalah, zman* — with *she-hecheyanu* pushed to the very end, which is the practice we follow.

In all of these arrangements, the *kiddush* and *havdalah* are performed on the same cup. This seems to stand in direct tension with *ein osin mitzvot chavilot chavilot*. One is using a single cup for both *kiddush* and *havdalah* — two distinct ritual acts.

### Havdalah Kiddush One

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא: הַבְדָּלָה וְקִידּוּשׁ חֲדָא מִילְּתָא הִיא, בִּרְכַּת הַמָּזוֹן וְקִידּוּשׁ תְּרֵי מִילֵּי נִינְהוּ.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** the Gemara rejects the previous explanation in favor of the following: ***Havdala* and *kiddush* are one matter,** as they both mark and draw attention to the sanctity of certain days. By contrast, **Grace after Meals and *kiddush* are two** entirely different **matters.** Therefore, one who recites both of them over the same cup of wine is combining two unrelated mitzvot, apparently so that he can be done with them as quickly as possible. Consequently, this practice is prohibited.

The *Gemara* resolves the apparent tension: *Havdalah ve-Kiddush chada milta hi* — *havdalah* and *kiddush* are one concept. They are not, of course, identical — one is entering sanctity and the other is departing from a higher sanctity into a lesser one — but they are engaged in the same essential enterprise: defining the nature of the day and articulating its relationship to what comes before and after. Both *kiddush* and *havdalah* are about the identity of *kedushah* and its boundaries. They are, as it were, two aspects of a single conceptual act of demarcation, and therefore combining them on one cup does not constitute the bundling of two alien *mitzvot* — the cup retains a coherent single identity as the cup of sanctification.

By contrast, *birkat hamazon ve-kiddush trei milei ninhu* — *birkat hamazon* and *kiddush* are two entirely different things. One arises from the fact that one has eaten a meal; the other from the holiness of the day. They come from different conceptual universes, and combining them on a single cup would cause each to lose its distinct identity. Therefore two cups are required for that combination, but one cup suffices — indeed is appropriate — for the combination of *havdalah* and *kiddush*.

## Chuppah Cups



### Separate Brachot

The *Gemara*'s principle of *ein osin mitzvot chavilot chavilot* has direct practical implications for the wedding ceremony, as *tosafot* explicitly draws out. *Tosafot* states: *ve-yeish nohagim ba-chuppah mi-ta'am zeh shelo lomar Sheva Brachot al kos Birkat Hamazon* — there is a practice at the *chuppah* not to recite the *sheva brachot* over the cup of *birkat hamazon*. The reference here is not to the *brachot* recited under the *chuppah* itself but to those recited at the wedding meal that follows. Technically, the cup of wine at the meal is a cup of *birkat hamazon* — everyone holds a cup of wine for benching and the *brachot* are recited over it. But if one uses that same cup for the *sheva brachot*, one is combining two conceptually distinct things: *birkat hamazon*, which arises from the act of eating, and *sheva brachot*, which are blessings of joy and sanctification appropriate to the marriage. These are conceptually separate, from different domains, and combining them on one cup would cause each to lose its independent identity.

### Two Cups Custom

The practice therefore is to bring a second cup. The cup of *birkat hamazon* remains for benching, and a separate second cup is brought for the *sheva brachot*. Crucially, one does not recite a new *borei pri hagafen* over the second cup: *ve-ein mevorchin al ha-sheini borei pri hagafen, keivan she-kvar beirach al kos birkat hamazon, d'ein nachon le-varech pa'amayim* — since one has already recited *borei pri hagafen* over the first cup, it would be inappropriate to recite it again over the second.

### Cup Mixing

This two-cup arrangement gives rise to a well-known custom. The leader of benching holds one cup and a second cup is brought for the *sheva brachot*. After the *brachot* are recited, wine is poured between the two cups into a third vessel, and then distributed back, so that each cup contains wine from both. This ensures that the *chatan*, the *kallah*, and those who made the *brachot* each drink from wine connected to both cups. The entire elaborate process of mixing and pouring flows from the concern of *tosafot* — that the cup of benching and the cup of *sheva brachot* must remain conceptually distinct, each retaining its own identity, while everyone still has the opportunity to benefit from both.

### Meshullam Dissent

*Tosafot* records, however, a dissenting view: *Ach Rabbeinu Meshullam hayah omer hakol al kos echad* — *Rabbeinu Meshullam* held that all the *brachot* may be recited over a single cup. His reasoning: *d'lo dami le-birkat hamazon ve-kiddush d'trei milei ninhu* — this case is not comparable to the *Gemara*'s case of *birkat hamazon* and *kiddush*, which are two entirely unrelated matters, as the *Gemara* explicitly stated. Here, *k'd'amrinan be-samuch, ve-hacha chada milta hi* — these are one integrated concept. *Birkat hamazon gorim le-virchas nisuin* — the *sheva brachot* come as a result of the meal; they are being recited because of the meal. According to some *rishonim*, the *sheva brachot* were even originally established as part of the *birkat hamazon* framework. Since the *sheva brachot* are causally and conceptually connected to the meal, and indeed stem from the meal, they constitute one integrated entity together with *birkat hamazon* — just as *havdalah* and *kiddush* were considered *chada milta* because they both define the day's sanctity. Therefore a single cup not only suffices but may even be appropriate. This is a persuasive position, and though we do not follow it in practice, it has genuine logical force.

## Eirusin Nisu'in



### Different Times

*Tosafot* continues the analysis to address *birchat eirusin* and *birchat nisu'in* under the *chuppah*. *U-virchas eirusin u-nisuin nahagu lomar al shtei kosot* — the custom is to recite the *brachot* of *eirusin* and *nisu'in* on two separate cups. The reason: *ha-ta'am d'hergilim zeh be-lo zeh* — they were historically performed separately from one another. The *Gemara* in *Ketubot* states: *mevorchin birkat ha-eirusin be-veit eirusin u-virchat chasanim be-veit chasanim* — the blessing of *eirusin* was recited at the *eirusin* ceremony, while the blessings of marriage were recited at the wedding itself, potentially a full year later in a different home. Although today *eirusin* and *nisu'in* are combined in a single ceremony under the *chuppah*, and they are clearly conceptually related — both are stages in the process of marriage — their historical origins as entirely separate events occurring at different times and in different places means they retain distinct identities requiring separate cups.

### Ketubah Break

*Tosafot* adds a further dimension: *ve-od d'nahagu likros ketubah bein-tayim ve-havei hefsek, u-le-khach niskuvnu* — the custom to read the *ketubah* between *eirusin* and *nisu'in* serves as a deliberate *hefsek*, an intentional interruption. Like the principle of *siluk ha-shulchan* in the Friday evening meal case — removing the table to create a formal demarcation — the reading of the *ketubah* is designed to create a break that separates *eirusin* from *nisu'in* and underscores their distinctiveness. *Tosafot* stresses that this is intentional: *u-le-khach niskuvnu* — they specifically meant it to function this way.

One might argue that since both *eirusin* and *nisu'in* are happening in the same ceremony at the same time, they could be considered sufficiently unified to share a cup. But *tosafot* rejects this: when the acts involved are conceptually and historically distinct events, their being performed in temporal proximity within a single ceremony does not merge them into a single act. The distinctness demands separate cups.

### Modern Custom

In contemporary practice, a true two-cup arrangement under the *chuppah* is relatively rare. Most commonly, the officiant simply slightly refills the cup between *eirusin* and *nisu'in* — which is in any case necessary, since drinking from a cup renders it *pagum* (defective for use in a *bracha*) unless wine is added. The full two-cup practice may still be observed in some communities, but concerns about spillage and practicality have led most to rely on refilling a single cup. What matters according to *tosafot*'s stricter view is not merely the physical quantity of wine but the formal identity of having a distinct cup for each set of *brachot*.

## Yom Tov After Shabbat



### Rav's Order

The *Gemara* returns to the dispute between *Rav* and *Shmuel* regarding the order of *havdalah* and *kiddush* when *Yom Tov* follows *Shabbat*. *Rav amar Yakneh* — *yayin, kiddush, ner, havdalah*. The *Rashbam* explains: *sevira lei kedushat ha-yom adifa me-havdalah* — *Rav* holds that the sanctity of the incoming day takes precedence. By placing *kiddush* before *havdalah*, one emphasizes the positive act of entering into holiness rather than the departure from the greater holiness of *Shabbat*. One focuses, as it were, on what one is arriving at rather than what one is leaving.

There is another way to express the same idea: *inami de-chi mavdil be-resha michzei k'de-havya lei kedushat ha-yom ke-masuy* — if one were to make *havdalah* first, it would appear as though the incoming holiness of *Yom Tov* is a burden — something being wearily taken on after the effort of departing from *Shabbat*. Better to lead with *kiddush*, embracing the new *Yom Tov*, and then acknowledge the transition from *Shabbat*. This is a theme that appears in other contexts as well, such as the practice on *Chanukah* to light the *Chanukah* candles before *havdalah* in the synagogue — even though *Shabbat* is already over, one shows reluctance to formally end it by delaying *havdalah* slightly. Here *Rav* applies the analogous logic: even though *Shabbat* has ended, do not rush to formally declare its conclusion; instead, first embrace the new *Yom Tov*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוּפָא. יוֹם טוֹב שֶׁחָל לִהְיוֹת אַחַר הַשַּׁבָּת, רַב אָמַר: יַקְנֶ״ה. וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: יִנְהַ״ק.
> **Translation:** After raising the issue of the proper order of the blessings for *kiddush* on a Festival that occurs right after Shabbat, the Gemara addresses **the** matter **itself.** With regard to **a Festival that occurs after Shabbat, Rav said** that the proper order of the blessings follows the acronym ***yod*, *kuf*, *nun*, *heh*:** Wine [*yayin*], *kiddush*, candle [*ner*], and *havdala*; **and Shmuel said** the proper order is ***yod*, *nun*, *heh*, *kuf*:** Wine [*yayin*], candle [*ner*], *havdala*, and *kiddush*.

### Shmuel's Order


*De-Shmuel amar Yanhak* — *Shmuel* says: *yayin, ner, havdalah, kiddush*. Wine first, then the blessing over the candle, then *havdalah*, and only then *kiddush*. *Shmuel*'s order has its own logic: *havdalah* precedes *kiddush* because one should first formally conclude *Shabbat* before introducing the new *Yom Tov*. One does not begin *kiddush* for the new day until *Shabbat* has been properly ended. This is the more intuitively natural sequence. Indeed we rule, as noted, like *Rav* — *Yaknehaz* — though *Shmuel*'s order has an evident coherence.

The standard ruling we follow, incorporating *she-hecheyanu*, is *Yaknehaz*: *yayin, kiddush, ner, havdalah, zman* — with *she-hecheyanu* pushed all the way to the end. The position of *she-hecheyanu* at the conclusion is itself notable and will be addressed in further discussion.

### No Spices

Why, in the *havdalah* recited when *Yom Tov* follows *Shabbat*, are no *besamim* (spices) included? The *Rashbam* explains that just as *Shabbat* possesses a *neshama yeseira* — an additional soul — so too does *Yom Tov*. The purpose of *besamim* at the conclusion of *Shabbat* is to revive and console the person for the loss of the *neshama yeseira* as *Shabbat* ends. But since *Yom Tov* also carries a *neshama yeseira*, there is no loss to compensate for.

*Tosafot* challenges this: if *Yom Tov* itself possesses a *neshama yeseira*, then at the conclusion of every *Yom Tov* — *motzei Yom Tov* — one would be obligated to use *besamim* at *havdalah*, since one would also be losing the *neshama yeseira* of *Yom Tov*. But that is not the practice. Therefore the reason cannot be that *Yom Tov* itself has a *neshama yeseira*.

*Tosafot*'s own explanation: the *simcha* of *Yom Tov* is sufficient. Even though the *neshama yeseira* of *Shabbat* departs at the conclusion of *Shabbat*, the person entering into *Yom Tov* is enlivened and sustained by the *simcha*, the joy, of the incoming festival. The festive gladness of *Yom Tov* provides the same restorative function that *besamim* ordinarily serve — it revives the spirit and compensates for the departure of the *Shabbat* soul. Therefore *besamim* are unnecessary, and are not recited in the *havdalah* of *Yaknehaz*.
