## Kohen Gadol & Yibum



[DAF:19b]
### Perek Overview

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מַלְכֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל מַאי טַעְמָא לָא? מִשּׁוּם מַעֲשֶׂה שֶׁהָיָה, דְּעַבְדֵּיהּ דְּיַנַּאי מַלְכָּא קְטַל נַפְשָׁא. אֲמַר לְהוּ שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן שָׁטַח לַחֲכָמִים: תְּנוּ עֵינֵיכֶם בּוֹ וּנְדוּנֶנּוּ. שְׁלַחוּ לֵיהּ: עַבְדָּךְ קְטַל נַפְשָׁא. שַׁדְּרֵיהּ לְהוּ. שְׁלַחוּ לֵיהּ: תָּא אַנְתְּ נָמֵי לְהָכָא, ״וְהוּעַד בִּבְעָלָיו״ אָמְרָה תּוֹרָה – יָבֹא בַּעַל הַשּׁוֹר וְיַעֲמוֹד עַל שׁוֹרוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But what is the reason** that others **do not** judge **the kings of Israel?** It is **because of an incident that happened, as the slave of Yannai the king killed a person. Shimon ben Shataḥ said to the Sages: Put your eyes on him and let us judge him. They sent** word **to** Yannai: **Your slave killed a person.** Yannai **sent** the slave **to them. They sent** word **to** Yannai: **You also come here,** as the verse states with regard to an ox that gored a person to death: **“He should be testified against with his owner”** (Exodus 21:29). **The Torah stated: The owner of the ox should come and stand over his ox.**

> **Translation:** The Gemara continues to narrate the incident: Yannai **came and sat down. Shimon ben Shataḥ said to him: Yannai the king, stand on your feet and** witnesses **will testify against you. And** it is **not before us** that **you are standing,** to give us honor, **but** it is **before the One Who spoke and the world came into being** that **you are standing, as it is stated: “Then both the people, between whom the controversy is, shall stand** before the Lord, before the priests and the judges that shall be in those days” (Deuteronomy 19:17). Yannai the king **said to him:** I will **not** stand **when you** alone **say** this to me, **but according to what your colleagues say,** and if the whole court tells me, I will stand.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נִפְנָה לִימִינוֹ – כָּבְשׁוּ פְּנֵיהֶם בַּקַּרְקַע. נִפְנָה לִשְׂמֹאלוֹ – וְכָבְשׁוּ פְּנֵיהֶם בַּקַּרְקַע. אָמַר לָהֶן שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן שָׁטַח: בַּעֲלֵי מַחְשָׁבוֹת אַתֶּם, יָבֹא בַּעַל מַחְשָׁבוֹת וְיִפָּרַע מִכֶּם! מִיָּד בָּא גַּבְרִיאֵל וַחֲבָטָן בַּקַּרְקַע, וּמֵתוּ. בְּאוֹתָהּ שָׁעָה אָמְרוּ: מֶלֶךְ לֹא דָּן וְלֹא דָּנִין אוֹתוֹ, לֹא מֵעִיד וְלֹא מְעִידִין אוֹתוֹ.
> **Translation:** Shimon ben Shataḥ **turned to his right.** The judges **forced their faces to the ground** out of fear and said nothing. **He turned to his left, and they forced their faces to the ground** and said nothing. **Shimon ben Shataḥ said to them: You are masters of thoughts,** enjoying your private thoughts, and not speaking. **May the Master of thoughts,** God, **come and punish you. Immediately,** the angel **Gabriel came and struck** those judges **to the ground, and they died. At that moment,** when they saw that the Sanhedrin does not have power to force the king to heed its instructions, the Sages **said: A king does not judge** others **and** others **do not judge him,** and **he does not testify and** others **do not testify concerning him,** due to the danger of the matter.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֹא חוֹלֵץ וְלֹא חוֹלְצִין וְכוּ׳. אִינִי? וְהָאָמַר רַב אָשֵׁי: אֲפִילּוּ לְמַאן דְּאָמַר נָשִׂיא שֶׁמָּחַל עַל כְּבוֹדוֹ – כְּבוֹדוֹ מָחוּל, מֶלֶךְ שֶׁמָּחַל עַל כְּבוֹדוֹ – אֵין כְּבוֹדוֹ מָחוּל, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״שׂוֹם תָּשִׂים עָלֶיךָ מֶלֶךְ״, שֶׁתְּהֵא אֵימָתוֹ עָלֶיךָ! מִצְוָה שָׁאנֵי.
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches that the king **does not perform *ḥalitza*** with his brother’s widow **and** his brother **does not perform *ḥalitza*** with his wife, and Rabbi Yehuda says that he may do so if he wishes. The Gemara challenges Rabbi Yehuda’s opinion: **Is that so? But doesn’t Rav Ashi say: Even according to the one who says** that with regard to **a *Nasi* who relinquished** the **honor** due **him, his honor is relinquished,** nevertheless, with regard to **a king who relinquished** the **honor** due **him, his honor is not relinquished, as it is stated: “You shall set a king over you”** (Deuteronomy 17:15), meaning **that his fear should be upon you.** The preservation of a king’s honor is mandated by the Torah. How could Rabbi Yehuda allow him to waive it? The Gemara answers: **A mitzva is different;** a king is not disgraced if he relinquishes his honor to perform a mitzva.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵין נוֹשְׂאִין כּוּ׳. תַּנְיָא, אָמְרוּ לוֹ לְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה: נָשִׁים הָרְאוּיוֹת לוֹ מִבֵּית הַמֶּלֶךְ. וּמַאי נִינְהוּ? מֵירַב וּמִיכַל.
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches: **And no one may marry** the king’s widow, and Rabbi Yehuda says that a king may marry another king’s widow, as proven by King David, who was promised with regard to King Saul after his death: “And I have given you the house of your master and the wives of your master” (II Samuel 12:8). **It is taught** in a *baraita*: The Sages **said to Rabbi Yehuda:** The meaning of the verse is not that David married Saul’s widows, but that he married **women appropriate for him from the house of the king. And who are they? Merab and Michal,** the daughters of Saul.

We begin the second *perek* of Masechet Sanhedrin, on 19b, starting three lines down from the word *kapasik vetani*. The *perek* is broadly concerned with the special laws governing the dignity and personal status of the *kohen gadol* and the *melech*. The *mishnah* established that the *kohen gadol* does not perform *yibbum*. More precisely, the *mishnah* states: *kohen gadol dan vedannin oto, meid umeidim oto, choletz vecholtzin le-ishto umeyabmin et ishto* — the *kohen gadol* is judged and judges, testifies and others testify against him, performs *chalitzah* and others perform *chalitzah* for his wife, and others may perform *yibbum* with his wife. *Aval hu eino meyabbem* — but he himself does not perform *yibbum* if his brother dies without children. The reason given is *mipnei shehu assur be-almanah*: one of the prohibitions incumbent upon the *kohen gadol* is that he may not marry a widow, and once his brother dies, the sister-in-law becomes an *almanah*, making *yibbum* impossible for him.

### Kohen Gadol Status

The *kohen gadol* bears two distinct laws concerning his personal status in marriage. First, he is prohibited from marrying an *almanah* — a widow. All *kohanim* are prohibited from marrying a divorcee, and the *kohen gadol* bears the additional prohibition against marrying a widow. Second, stated in the form of a positive commandment, the Torah requires him to marry a *betulah*: *betulah yikach*. These two obligations — the *lo ta'aseh* of *almanah lo yikach* and the *aseh* of *betulah yikach* — combine to create the complex halachic tension that the *gemara* proceeds to analyze.

### Erusin vs Nisu'in

The *gemara* opens with the observation that the *mishnah* made no distinction between a brother who had reached the stage of *erusin* — the initiation of marriage, equivalent to *kiddushin*, where the marriage has not yet been consummated — and the stage of *nisu'in*, where the marriage is fully completed. The *mishnah* categorically prohibits the *kohen gadol* from performing *yibbum* in both cases.

## Erusin Nisu'in Debate



### Aseh vs Lo Taseh

The *gemara* explains why the categorical ruling is understandable in the case of *nisu'in*: *bishlama min ha-nisu'in havi aseh velo ta'aseh* — in the case of *nisu'in*, we have both an *aseh* and a *lo ta'aseh* arrayed against the *mitzvah* of *yibbum*. The *mitzvah* of *yibbum* is itself an *aseh*, but the *kohen gadol* faces the *lo ta'aseh* of *almanah lo yikach* and the *aseh* of *betulah yikach* working against him. *Ve-ein aseh docheh lo ta'aseh va-aseh* — the general principle is that a positive commandment does not override the combination of a *lo ta'aseh* and an *aseh*. So in the case of *nisu'in*, the *kohen gadol* faces two prohibitions standing against one *mitzvah*, and the case for prohibiting *yibbum* is clear.

### Aseh Docheh Principle

The difficulty arises with *erusin*. At the stage of *erusin*, we presume the woman is still a *betulah* — she has not had relations with her husband. If we remove the *aseh* of *betulah yikach* from the equation, what remains is simply an *aseh* of *yibbum* against a *lo ta'aseh* of *almanah lo yikach*. And there is a well-established principle — derived from *psukim* in Yevamos — that *aseh docheh lo ta'aseh*, a positive commandment overrides a negative one. If so, the *mishnah* should have drawn a distinction: in the case of *erusin*, the *kohen gadol* should be permitted to perform *yibbum*, since the *mitzvah* of *yibbum* as an *aseh* would override the *lo ta'aseh* alone. Why does the *mishnah* state the prohibition categorically?

### Gzerah Biah Rishonah

The *gemara* answers: *gzeira biah rishonah atu biah shniyah*. There is in fact no biblical prohibition preventing the *kohen gadol* from performing *yibbum* with a *yevamah* from *erusin* — the prohibition is rabbinic. The *chachamim* were concerned that if the first act of *biah* — which is itself the *mitzvah* of *yibbum*, as the *psukim* make clear — were permitted, the couple would continue with a second *biah*. The second *biah* is not a *mitzvah*, and therefore the considerations of *betulah yikach* and *almanah lo yikach* reassert themselves in full force. At that point the *kohen gadol* faces both the *aseh* and the *lo ta'aseh* against him, and the *aseh* of *yibbum* cannot overcome that combination. In order to prevent the *kohen gadol* from arriving at that forbidden second *biah*, the *chachamim* issued a *gzeira* prohibiting even the first *biah*.

It is worth pausing to appreciate what the *gemara* has done here. The *mishnah*'s categorical prohibition, which seemed to ignore the distinction between *erusin* and *nisu'in* entirely, turns out to rest on a sophisticated rabbinic calculation: the very act that would be permitted — indeed, obligatory — at the biblical level is prohibited because of what it leads to. The first *biah* is not problematic in itself; it is prohibited only because it is the natural precursor to a second *biah* that would be genuinely forbidden.

The *gemara* notes that a *baraita* supports this position: *im kadmu u-va'alu biah rishonah, kanu* — if the *kohen gadol* went ahead and violated the *gzeira* and had the first *biah*, the marriage takes effect (*kanu*), but they are thereafter prohibited from continuing with a second *biah* and the relationship must be dissolved.

## Funeral Procession



### Rav Meir Position

The *gemara* now turns to the *mishnah*'s discussion of how the *kohen gadol* conducts himself in relation to a funeral procession. The *mishnah* records a debate between Rav Meir and Rav Yehudah. Rav Meir holds that when the *kohen gadol* has a death in his family — *meis lo meis* — he does not follow the funeral procession directly. Instead, the two processionals alternate their visibility: *hen nichsin v'hu nigleh, hen niglin v'hu nichseh* — when the funeral party is hidden from view, he appears, and when they emerge, he withdraws. The two processionals thus remain perpetually separate. Nevertheless, he does accompany the procession up to the gate of the city: *v'yotzei imahen ad pesach shaar ha-ir*.

### Rav Yehudah Position

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁאֲלוּ תַּלְמִידָיו אֶת רַבִּי יוֹסֵי: הֵיאַךְ נָשָׂא דָּוִד שְׁתֵּי אֲחָיוֹת בְּחַיֵּיהֶן? אָמַר לָהֶן: מִיכַל אַחַר מִיתַת מֵירַב נְשָׂאָהּ. רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ בֶּן קׇרְחָה אוֹמֵר: קִידּוּשֵׁי טָעוּת הָיוּ לוֹ בְּמֵירַב, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״תְּנָה אֶת אִשְׁתִּי אֶת מִיכַל אֲשֶׁר אֵרַסְתִּי לִי בְּמֵאָה עׇרְלוֹת פְּלִשְׁתִּים״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara relates a discussion about David’s marriage to Merab and Michal from a *baraita* (*Tosefta*, *Sota* 11:9): **Rabbi Yosei’s students asked him: How did David marry two sisters while they were** both **alive?** Rabbi Yosei **said to them: He married Michal** only **after the death of Merab,** which is permitted. **Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa says** a different explanation: **His betrothal to Merab was in error** and therefore void from the outset, and so Michal was permitted to him. This is **as it is stated** in the words of King David to Saul’s son Ish-bosheth: **“Deliver me my wife Michal, whom I betrothed to me for one hundred foreskins of the Philistines”** (II Samuel 3:14).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי תַּלְמוּדָא? אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: ״מִיכַל אִשְׁתִּי״, וְלֹא מֵירַב אִשְׁתִּי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What** is **the** biblical **derivation** here? How does Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa learn from this verse that King David’s betrothal to Merab was in error? **Rav Pappa says:** In the verse, David indicates: **Michal** is **my wife but Merab** is **not my wife.**

Rav Yehudah takes a stricter position: *eino yotzei min ha-Mikdash* — the *kohen gadol* may not leave the Temple precinct at all. The *gemara* quotes the *baraita* which presents the *pasuk* underlying Rav Yehudah's ruling: *u-min ha-Mikdash lo yeitzei* — "and from the Temple he shall not depart" (in *Parshas Emor*, in the laws of *aveilus* for the *kohen gadol*). The *baraita* itself, however, reads the *pasuk* in the manner of Rav Meir: *lo yeitze imahen* — he shall not go out *with them*, but *aval yotzei hu achareihem* — he may follow behind them. *Keitzad? Hen nichsin v'hu nigleh, hen niglin v'hu nichseh* — the same alternating arrangement described in the *mishnah*.

The *gemara* says *shapir ka'amar Rav Yehudah* — Rav Yehudah is at least quoting a *pasuk*, and the plain words of that *pasuk* seem to support his position. Rav Meir then offers his *reductio*: *amar lach Rav Meir, i hachi, levayto nami lo* — if Rav Yehudah's concern is that the *kohen gadol* not leave the Temple, then by the same logic he should be prohibited from returning to his home, lest he encounter *tumah* of a corpse along the way. Since we know he is obviously permitted to go home, that reading of the *pasuk* is untenable. This is a striking move: Rav Meir is essentially forcing Rav Yehudah to concede that the *pasuk* cannot be read as a blanket prohibition on leaving the Temple grounds, which in turn demands a different account of what the *pasuk* is actually doing — a question the *gemara* takes up directly in its discussion of how Rav Meir reads *u-min ha-Mikdash lo yeitzei*.

### Rav Meir Pasuk

How then does Rav Meir read *u-min ha-Mikdash lo yeitzei*? *Ela hachi ka'amar: min ha-Mikdash lo yetzei — mikedushato lo yetzei* — the *pasuk* means not that he may not physically leave the Temple building, but that he must not depart from his state of sanctity. The concern the *pasuk* addresses is *tum'at meit*. The *kohen gadol* is prohibited from contracting corpse *tumah*, and the *pasuk* is warning him to guard his sanctity in that regard. *V'keivan de-is lei hekera* — and since he has a *hekera*, a visible act of distinction that separates him from the funeral party, that is sufficient. When a person is publicly and visibly acting in a manner distinct from the procession, he will be reminded to remain on guard and *lo ati leminga* — he will not come to touch anything that is *tamei meis*.

### Yehudah Concern

Rav Yehudah's counter-position is that this *hekera* is inadequate: *agav merira dilma mikrei ve-ati venaga* — in the midst of grief, a person's judgment is diminished, and he may come to inadvertently touch something that causes *tum'at meit*. The visible separation of the two processionals is therefore not a sufficient safeguard. Rav Yehudah thus requires the *kohen gadol* to remain within the Temple entirely.

## Nichimat Avelim



### Temple Officials

A *baraita* now describes the physical arrangements for *nichum avelim* involving the *kohen gadol*. *Tanu rabanan: keshehu over ba-shura lenachem et acherim* — when the *kohen gadol* passes along the *shura*, the line of comfort extended to mourners, the ceremonial attendants accompanying him are: the *sgan* — the assistant to the *kohen gadol* — and the *mashuach she'avar*, both positioned to his right. Rashi explains that the *mashuach she'avar* is a former *kohen gadol* who was temporarily removed from service due to some *psul* — a disqualification — and who was replaced for the interim, but who retains his status and returns to his service when the *psul* passes. To the left of the *kohen gadol* stands the *rosh beis av* — the head of the mourning family — and then *vechol ha'am mismol*, all the people arrayed to the left.

### Kohen Comforts

The physical arrangement thus places the *kohen gadol* at the center, with the *sgan* and *mashuach she'avar* to his right and the mourning family and community to his left. It is worth pausing to note what this arrangement signals about hierarchy: the *mashuach she'avar*, despite having been displaced from active service by a *psul*, retains enough standing to occupy a position of honor beside the *kohen gadol* during a public ceremony — a reminder that his removal was understood as temporary and his status as fundamentally intact.

### Kohen Mourns

The *baraita* then turns to the reverse situation: *keshehu omed ba-shura u-misnachem me-acherim* — when the *kohen gadol* himself is the mourner and receives comfort from others. Here the *sgan* is to his right, and to his left stands the *rosh beis av* and all the people. A careful reader will notice that someone is conspicuously absent from this list: the *mashuach she'avar*, who was present in the previous scenario, does not appear when the *kohen gadol* is the mourner.

### Mashuach Concern

The *gemara* raises the question: *aval mashuach she'avar lo asi gabei?* — does the *mashuach she'avar* not come to visit the *kohen gadol* when he is in mourning? The answer given is: *chalsha date-ih, savar kachadi bi* — the *kohen gadol's* spirit would be weakened by his presence; he would think that the *mashuach she'avar* is inwardly pleased at his misfortune. The logic is that the *mashuach she'avar* was displaced from the position of *kohen gadol* due to his temporary *psul*, and during that interval he fills the role. If something befalls the current *kohen gadol*, the *mashuach she'avar* steps in. There is thus a structural tension between the two — not necessarily personal animosity, but an objective conflict of interests — and the *kohen gadol* in his grief might naturally suspect the *mashuach she'avar* of *schadenfreude*. To protect the *kohen gadol* from this weakening of spirit, the *chachamim* ruled that the *mashuach she'avar* does not attend the mourning house of the *kohen gadol*.

## Pappa's Inferences



### Sgan Mamona

Rav Pappa states: *amar Pappa, shma mina tlat* — from this *baraita* we derive three things. First: *hainu sgan hainu mamona* — the *sgan* is the same figure referred to elsewhere as the *mamona*. Another *baraita* uses the term *mamona* for this official, and Rav Pappa confirms that the two terms refer to the same person.

### Original Practice

Second: *avelim omdim ve-chol ha'am ovrim* — we learn from here that the original procedure for *nichum avelim* was for the mourners to stand and the community to pass by them.

### Positioning Rules

Third: *avelim li-smol ha-menachamim hen omdim* — we learn that the mourners are positioned to the left of those providing comfort, and those providing comfort are the ones standing.

## Historical Development



### Two Families Dispute

A *baraita* now provides the historical context for why the procedure was changed. *Tanu rabbanan: be-rishona hayu avelim omdim ve-chol ha'am ovrim* — originally, the mourners stood and the community passed by. *Ve-hayu shtei mishpachot bi-Yerushalayim mitgarot zu ba-zu* — but there were two families in Jerusalem who would quarrel with one another. *Zot omeret ani overet techila, zot omeret ani overet techila* — each one would insist that she had the right to pass by the mourners first, each claiming priority of honor.

### Takana Reversal

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי קִידּוּשֵׁי טָעוּת? דִּכְתִיב: ״וְהָיָה הָאִישׁ אֲשֶׁר יַכֶּנּוּ יַעְשְׁרֶנּוּ הַמֶּלֶךְ עֹשֶׁר גָּדוֹל וְגוֹ׳״. אֲזַל קַטְלֵיהּ. אָמַר לוֹ: מִלְוָה אִית לָךְ גַּבַּאי, וְהַמְקַדֵּשׁ בְּמִלְוֶה אֵינָהּ מְקוּדֶּשֶׁת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What** caused the betrothal between David and Merab to be **a mistaken betrothal?** The Gemara responds: **As it is written** about Israel’s war against the Philistines and Goliath: **“And it shall be that the man who kills him, the king will enrich him with great riches** and will give him his daughter, and make his father’s house free in Israel” (I Samuel 17:25). David **went** and **killed** Goliath. King Saul **said to him: You have a loan** in **my** possession, as I owe you the great wealth that I promised, though David had not given him an actual monetary loan. **And** the *halakha* is that with regard to **one who betroths** a woman **by** forgiving **a loan, she is not betrothed,** and therefore David’s betrothal of Merab did not take effect.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲזַל יַהֲבַהּ לְעַדְרִיאֵל, דִּכְתִיב: ״וַיְהִי בְּעֵת תֵּת אֶת מֵירַב בַּת שָׁאוּל לְדָוִד וְגוֹ׳״. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אִי בָּעֵית דְּאֶתֵּן לָךְ מִיכַל, זִיל אַיְיתִי לִי מֵאָה עׇרְלוֹת פְּלִשְׁתִּים. אֲזַל אַיְיתִי לֵיהּ. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מִלְוָה וּפְרוּטָה אִית לָךְ גַּבַּאי.
> **Translation:** Saul **went** and **gave** Merab **to Adriel, as it is written: “But it came to pass at the time when Merab, Saul’s daughter, should have been given to David,** that she was given to Adriel the Meholathite as a wife” (I Samuel 18:19). Saul **said to** David: **If you want me to give you Michal, go bring me one hundred foreskins of the Philistines** (see I Samuel 18:25–27). David **went** and **brought** Saul two hundred foreskins. Saul **said to him:** Even though you brought the foreskins, the betrothal is not valid, as **you,** David, **have a loan and** one ***peruta*** in **my** possession, i.e., the wealth Saul owed him for slaying Goliath as well as the item of lesser monetary value, the foreskins of the Philistines.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁאוּל סְבַר: מִלְוָה וּפְרוּטָה – דַּעְתֵּיהּ אַמִּלְוָה, וְדָוִד סְבַר: מִלְוָה וּפְרוּטָה – דַּעְתֵּיהּ אַפְּרוּטָה.
> **Translation:** Saul and David had a halakhic dispute on this point: **Saul reasoned** that in the case of one who betroths a woman **by** forgiving **a loan and** giving her one ***peruta*, his mind** is focused **on the loan** and not on the additional *peruta*, and therefore the betrothal is not valid. **And David reasoned** that in the case of one who betroths a woman **by** forgiving **a loan and** giving her one ***peruta*, his mind** is focused **on the *peruta*** and therefore the betrothal is valid.

To eliminate this conflict, the *chachamim* instituted a *takana*: *hitkinu she-yihiyu ha-am omdim ve-avelim ovrim* — they reversed the procedure, making the community stand and the mourners pass by. Even though this reversal is, in some sense, not the ideal arrangement for the procedures of *halachos aveilus*, the values of *kavod ha-briyos* and the prevention of dispute were deemed sufficient grounds to alter the practice. The awareness that *shalom* is built into the historical origins of this reversal is something worth carrying in mind.

## Rabbi Yosi Takanos



### Shura Restoration

Rabbi Bar Abba reports: *hechzir Rabbi Yosi et ha-davar le-yoshno be-Tzipori* — Rabbi Yosi restored the original procedure in Tzipori, instituting that the mourners would stand and the community would pass by, returning the *shura* to its original form.

### Child Safety Takana

Rabbi Yosi enacted a second *takana* in Tzipori: *shelo tehei isha mehalacḥet ba-shuk u-vnah achareha* — a woman should not walk through the marketplace with her child walking behind her. The reason: *mishum ma'aseh she-hayah* — because of an incident that occurred. Rashi supplies the details of that incident: kidnappers seized the child from behind his mother. When she turned around and did not see him, she began to cry out. One of the kidnappers approached her and said, come, I will show you where he is. When she followed him in, she was assaulted. The *takana* thus requires that the child walk close to the mother rather than trailing behind, to prevent the exploitation of that physical separation.

It is worth noting that this *takana*, like the bathroom *takana* that follows, is oriented around a practical anti-assault concern rather than a formal *halachic* category — a pattern that will become relevant when the Gemara turns to the question of what precisely *yichud* means in the context of Rabbi Yosi's third enactment.

### Bathroom Yichud

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: דְּכוּלֵּי עָלְמָא מִלְוָה וּפְרוּטָה – דַּעְתֵּיהּ אַפְּרוּטָה. שָׁאוּל סְבַר: לָא חֲזוּ וְלָא מִידֵּי, וְדָוִד סְבַר: חֲזוֹ לְכַלְבֵי וְשׁוּנָּרֵי.
> **Translation:** **And if you wish, say** instead: **Everyone** reasons that in the case of one who betroths a woman **by** forgiving **a loan and** giving her one ***peruta*, his mind** is focused **on the *peruta*. Saul reasoned** that foreskins of Philistines **are not fit for any** purpose and as such are worth not even one *peruta*, and that consequently the betrothal did not take effect. **And David reasoned** that **they are fit for dogs and cats** as food and as such are worth at least one *peruta*, and therefore the betrothal takes effect.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יוֹסֵי, הַאי ״תְּנָה אֶת אִשְׁתִּי אֶת מִיכַל״ מַאי דָּרֵישׁ בֵּיהּ? רַבִּי יוֹסֵי לְטַעְמֵיהּ, דְּתַנְיָא: רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הָיָה דּוֹרֵשׁ מִקְרָאוֹת מְעוֹרָבִין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** according to **Rabbi Yosei,** who explains that David married Michal after the death of Merab, with regard to **this** verse: **“Deliver me my wife Michal”** (II Samuel 3:14), **what** does he **derive from it?** The Gemara answers: **Rabbi Yosei** conforms **to his** standard line of **reasoning, as it is taught** in a *baraita* (*Tosefta*, *Sota* 11:8): **Rabbi Yosei would derive** meaning from **mixed verses** that seem contradictory.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּתִיב: ״וַיִּקַּח הַמֶּלֶךְ אֶת שְׁנֵי בְּנֵי רִצְפָּה בַת אַיָּה אֲשֶׁר יָלְדָה לְשָׁאוּל אֶת אַרְמֹנִי וְאֶת מְפִיבֹשֶׁת וְאֶת חֲמֵשֶׁת בְּנֵי מִיכַל אֲשֶׁר יָלְדָה לְעַדְרִיאֵל הַמְּחֹלָתִי וְגוֹ׳״. וְכִי לְעַדְרִיאֵל נִתְּנָה? וַהֲלֹא לְפַלְטִי בֶּן לַיִשׁ נִתְּנָה! דִּכְתִיב: ״וְשָׁאוּל נָתַן אֶת מִיכַל בִּתּוֹ אֵשֶׁת דָּוִד לְפַלְטִי בֶן לַיִשׁ וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** The *Tosefta* continues. **It is written: “But the king took the two sons of Rizpah, daughter of Aiah, whom she bore unto Saul, Armoni and Mephibosheth, and the five sons of Michal,** daughter of Saul, **whom she bore to Adriel,** son of Barzillai **the Meholathite”** (II Samuel 21:8). **But did** Saul **give** Michal **to Adriel? But didn’t he give her to Palti, son of Laish, as it is written: “Now Saul had given Michal his daughter, David’s wife, to Palti, son of Laish”** (I Samuel 25:44)?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מַקִּישׁ קִידּוּשֵׁי מֵירַב לְעַדְרִיאֵל לְקִידּוּשֵׁי מִיכַל לְפַלְטִי: מָה קִידּוּשֵׁי מִיכַל לְפַלְטִי – בַּעֲבֵירָה, אַף קִידּוּשֵׁי מֵירַב לְעַדְרִיאֵל – בַּעֲבֵירָה.
> **Translation:** The *Tosefta* continues: The first verse does not mean, then, that Michal married Adriel. **Rather,** the verse **compares Merab’s betrothal to Adriel to Michal’s betrothal to Palti: Just as Michal’s betrothal to Palti** was effected **in transgression,** according to all opinions, since she was already married to David, **so, too, Merab’s betrothal to Adriel** was effected **in transgression,** since according to *halakha* she was betrothed to David.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ בֶּן קׇרְחָה נָמֵי, הָכְתִיב: ״אֶת חֲמֵשֶׁת בְּנֵי מִיכַל בַּת שָׁאוּל״? אָמַר לְךָ רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: וְכִי מִיכַל יָלְדָה? וַהֲלֹא מֵירַב יָלְדָה! מֵירַב יָלְדָה וּמִיכַל גִּידְּלָה, לְפִיכָךְ נִקְרְאוּ עַל שְׁמָהּ. לְלַמֶּדְךָ שֶׁכׇּל הַמְגַדֵּל יָתוֹם בְּתוֹךְ בֵּיתוֹ מַעֲלֶה עָלָיו הַכָּתוּב כְּאִילּוּ יְלָדוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** according to **Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa as well, isn’t it written: “And the five sons of Michal, daughter of Saul,** whom she bore to Adriel” (II Samuel 21:8). **Rabbi Yehoshua** ben Korḥa could have **said to you** to understand it this way: **And did Michal give birth** to these children? **But didn’t Merab give birth** to them for Adriel? Rather, **Merab gave birth** to them and died, **and Michal raised** them in her house. **Therefore,** the children **were called by her name, to teach you that** with regard to **anyone who raises an orphan in his house, the verse ascribes him** credit **as if he gave birth to him.**

Rabbi Yosi's third *takana* in Tzipori: *sheyihiyu nashim mesaprot be-veit ha-kisse mishum yichud* — women should speak aloud to one another while in the bathroom, on account of the laws of *yichud*. The underlying concern, as Rashi explains, is that a man might approach and, hearing no sound indicating who is present, enter and create a situation of *yichud*.

The Talmud cites the *mishnah*: *lo yityached adam im shtei nashim* — a man may not have *yichud* even with two women. So the remedy is for the two women to speak to each other, thereby alerting any approaching man that two women are present and thus removing the concern of *yichud*.

There is a notable analytical tension here concerning the time element of *yichud*. Ordinarily, *yichud* requires the elapse of a duration equivalent to *zman biah* — perhaps five or six minutes — not merely the existence of a situation. According to Rashi's framing, however, there is no mention of any such time threshold; the setting itself appears to constitute the problem. The Yad Ramah addresses this difficulty and takes a different approach: he understands the women's speaking aloud not as a *halachic* fix for *yichud* in the technical sense, but as a practical deterrent — the noise creates an impression that many people are present, and anyone with harmful intent will be dissuaded. For Rashi, the concern appears to be the *halachic* category of *yichud* itself; for the Yad Ramah, it functions more as an anti-assault protection.

It is worth pausing to note the broader principle embedded in Rabbi Yosi's *takana* here, and indeed in all three of these enactments. The *takana* is not simply a narrow procedural fix for a single incident; it is a formalization of communal vigilance. Even in a private or semi-private setting like a bathroom, where the concern might seem remote, the rabbis required a positive, ongoing act — speaking aloud — to maintain that vigilance. The burden is placed on those present to actively signal safety rather than to assume it.

## Shura Requirements



### Ten Person Minyan

Rav Menashya bar Shlub reports: Rav Yosef asked Rav Yoshiya Rabbah this question at the cemetery in Hutzal, and he was told: *ein shura pechuta me-asara bnei adam* — the *shura* of *nichum avelim* requires a minimum of ten people. *Ve-ein avelim min ha-minyan* — and the mourners themselves do not count toward the ten. This is intuitive: the entire purpose of the *shura* is for the community to gather around the mourners and comfort them. The mourners are the center of the ritual, not participants who constitute it.

### Flexible Arrangement

The same authority ruled: *bein she-avelim omdim ve-chol ha-am ovrim, bein she-avelim ovrim ve-chol ha-am omdim* — whether the mourners stand and the community passes by, or the mourners pass and the community stands, both arrangements are acceptable. The essential requirement is the gathering of ten, not the specific physical choreography.

## Comfort Formula



### Formula Question

The *mishnah* described what is said to the *kohen gadol* when he is in mourning, but did not address what the *kohen gadol* himself says when he performs *nichum avelim* for others. The *gemara* raises this question: *ki menachem hu achrinei, heicha amar lehu?* — when the *kohen gadol* comforts others, what does he say to them?

### Titnachamu Formula

The *gemara* offers: *ta shema, ve-hu omer titnachamu* — come and hear, for he says *titnachamu*, "you shall be comforted." The *gemara* then pauses to clarify: *heichi dami?* — in what case is this said?

The *gemara* considers the possibility that *titnachamu* is said when others are comforting him and he responds. But this reading is rejected immediately: *nechasha ka-rami lehu?* — if he is the mourner and others are comforting him, and he responds with "you shall be comforted," that sounds like a curse. It would be as if a mourner, upon receiving comfort, turns to the comforters and wishes upon them that they too should need to be comforted. This cannot be the intended reading. And it is worth pausing on just how sharp this objection is: the very word that functions as a blessing in one direction becomes, when reversed, something that sounds unmistakably like an ill wish. The identical phrase carries opposite valences depending entirely on who is speaking to whom — a reminder that the formulas of mourning are not merely conventional expressions but structurally sensitive to the roles of the speaker and the recipient.

### Comforter Context

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** (חֲנִינָא קָרָא יוֹחָנָן וְאִשְׁתּוֹ אֶלְעָזָר וּגְאוּלָּה וּשְׁמוּאֵל בְּלִימּוּדוֹ סִימָן.) רַבִּי חֲנִינָא אוֹמֵר, מֵהָכָא: ״וַתִּקְרֶאנָה לוֹ הַשְּׁכֵנוֹת שֵׁם לֵאמֹר יוּלַּד בֵּן לְנׇעֳמִי״. וְכִי נָעֳמִי יָלְדָה? וַהֲלֹא רוּת יָלְדָה! אֶלָּא רוּת יָלְדָה וְנׇעֳמִי גִּידְּלָה, לְפִיכָךְ נִקְרָא עַל שְׁמָהּ.

Rabbi Ḥanina says:** Proof for the aforementioned statement can be derived **from here: “And the neighbors gave him a name, saying: There is a son born to Naomi”** (Ruth 4:17). **And did Naomi give birth** to the son? **But didn’t Ruth give birth** to him? **Rather, Ruth gave birth and Naomi raised** him. **Therefore, he was called by her name:** “A son born to Naomi.”

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר, מֵהָכָא: ״וְאִשְׁתּוֹ הַיְהֻדִיָּה יָלְדָה אֶת יֶרֶד אֲבִי גְדוֹר וְגוֹ׳ וְאֵלֶּה בְּנֵי בִּתְיָה בַת פַּרְעֹה אֲשֶׁר לָקַח (לוֹ) מָרֶד״. מָרֶד זֶה כָּלֵב, וְלָמָּה נִקְרָא שְׁמוֹ מֶרֶד? שֶׁמָּרַד בַּעֲצַת מְרַגְּלִים. וְכִי בִּתְיָה יָלְדָה? וַהֲלֹא יוֹכֶבֶד יָלְדָה! אֶלָּא יוֹכֶבֶד יָלְדָה וּבִתְיָה גִּידְּלָה, לְפִיכָךְ נִקְרָא עַל שְׁמָהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan says:** Proof for the aforementioned statement can be derived **from here: “And his wife Hajehudijah gave birth to Jered the father of Gedor,** and Heber the father of Soco, and Jekuthiel the father of Zanoah, **and these are the sons of Bithiah, daughter of Pharaoh, whom Mered took”** (I Chronicles 4:18). **Mered is Caleb, and why was his name called Mered? Because he rebelled [*marad*] against the counsel of the spies.** And according to the midrash, Jered, Heber, and Jekuthiel all refer to Moses our teacher. **And did Bithiah give birth** to Moses? **But didn’t Jochebed give birth** to him? **Rather, Jochebed gave birth** to him **and Bithiah raised** him. **Therefore, he was called by her name** as though she had given birth to him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר אָמַר, מֵהָכָא: ״גָּאַלְתָּ בִּזְרוֹעַ עַמֶּךָ בְּנֵי יַעֲקֹב וְיוֹסֵף סֶלָה״. וְכִי יוֹסֵף יָלַד? וַהֲלֹא יַעֲקֹב יָלַד! אֶלָּא, יַעֲקֹב יָלַד וְיוֹסֵף כִּילְכֵּל, לְפִיכָךְ נִקְרְאוּ עַל שְׁמוֹ.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Elazar says:** Proof for the aforementioned statement can be derived **from here: “You have with Your arm redeemed your people, the children of Jacob and Joseph, Selah”** (Psalms 77:16). **And did Joseph sire** all of the children of Israel? **But didn’t Jacob sire them? Rather, Jacob sired** them **and Joseph sustained** them financially. **Therefore, they were called by his name;** all of Israel were called the children of Joseph.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר נַחְמָנִי אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹנָתָן: כׇּל הַמְלַמֵּד בֶּן חֲבֵירוֹ תּוֹרָה, מַעֲלֶה עָלָיו הַכָּתוּב כְּאִילּוּ יְלָדוֹ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדֹת אַהֲרֹן וּמֹשֶׁה״, וּכְתִיב: ״וְאֵלֶּה שְׁמוֹת בְּנֵי אַהֲרֹן״. לוֹמַר לָךְ: אַהֲרֹן יָלַד וּמֹשֶׁה לִימֵּד, לְפִיכָךְ נִקְרְאוּ עַל שְׁמוֹ.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani says** that **Rabbi Yonatan says: Anyone who teaches another** person’s **son Torah, the verse ascribes him** credit **as if he sired him, as it is stated: “Now these are the generations of Aaron and Moses”** (Numbers 3:1), **and it is written** immediately afterward: **“And these are the names of the sons of Aaron:** Nadav the firstborn and Avihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar” (Numbers 3:2), but it does not mention the names of Moses’ children. This serves **to say to you** that **Aaron sired** his children, **but Moses taught** them Torah. **Therefore,** the children **were** also **called by his name.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״לָכֵן כֹּה אָמַר ה׳ אֶל בֵּית יַעֲקֹב אֲשֶׁר פָּדָה אֶת אַבְרָהָם״, וְכִי הֵיכָן מָצִינוּ בְּיַעֲקֹב שֶׁפְּדָאוֹ לְאַבְרָהָם? אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: שֶׁפְּדָאוֹ מִצַּעַר גִּידּוּל בָּנִים. וְהַיְינוּ דִּכְתִיב: ״לֹא עַתָּה יֵבוֹשׁ יַעֲקֹב וְגוֹ׳״, ״לֹא עַתָּה יֵבוֹשׁ יַעֲקֹב״ – מֵאָבִיו, ״וְלֹא עַתָּה פָּנָיו יֶחֱוָרוּ״ – מֵאֲבִי אָבִיו.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites another derivation connected to child-rearing: **“Therefore, so says the Lord to the house of Jacob, who redeemed Abraham;** Jacob shall not now be ashamed, neither shall his face now wax pale” (Isaiah 29:22). **But where have we found** any indication **about Jacob that he redeemed Abraham? Rav Yehuda says:** It means **that he redeemed him from the suffering of raising children,** in that Abraham did not have twelve tribes and the resultant hardships involved in raising them, as Jacob did, as Jacob assumed the burden of raising the tribes of Israel. **And this is as it is written: “Jacob shall not now be ashamed,** neither shall his face now wax pale,” meaning: **“Jacob shall not now be ashamed” before his father,** and **“neither shall his face now wax pale” before his father’s father,** since he took upon himself the role that they bore as well.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּתִיב ״פַּלְטִי״ וּכְתִיב ״פַּלְטִיאֵל״. אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: פַּלְטִי שְׁמוֹ, וְלָמָּה נִקְרָא שְׁמוֹ פַּלְטִיאֵל? שֶׁפִּלְּטוֹ אֵל מִן הָעֲבֵירָה. מָה עָשָׂה? נָעַץ חֶרֶב בֵּינוֹ לְבֵינָהּ. אָמַר: כׇּל הָעוֹסֵק בְּדָבָר זֶה יִדָּקֵר בֶּחָרֶב (זֶה).
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites a tradition with regard to Palti, son of Laish: **It is written** in one place **“Palti”** (I Samuel 25:44), **and it is written** in another place **“Paltiel”** (II Samuel 3:15). **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: Palti** was **his** real **name, and why was his name called Paltiel?** To teach **that God [*El* ] saved [*pelato*] him from the sin,** by giving him the insight that he may not touch Michal, understanding that she was still David’s wife and therefore forbidden to him. **What did he do? He embedded a sword** in the bed **between him and her,** and **said: Anyone who engages in this matter,** i.e., sexual intercourse, **should be stabbed by this sword.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָכְתִיב: ״וַיֵּלֶךְ אִתָּהּ אִישָׁהּ״? שֶׁנַּעֲשָׂה לָהּ כְּאִישָׁהּ. וְהָכְתִיב: ״הָלוֹךְ וּבָכֹה״? עַל מִצְוָה דַּאֲזַיל מִינֵּיהּ. ״עַד בַּחֻרִים״, שֶׁנַּעֲשׂוּ שְׁנֵיהֶם כְּבַחוּרִים, שֶׁלֹּא טָעֲמוּ טַעַם בִּיאָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges this: **But isn’t it written: “And her husband went with her,** weeping as he went, and followed her to Bahurim” (II Samuel 3:16), referring to Palti as Michal’s husband? The Gemara responds: This means **that he became like a husband for her** through his affection and concern for her. The Gemara counters: **But isn’t it written** in that very verse: **“weeping as he went”?** If from the outset he thought that she was David’s wife, why was he crying? The Gemara responds: He was weeping **about the mitzva that left him,** as from now on, he would receive no reward for restraining his desire. The end of the verse says that they went **“to Bahurim,”** meaning **that they both became like young men [*baḥurim*]** in **that they did not taste sexual intercourse** at all.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: תּוֹקְפּוֹ שֶׁל יוֹסֵף – עִנְוְותָנוּתוֹ שֶׁל בּוֹעַז, תּוֹקְפּוֹ שֶׁל בּוֹעַז – עִנְוְותָנוּתוֹ שֶׁל פַּלְטִי בֶּן לַיִשׁ. תּוֹקְפּוֹ שֶׁל יוֹסֵף – עִנְוְותָנוּתוֹ שֶׁל בּוֹעַז, דִּכְתִיב: ״וַיְהִי בַּחֲצִי הַלַּיְלָה וַיֶּחֱרַד הָאִישׁ וַיִּלָּפֵת״. מַאי ״וַיִּלָּפֵת״? אָמַר רַב: שֶׁנַּעֲשָׂה בְּשָׂרוֹ כְּרָאשֵׁי לְפָתוֹת.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: Joseph’s power** is the **humility of Boaz,** as Joseph is praised for showing strength with regard to an accomplishment that was insignificant for Boaz (see Genesis, chapter 39). Likewise, **Boaz’s power** is the **humility of Palti, son of Laish,** as Palti’s capacity for restraint was greater still. **Joseph’s power** is the **humility of Boaz, as it is written** about Boaz: **“And it came to pass at midnight that the man was startled and turned himself,** and behold, a woman lay at his feet” (Ruth 3:8). **What** is the meaning of **“and turned himself [*vayyilafet*]”? Rav says:** The meaning is **that his flesh became like the heads of turnips [*lefatot*],** his sexual organ hardening out of arousal, but even though Ruth was not married he refrained from engaging in intercourse with her; while Joseph had to exert more effort, despite the fact that Potiphar’s wife was married.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תּוֹקְפּוֹ שֶׁל בּוֹעַז – עִנְוְותָנוּתוֹ שֶׁל פַּלְטִי בֶּן לַיִשׁ, כְּדַאֲמַרַן.
> **Translation:** **Boaz’s power** is the **humility of Palti, son of Laish, as we said,** for he conquered his desire not only for one night, as Boaz did, but for many nights

The *gemara* therefore concludes: *ela ki menachem le-achrinei, amar lehu titnachamu* — rather, it is when the *kohen gadol* is himself the active comforter of others that he pronounces the phrase *titnachamu*. *Shma mina* — and from this we derive the formula.

One observation worth noting: the word *titnachamu* is in the plural. This is structurally analogous to the standard formula in use today — *ha-Makom yenachem etchem* — which is likewise phrased in the plural even when addressing a single mourner. The formula *titnachamu* similarly uses the plural regardless of the number of mourners present, suggesting that the conventional use of a *lashon rabbim* in the comforting formula may have deep roots in this very practice.
