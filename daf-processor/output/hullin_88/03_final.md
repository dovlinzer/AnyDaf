## Blood & Water Mixtures

Today's *daf* is 88. We begin at the bottom of 87b, continuing the discussion of blood that has become mixed with water or other liquids, and when such a mixture retains the legal status of blood. The previous discussion had addressed whether blood mixed with rainwater qualifies as *machshir zera'im* — that is, whether it renders foodstuff susceptible to becoming impure through contact with liquid. We now continue with several further observations about the nature of blood and blood mixtures.

### Rabbi Assi: Clear Serum

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי אַסִּי מִנְּהַרְבִּיל אוֹמֵר: בִּצְלַלְתָּא דִּדְמָא. רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה מִדִּפְתִּי אָמַר: עָנוּשׁ כָּרֵת, וְהוּא דְּאִיכָּא כְּזַיִת. בְּמַתְנִיתָא תָּנָא: מְטַמְּאִים בְּאֹהֶל, וְהוּא דְּאִיכָּא רְבִיעִית.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Asi of Neharbil says:** The statement of Rav Yehuda is referring **to blood plasma,** i.e., if the plasma has a reddish hue due to the blood, it has the status of blood and can render food susceptible to contracting ritual impurity. **Rabbi Yirmeya of Difti said:** Consumption of this plasma is **punishable by *karet*,** as is the *halakha* with regard to one who consumes blood (see Leviticus 17:14), **provided that there is** at least one **olive-bulk** of actual blood. **It was taught in a *baraita*:** Blood plasma that issues from a corpse that has a reddish hue **imparts ritual impurity in a tent, provided that there is** at least **a quarter-***log* of actual blood, which is the amount of a corpse's blood that imparts ritual impurity.

The *daf* opens with a statement by Rabbi Assi from Naharville. Eight lines from the bottom, the passage begins: *Rabbi Assi mi-Naharville omer: b'tzilusah d'dama* — Rabbi Assi from Naharville says this ruling applies to the clear serum of blood. The earlier statement had held that as long as a blood mixture still appears red, it qualifies as *machshir zera'im*. The *Gemara* had objected: what is the novelty? If blood is mixed with water, the water itself renders food susceptible. The answer, as we saw, was that the case involves blood mixed with rainwater. Now Rabbi Assi addresses a different question entirely — not a mixture of blood with some external liquid, but the clear, watery component that is a natural part of blood itself.

As *Rashi* explains, if blood is allowed to settle, the denser matter collects toward the center while the clearer, more watery portion pools at the edges. Rabbi Assi's ruling is that even this clear, separated serum — even though it no longer appears deeply red — nonetheless retains sufficient connection to blood to qualify as *machshir zera'im*.

### Rabbi Yirmiyah: No Karet

Immediately following is a statement by Rabbi Yirmiyah from Difti: *Rabbi Yirmiyah mi-Difti amar: ein bo karet* — Rabbi Yirmiyah from Difti says there is no punishment of excision for consuming it. There is a question as to what exactly this statement is addressing: is it going on the blood-water mixture discussed previously, or on this water-component of blood just mentioned by Rabbi Assi? *Rashi* is somewhat ambiguous. *Tosafot* says it applies to both — that one would incur *karet* for consuming the water-component of blood precisely because that component was originally all blood and has only now separated into its constituent parts.

But there is a critical qualification: *v'hu d'ika k'zayis* — this only applies if there is a *kezayis* of actual blood present. At first glance this seems unremarkable, since one always requires a *kezayis* of a prohibited substance to incur *karet* — what is the novelty? The novelty becomes clear once we consider the two possible interpretations.

If the ruling concerns blood mixed with water, the point is straightforward: one transgresses only if there is a full *kezayis* of the blood itself within the mixture consumed, not merely a *kezayis* of the mixture as a whole. This is no different from the rule by any other prohibited substance — if forbidden fat were mixed into a stew, one would not transgress merely by eating a *kezayis* of the stew; one would need a *kezayis* of the forbidden fat itself within what was consumed.

If, however, the ruling concerns the separated water-component of blood — not blood mixed with something external, but the natural serum that has settled out from the blood — then the statement is far more striking and nearly neutralizes the previous ruling. In that reading, if blood has settled and its clear watery component has separated off, consuming that liquid does not incur *karet* even if a *kezayis* of the total is present, because the actual red matter within it is less than a *kezayis*. If that is correct, then the water-component of blood is being assigned a curious intermediate status: it is sufficiently connected to blood to function as *machshir zera'im*, yet it is not sufficiently blood to cause *karet* or, as we will see, to convey corpse-impurity. The prohibited substance for purposes of eating and for purposes of conveying impurity is only the solid, red component — not the clear serum that has separated from it. The serum, on this reading, may well be what we would today call blood plasma.

### Tannaim: Blood & Tumah

A *baraita* is then cited: *b'masnisa tana: mitamin b'ohel* — we taught in a *baraita* that such a mixture causes impurity in a tent. Here again the same ambiguity applies — does this refer to blood mixed with water, or to the water-component of blood? And again there is the same qualifying clause: *v'hu d'ika revieis* — only if there is a full *revieis* of blood present. We know that a *revieis* of human blood is required to convey corpse-impurity to everything beneath the same roof. So once again, if this concerns blood mixed with water, the ruling is that the blood must constitute a full *revieis* of the mixture — not that the mixture itself must amount to a *revieis*. If it concerns the separated water-component, the ruling is far more pointed: the clear serum alone does not convey *tumas ohel*; only the red component counts for that purpose.

The text at this point is introduced as *tnan hatam* — we taught "over there." The marginal references in the printed *Gemara* indicate this passage comes from the *Tosefta*, not from a *Mishnah*. The commentary of Rabbi Shimshon, one of the *Ba'alei haTosafot* who wrote a commentary on the *Mishnayot*, notes that all manuscripts read *tnan* — "we taught in a *Mishnah*" — yet the passage appears in no *Mishnah*. *Tosafot* acknowledges that the *Gemara* sometimes uses such language imprecisely or in error.

### Corpse Liquids & Blood

The *baraita* or passage now cited reads: *kol mashkeh meit tehorim chutz midamo* — all liquids of a corpse are *tahor*, they do not share the corpse's impurity-status, except for the blood. As we have seen, the blood of a corpse conveys impurity and indeed conveys *tumas ohel*. Then: *v'chol marei admumit shebo metam'in b'ohel* — and all reddish appearances in it cause *tumah* in the tent. This echoes the earlier point: anything with even a somewhat reddish appearance — perhaps a less intensely red, more liquidy part of the blood — is still treated as blood. And then: *umashkei hameit tehorin* — but all other liquids of the corpse are pure.

The passage seems somewhat repetitive in its structure, but the essential point stands: only blood, and anything bearing a reddish appearance, conveys corpse-impurity; all other bodily fluids of a corpse do not.

## Impure Liquids & Tumah



### Corpse Exception

The *Gemara* now raises a challenge — *raminihu* — against the ruling that the non-blood liquids of a corpse are *tahor*. The challenge comes from a *Mishnah* concerning a *tevul yom*.

[DAF:88a]
### Tevul Yom Status

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵלּוּ וָאֵלּוּ אֵין מְטַמְּאִין, וּשְׁאָר כׇּל הַטְּמֵאִין, בֵּין קַלִּין בֵּין חֲמוּרִין – מַשְׁקִין הַיּוֹצְאִין מֵהֶן כְּמַשְׁקֶה הַנּוֹגֵעַ בָּהֶן, וְאֵלּוּ וָאֵלּוּ תְּחִלָּה, חוּץ מִן הַמַּשְׁקֶה שֶׁהוּא אַב הַטּוּמְאָה.
> **Translation:** **And** both **these and those,** i.e., both liquids that issue from one who immersed himself that day and liquids he touches, **do not impart ritual impurity. And** with regard to **all other** sources of **impurity, whether minor** sources **or major** sources, **liquids that issue from them** have the same status **as liquids that touch them, and** both **these and those have** first-degree ritual impurity, since they touched a primary source of ritual impurity upon exiting the source. This is the *halakha* **except for a liquid that is** itself **a primary source of** ritual **impurity.**

A *tevul yom* is a person who has immersed in the *mikvah* on that day and will attain full purity at nightfall. Until sunset, his status is equivalent to that of a *sheni* — second-degree impurity. He can, for example, invalidate *terumah* by touching it, but he cannot convey impurity further in the ordinary way. The *Mishnah* teaches regarding this person: *k'mashkin shenogea bahen* — liquids that emerge from his body are treated as if he merely touched them. If a *sheni* touches a liquid, that liquid rises to become a *rishon* — first-degree impurity. This requires some explanation.

It is a rabbinic enactment that a person's hands are treated as having second-degree impurity, since we cannot always know where they have been. If such hands come into contact with a liquid, the liquid rises to become a *rishon*. This is a unique property of liquids — that even a *sheni* touching a liquid can cause that liquid to rise a level. (This is also why one must dry one's hands after the ritual washing of *netilat yadayim* before eating bread: the washing was instituted because the *kohanim* would wash their hands before eating *terumah*, since hands with second-degree impurity would invalidate *terumah*. But once water is on the hands, if those hands touch the water before it is shaken off, the water itself becomes a *rishon*, and touching bread with it would re-contaminate. Hence the requirement to dry.)

However, the *tevul yom* is a special case. Because he is already on his way out of impurity — he will be fully pure by nightfall — the Rabbis did not apply the same stringency. If a *tevul yom* touches a liquid, it does not become impure. Therefore, the *Mishnah* rules: *v'eilu v'eilu ein metamin* — neither the *tevul yom*'s own body nor the liquids that emerge from it cause impurity.

The *Mishnah* then continues: *u'sh'ar kol hatemei'im, bein kalim bein chamurim, mashkin hayotzei meihem k'mashkeh shenogea bahem* — all other impure persons, whether of a lighter or a heavier level of impurity, the liquids that emerge from their bodies are treated as if they had touched those liquids. *V'eilu v'eilu techilah* — and in all these cases, the liquid becomes a *rishon*. Even if the person is merely a *rishon* himself and touches a liquid, the liquid rises to a *rishon*; even a *sheni* touching a liquid makes it a *rishon*, because of the special stringency that applies to liquids.

There is one exception: *chutz min hamashkeh shehu av hatumah* — except when the liquid itself is already an *av hatumah*, a primary source of impurity. Which liquids are *avot hatumah* in their own right? Those that emerge from persons whose very impurity derives from what emerges from their bodies — the *niddah*, the *zavah*, and the *zav*. For these persons, certain bodily fluids are themselves primary sources of impurity. Specifically: saliva (*roki*), urine, and semen — the three fluids that the Rabbis understand to be included in the *Torah*'s language of *v'chi yarok hazav al hatahor*, "if the *zav* spits on a pure person." From the word "spitting" the *Gemara* derives all substances that collect in the body before being expelled — which includes semen and urine, but not blood, not tears, and not sweat. These latter do not collect internally in the same way, and therefore the blood and tears of a *zav*, *zavah*, or *niddah*, while *tamei*, are only a *rishon*, not an *av hatumah*.

One might note that the *ba'al keri* — a person made impure through an emission — should also be considered in this context. Whether the emission of a *ba'al keri* is itself an *av hatumah* is a matter of separate discussion, touching on the question of whether such a person is a *ro'eh* or merely a *nogea*.

An important clarification regarding the seven liquids that are formally recognized as susceptible to, and capable of conveying, impurity: these are *yayin* (wine), *devash* (honey), *shemen* (oil), *chalav* (milk), *tal* (dew), *dam* (blood), and *mayim* (water). Not everything with a water component qualifies as *mayim* for this purpose — the very fact that dew must be listed separately, even though it is essentially pure water, demonstrates that the category of "water" was understood in a more restricted sense. It follows that tears and urine are not formally among the seven recognized liquids, and therefore, *Tosafot* suggests, they may not be biblically subject to impurity at all. On this reading, when a liquid not among the seven emerges from a corpse and touches the skin, it may simply not be classified as a *liquid* in the relevant biblical sense, and thus never becomes impure in the first place — not even as a *rishon*.

### Zav vs. Corpse Logic

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי קַלִּין וּמַאי חֲמוּרִין? מַאי לָאו: קַלִּין – שֶׁרֶץ וְזָב, וַחֲמוּרִין – מֵת? לָא, קַלִּין – שֶׁרֶץ, וַחֲמוּרִין – זָב.
> **Translation:** The Gemara analyzes the mishna: **What** is the meaning of **minor** sources **and what** is the meaning of **major** sources? **What, is it not** that the term: **Minor** sources, is referring to **a creeping animal or a *zav*, and** that the term: **Major** sources, is referring to **a corpse?** If so, the mishna teaches that all liquids that issue from a corpse are ritually impure, in contradiction to the *baraita*. The Gemara responds: **No,** the term: **Minor** sources, is referring to **a creeping animal,** e.g., urine found inside its body, which is not considered ritually impure, **and** the term: **Major** sources, is referring to **a *zav*,** concerning whom all liquids that issue from him are ritually impure. By contrast, liquids that issue from a corpse are ritually pure with the exception of blood.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי שְׁנָא זָב, דִּגְזַרוּ בֵּיהּ רַבָּנַן, וּמַאי שְׁנָא מֵת דְּלָא גְּזַרוּ בֵּיהּ רַבָּנַן? זָב דְּלָא בְּדִילִי אִינָשֵׁי מִינֵּיהּ – גְּזַרוּ בֵּיהּ רַבָּנַן, מֵת דִּבְדִילִי אִינָשֵׁי מִינֵּיהּ – לָא גְּזַרוּ בֵּיהּ רַבָּנַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is different** with regard to **a *zav*, concerning whom the Sages decreed** that liquids that issue from him are ritually impure, **and what is different** with regard to **a corpse, concerning which the Sages did not decree** that liquids that issue from it are ritually impure? The Gemara responds: In the case of **a *zav*, since people do not** naturally **separate from him, the Sages decreed** additional restrictions **with regard to him** in order to prevent others from contracting impurity from liquids that issue from a *zav* that are impure by Torah law. In the case of **a corpse, since people** naturally **separate from it, the Sages did not decree** additional restrictions **with regard to it.**

Having established that all other impure persons' liquids are at least a *rishon*, the *Gemara* raises the apparent contradiction with the ruling that non-blood liquids of a corpse are *tahor*. The challenge: *mai shena zav de-gazru bei rabbanan u-mai shena met de-lo gazru bei rabbanan?* — What is the difference by a *zav*, where the Rabbis enacted a decree, and by a corpse, where no decree was enacted?

The irony is pointed: the corpse is the heaviest source of impurity — the *avi avot hatumah*, the ultimate origin of all impurity — yet its non-blood liquids are treated more leniently than those of a *zav*, whose impurity is far lesser.

The *Gemara* answers: *zav de-lo badili inshi minei gazru bei rabbanan* — by a *zav*, people do not naturally keep their distance. First, his condition is not repulsive in the way a corpse is. Second, as *Rashi* notes, no one can tell who is a *zav* or who is a *niddah* simply by looking at them. Because people are thus unlikely to exercise caution around them and are prone to inadvertent contact, the Rabbis had to extend the impurity to their bodily liquids as well, so that people who care about maintaining purity would remain alert.

By contrast: *met de-badili inshi minei* — people instinctively keep away from a corpse. As *Rashi* also notes, a corpse is clearly identifiable. People who are trying to remain pure naturally avoid it. Therefore, *lo gazru bei rabbanan* — the Rabbis did not need to extend the impurity to the corpse's non-blood liquids, since the natural aversion already provides sufficient protection.

*Tosafot* then asks a more fundamental question: why do we describe the impurity of liquids emerging from a *zav* as rabbinic? When any liquid emerges from a body, it is touching that body. Should it not be *tamei* at the biblical level on account of having contacted an *av hatumah*? *Tosafot* offers two answers. The first, admittedly hypothetical, is that the case concerns a scenario where the liquid was somehow extracted without ever contacting the outer surface of the skin — perhaps drawn off through a siphon of some kind. The second answer is more conceptually significant: perhaps tears, urine, and similar fluids are simply not classified as formal liquids at the biblical level. Just as a corpse cannot convey impurity to a rock — since impurity only attaches to people, food, and vessels — if these substances do not meet the biblical definition of liquid, they would not become impure through contact even with the most severe source of impurity, and would only be subject to rabbinic extension.

### Seven Liquids Rule

This leads naturally back to the list of seven recognized liquids — *yayin, devash, shemen, chalav, tal, dam, mayim* — encoded in the mnemonic *yad shachat dam*. Not all seven are present in the human body, and not everything chemically similar to one of the seven necessarily shares its legal status. Tears, while largely composed of water, are not classified as *mayim* for this purpose — a conclusion supported by the very fact that dew must be listed explicitly despite being water. Blood of a corpse is *tamei*; the other fluids are not. The blood and other bodily fluids of a *zav* and *niddah* are subject to their own distinct rules. With these distinctions in place, the *Gemara* is able to account for the seemingly paradoxical leniency regarding corpse-liquids, and the discussion of blood-and-water mixtures is brought to its conclusion.

## Covering Blood Shechita



### Damo Debate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דָּם הַנִּיתָּז וְשֶׁעַל הַסַּכִּין, תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״וְכִסָּהוּ״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁדַּם הַנִּיתָּז וְשֶׁעַל הַסַּכִּין חַיָּיב לְכַסּוֹת. אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: אֵימָתַי? בִּזְמַן שֶׁאֵין שָׁם דָּם אֶלָּא הוּא, אֲבָל יֵשׁ שָׁם דָּם שֶׁלֹּא הוּא – פָּטוּר מִלְּכַסּוֹת.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that one is obligated to cover **blood that spurts** outside the pit over which the animal was slaughtered, or onto the wall, **and** blood **that** remained **on the** slaughtering **knife.** With regard to this *halakha*, **the Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states: **“And he shall cover it”** (Leviticus 17:13), which **teaches that** one is **obligated to cover blood that spurted and** blood **that** remained **on the** slaughtering **knife. Rabbi Yehuda said: When** is this the *halakha*? **When there is no blood except that** blood. **But** if **there is** other **blood that is not that** blood, one is **exempt from covering** it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תַּנְיָא אִידַּךְ: ״וְכִסָּהוּ״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁכׇּל דָּמוֹ חַיָּיב לְכַסּוֹת, מִכָּאן אָמְרוּ: דָּם הַנִּיתָּז וְשֶׁעַל אֲגַפַּיִים חַיָּיב לְכַסּוֹת. אָמַר רַבָּן שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל: בַּמֶּה דְּבָרִים אֲמוּרִים – שֶׁלֹּא כִּסָּה דַּם הַנֶּפֶשׁ, אֲבָל כִּסָּה דַּם הַנֶּפֶשׁ – פָּטוּר מִלְּכַסּוֹת.
> **Translation:** **It is taught** in **another** *baraita*: The term: **“And he shall cover it,” teaches that** one is **obligated to cover all** of the **blood. From here** the Sages **stated:** With regard to **blood that spurts out and** blood **that** remains **on the sides** of the animal’s throat where it was slaughtered, one is **obligated to cover** it. **Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel said: In what** case **is this statement said?** It is said in a case **where** one **did not** already **cover the blood of the soul,** i.e., the blood that flows from the place of slaughter as the animal dies. **But if** one already **covered the blood of the soul,** he is **exempt from** the obligation **to cover** the blood that spurted out or the blood remaining on the sides of the animal’s neck in the area of slaughter.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּמַאי קָא מִיפַּלְגִי? רַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: ״דָּמוֹ״ – כׇּל דָּמוֹ, רַבִּי יְהוּדָה סָבַר: ״דָּמוֹ״ – וַאֲפִילּוּ מִקְצָת דָּמוֹ, וְרַבָּן שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל סָבַר: ״דָּמוֹ״ – הַמְיוּחָד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **With regard to what** matter **do they disagree?** The Gemara responds: **The Rabbis hold** that the expression: **“Its blood”** (Leviticus 17:13), indicates an obligation to cover **all of its blood,** even the blood that spurts out. **Rabbi Yehuda holds** that **“its blood”** indicates that one may fulfill the mitzva with any part of the blood, **even** with **a small amount of its blood. And Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds** that **“its blood”** refers to the **special** blood, i.e., the blood of the soul.

Having concluded the extended discussion of blood mixtures and their impurity-status, the *Gemara* now turns to the *Mishnah* on 87b concerning the obligation of *kissuy hadam* — the covering of blood after slaughter. The *Mishnah* had raised the case of blood that splatters (*hanitaz*) or remains on the knife (*she'al hasakin*): must one cover it as part of the *mitzvah*?

A *baraita* is cited: *Tannu Rabbanan: v'chiseihu* — you shall cover it. *Melamed she'dam hanitaz v'she'al hasakin chayav lechasot* — this teaches that the blood which splatters and the blood on the knife must be covered. Rabbi Yehuda then says: *eimasai? bizman she'ein sham dam ela hu* — when is this so? Only when there is no other blood present. But if there is other blood — *aval yeish sham dam she'lo hu, pattur mil'chasos* — one is exempt from covering the splattered blood or the blood on the knife.

A second *baraita* is then cited: *Tanya idach: v'chiseihu, melamed she'kol damo chayav lechasot* — a different *baraita* teaches that one must cover all of the blood. *Mikan amru: dam hanitaz v'she'al ha'agafayim chayav lechasot* — from here they said that blood that splatters and blood on the walls of the slaughterhouse (*agafayim*, literally "wings," which *Rashi* explains as the walls) must also be covered.

Then Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel states: *bameh devarim amurim? she'lo kissa dam hanefesh* — in what circumstances do those rulings apply? Only when one has not yet covered the life-blood — the blood that flows out immediately upon slaughter. *Aval kissa dam hanefesh, pattur mil'chasos* — but if one has covered the life-blood, one is exempt from covering the rest.

The *Gemara* then identifies the underlying three-way dispute: *b'mai kamifligi?* — what is the point of contention? The Rabbis hold that *damo* — "its blood" — means *kol damo*, all of its blood; therefore every drop, wherever it lands, must be covered. Rabbi Yehuda holds that *damo* — *afilu miktzas damo* — means even a portion of its blood suffices; there is no obligation to track down every splatter. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds that *damo* means *damo hameyuchad* — the particular, most significant blood, which is the life-blood that flows at the moment of slaughter.

### Pesak & Practice

How do we rule? The question is genuinely debated among the *Rishonim*. Ordinarily, we would follow the *tanna kamma* — the majority position. But there is a general principle that when Rabbi Yehuda introduces his ruling with the word *eimasai* — "when?" — he is understood to be clarifying the *tanna kamma*, not disagreeing with it. The complication is that the *Gemara* here explicitly frames the discussion as a three-way dispute, which suggests Rabbi Yehuda is not clarifying. Several *Rishonim*, including the *Rambam*, conclude that Rabbi Yehuda is indeed offering a clarification and that we rule accordingly. *Tosafot* considers two positions but appears to favor this view as well. The *Ra'avad*, by contrast, holds that Rabbi Yehuda is a dissenting opinion and that we rule like the *Chachamim* — one must cover all the blood.

In practice, however, even those who rule like the *Chachamim* would find it nearly impossible to require the covering of every splatter in a slaughterhouse setting — the amount of effort required would be extraordinary, and blood that reaches walls or splatters widely cannot realistically be gathered. One is effectively compelled to rule like Rabbi Yehuda or Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel in practice. Rabbi Yehuda's position, taken literally, is actually the most lenient: *miktzas damo* means any amount suffices, so theoretically a token covering would discharge the biblical obligation. In practice, however, even according to Rabbi Yehuda, the ideal is to cover as much blood as possible; the rule that *miktzas* suffices means that blood one cannot practically reach need not be pursued, not that one should deliberately do only a minimal amount.

In current slaughterhouse practice, the bulk of the blood is covered during and immediately after the slaughter, while the primary flow is going — the *pirkas* phase — and the knife is cleaned between one slaughter and the next. Any blood that goes into the sink or drain is handled as a practical matter, not separately gathered into dirt for *kissuy*.

## Materials for Kissuy



### Granular vs. Thick

The next *Mishnah* addresses the question of what materials may and may not be used for *kissuy hadam*: *Bameh mechassin uvameh ein mechassin* — with what does one cover, and with what does one not cover? The structure echoes *bameh madlikin uvameh ein madlikin* — the second chapter of tractate *Shabbat* — and raises the same formal question of which category is listed first.

The *Mishnah* states: *mechassin* — one may cover with the following: *be-zevel hadak* — thin manure (the granular, not the clumpy kind); *be-chol hadak* — thin sand; *be-sid* — plaster; *be-charsit* — crushed pottery; *u-ve-leveinah u-ve-magufah she-ketashan* — a brick or a barrel-seal, provided they have been ground into granular form. These last two items require processing before they may be used.

But one may not cover: *lo be-zevel hagas* — with thick, clumpy manure; *ve-lo be-chol hagas* — with thick, clumpy sand; *ve-lo be-leveinah u-ve-magufah shelo ketashan* — with a brick or barrel-seal that has not been ground; *ve-lo yichpeh alav et hakeli* — and one may not simply place a vessel over the blood.

The distinction being drawn is twofold. First, the material must be granular — not a solid object that is placed and removed. As *Rashi* explains, there is a conceptual difference between covering something with a solid object (which simply rests on top and can be lifted away) and covering something with granular material (which settles into and becomes part of the ground, so that the blood is genuinely buried beneath it). Second, as we will see, the material must be something in which vegetation can grow — something organically similar to earth.

The imagery here is worth pausing over. There is an interesting counterpoint in the *Torah* regarding blood and the earth. When God speaks to *Kayin* after he has killed *Hevel*, the language is: *kol d'mei achicha tzo'akim eilai min ha'adamah* — the blood of your brother cries out to Me from the ground. And later the *Torah* speaks of blood that cannot be atoned for except through the blood of the one who spilled it — *ha'aretz lo yechupar ki im b'dam shafcha*. There is also the phrase *eretz al techasi damam* — O earth, do not cover their blood. In murder, to cover the blood is to conceal a crime; the earth itself is described as crying out. Here, in the case of legitimately slaughtered animals, the blood must be covered — properly, respectfully buried in the earth, as it were. The contrast is between blood that was wrongly spilled and must cry out, and blood that was lawfully spilled and must be put away.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ בַּמֶּה מְכַסִּין וּבַמֶּה אֵין מְכַסִּין? מְכַסִּין בְּזֶבֶל הַדַּק, וּבְחוֹל הַדַּק, בְּסִיד, וּבְחַרְסִית, וּבִלְבֵנָה וּבִמְגוּפָה שֶׁכְּתָשָׁן, אֲבָל אֵין מְכַסִּין לֹא בְּזֶבֶל הַגַּס, וְלֹא בְּחוֹל הַגַּס, וְלֹא בִּלְבֵנָה וּבִמְגוּפָה שֶׁלֹּא כְּתָשָׁן, וְלֹא יִכְפֶּה עָלָיו אֶת הַכְּלִי. כְּלָל אָמַר רַבָּן שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל: דָּבָר שֶׁמְּגַדֵּל בּוֹ צְמָחִים – מְכַסִּין בּוֹ, וְשֶׁאֵינוֹ מְגַדֵּל צְמָחִים – אֵין מְכַסִּין בּוֹ.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **With what** substances **may one cover** the blood **and with what** substances **may one not cover** the blood? **One** may **cover** the blood **with fine** granulated **manure, with fine sand, with lime, with** crushed **potsherd, and with a brick or** the **lid** of an earthenware barrel **that one crushed. But one may not cover** the blood **with thick manure, nor with thick,** clumped **sand, nor with a brick or the lid** of an earthenware barrel **that one did not crush. Neither may one** merely **turn a vessel over** the blood. **Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel stated a principle:** With regard to **a substance in which plants grow, one may cover** blood **with it; and** with regard to a substance in **which plants do not grow, one may not cover** blood **with it.**

### RSBG: Growth Principle


Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel then articulates a general principle: *klal amar Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel: davar she-megadel bo tzmachim mechassin bo, ve-she'eino megadel bo tzmachim ein mechassin bo* — something in which plants can grow, one may cover with it; something in which plants cannot grow, one may not cover with it.

This appears not to be a dissenting opinion but a further clarification of the *tanna kamma*'s position. Yet it does not seem fully sufficient on its own: one might ask whether a thick clump of manure or a thick clump of earth could not also support plant growth. Clearly it can. So the principle of *megadel tzmachim* and the principle of granularity seem to operate as two independent requirements: the material must (a) be granular in form and (b) be the kind of substance in which things can grow.

One student noted that there may be a deeper organic connection being expressed here: the blood is a life-source, and the material used to cover it must itself be a medium that sustains life. This is a compelling reading — not merely that the covering material must resemble earth in its texture, but that it must share in earth's generative quality, returning what was a source of life to something that nurtures life again.

### Potter's Standard

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ הֵיכִי דָמֵי חוֹל הַדַּק? אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר בַּר חָנָה אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: כֹּל שֶׁאֵין הַיּוֹצֵר צָרִיךְ לְכׇתְשׁוֹ. וְאִיכָּא דְּמַתְנֵי לַהּ אַסֵּיפָא: אֲבָל אֵין מְכַסִּין לֹא בְּזֶבֶל הַגַּס וְלֹא בְּחוֹל הַגַּס. הֵיכִי דָּמֵי חוֹל הַגַּס? אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר בַּר חָנָה אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: כֹּל שֶׁהַיּוֹצֵר צָרִיךְ לְכׇתְשׁוֹ.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The mishna teaches that one may cover the blood with fine sand. The Gemara asks: **What is considered fine sand? Rabba bar bar Ḥana says** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says:** It is **any** sand **that the** pottery **producer does not need to crush** in order to use it. The Gemara notes: **And some teach this** statement **in reference to the latter clause** of the mishna, which states: **But one may not cover** the blood **with thick manure, nor with thick sand.** The Gemara asks: **What is considered thick sand? Rabba bar bar Ḥana says** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says:** It is **any** sand **that the** pottery **producer must crush** in order to use it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי בֵּינַיְיהוּ? אִיכָּא בֵּינַיְיהוּ דִּצְרִיךְ וְלָא צְרִיךְ, דְּמִיפְּרִיךְ אִיפְּרוֹכֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is** the practical difference **between** these two versions? The Gemara responds: **There is** a difference **between them** with regard to sand **that requires** some crushing **and** sand that **does not require** full crushing, i.e., **that crumbles** in one’s hand and does not require a tool. According to the first version, as long as the sand does not require crushing it may be used to cover the blood. Therefore, sand that requires crumbling may be used since it is not considered sand that requires crushing. According to the second version, any sand that requires some crushing may not be used. Therefore, sand that requires crumbling may not be used to cover the blood.

The *Gemara* then asks: *heichi dami chol hadak* — what exactly qualifies as thin sand? *Amar Rabbah bar bar Chana amar Rabbi Yochanan: kol she'ein hayotzer tzarich lechatsho* — anything that a potter does not need to grind before using it to make clay. Potters mix sand into their clay, and if the sand is already fine enough to use as-is, that is thin sand.

Some teach this ruling in connection with the latter clause of the *Mishnah* instead: *heichi dami chol hagas* — what is thick sand? *Amar Rabbah bar bar Chana amar Rabbi Yochanan: kol shehayotzer tzarich lekatsho* — anything that the potter does need to grind before using it. *Mai beinayhu?* — What is the practical difference between these two formulations? *Ika beinayhu ditzrich velo tzarich* — the difference lies in something that can be crumbled by hand but does not strictly require a grinder: *demifrach parich* — it crumbles easily if you press it, but formal grinding is not necessary. Under the formulation that defines thin sand as what the potter does not need to grind, this intermediate case would fall into the thin category, since the potter can manage without a grinder. Under the formulation that defines thick sand as what the potter does need to grind, this intermediate case would fall into the thin category as well, since he does not strictly need to grind it. The difference, then, is in how one frames the borderline case — and this is a recurring logical pattern worth noticing: defining one end of a spectrum necessarily shifts where the ambiguous middle lands, because the same intermediate substance can be described either way depending on which pole you treat as the anchor.

## Klal u'Prat Analysis

### Klal Tzarich l'Prat

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״וְכִסָּהוּ״, יָכוֹל יְכַסֶּנּוּ בַּאֲבָנִים אוֹ יִכְפֶּה עָלָיו אֶת הַכְּלִי? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״בֶּעָפָר״. וְאֵין לִי אֶלָּא עָפָר, מִנַּיִן לְרַבּוֹת זֶבֶל הַדַּק, וְחוֹל הַדַּק, וּשְׁחִיקַת אֲבָנִים, וּשְׁחִיקַת חַרְסִית, וּנְעוֹרֶת פִּשְׁתָּן דַּקָּה,
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states with regard to the mitzva of covering the blood: **“And cover it”** (Leviticus 17:13). One **might** have thought that **he may cover** the blood **with stones or** merely **turn a vessel over it.** Therefore, **the verse states: “With earth.”** Based on this expression **I have** derived **only** that **earth** may be used. **From where** does one derive **to include fine manure, fine sand, crushed stones, crushed potsherd, fine chaff of flax,**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּנְסוֹרֶת שֶׁל חָרָשִׁין דַּקָּה, וְסִיד, וְחַרְסִית לְבָנָה וּמְגוּפָה שֶׁכְּתָשָׁן? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וְכִסָּהוּ״. יָכוֹל שֶׁאֲנִי מְרַבֶּה אַף זֶבֶל הַגַּס, וְחוֹל הַגַּס, וּשְׁחִיקַת כְּלֵי מַתָּכוֹת, וּלְבֵנָה וּמְגוּפָה שֶׁלֹּא כְּתָשָׁן, וְקֶמַח, וְסוּבִּין, וּמוּרְסָן? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״בֶּעָפָר״.
> **Translation:** **fine sawdust of carpenters, lime,** crushed **potsherd,** and **a brick** or the **lid** of an earthenware barrel **that one crushed? The verse states: “And cover it,”** i.e., with any substance. One **might** have thought **that I** will **include even thick manure, and thick sand, filings of metal vessels, a brick** or **a lid that one did not crush, flour, bran, and coarse bran.** Therefore, **the verse states:** “And cover it **with earth,”** indicating that not all substances may be used to cover the blood.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמָה רָאִיתָ לְרַבּוֹת אֶת אֵלּוּ וּלְהוֹצִיא אֶת אֵלּוּ? אַחַר שֶׁרִיבָּה הַכָּתוּב וּמִיעֵט, מְרַבֶּה אֲנִי אֶת אֵלּוּ שֶׁהֵן מִין עָפָר, וּמוֹצִיא אֲנִי אֶת אֵלּוּ שֶׁאֵינָן מִין עָפָר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And what did you see** that led you **to include these** substances **and to exclude those?** The Gemara responds: **After** noting **that the verse included** certain substances with the term: “And cover it,” **and excluded** others with the term: “With earth,” **I include these** substances, e.g., fine sand, **which are a type of earth** in which plants grow, **and I exclude those** substances, e.g., thick sand, **which are not a type of earth,** as plants do not grow in them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵימָא: ״וְכִסָּהוּ״ – כָּלַל, ״עָפָר״ – פָּרַט, כְּלָל וּפְרָט – אֵין בַּכְּלָל אֶלָּא מַה שֶּׁבַּפְּרָט, עָפָר – אִין, מִידֵּי אַחֲרִינָא – לָא!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: Why must the verse be interpreted in this manner? **Say** that the term: **“And cover it,”** is **a generalization,** and the term: **“With earth,”** is **a detail.** Consequently, the verse constitutes **a generalization and a detail,** and according to the corresponding hermeneutical principle, **the generalization includes only what** is mentioned explicitly **in the detail.** Therefore, only **earth may** be used to cover the blood, while **any other** substance, even substances in which plants grow, may **not** be used.

The *Gemara* now presents a *baraita* that derives the full range of permissible materials from the biblical verse: *Tanu Rabbanan: vekhissahu — yakhol yekhasenu be'avanim o yikhpeh alav et hakeli* — one might think he may cover the blood with stones or simply place a vessel on top of it. *Talmud lomar be'afar* — the verse teaches: with dust. So the *Torah* specifies *afar* (dust or earth), excluding stones and vessels.

*Ve'ein li ella afar* — I know only dust itself. From where do I derive to include thin manure and thin sand, ground stones (*shchikat avanim*), ground pottery (*shchikat charsit*), very thin stalks of flax (*ne'oret shel pishtan dakah*), thin sawdust (*nesoret shel charashim dakah*), plaster, brick, and barrel-seals that have been ground? *Talmud lomar vekhissahu* — the verse also says "and he shall cover it," which is a broader term. So we use the broader term (*vekhissahu*) to expand beyond dust alone.

*Yakhol she'ani marbeh zevel hagas ve-chol hagas u-shchikat kelei mateches u-leveinah u-magufah shelo katshan* — one might think I should also include thick manure, thick sand, ground metal vessels, or unground brick and barrel-seal. *Ve-kemach ve-subin u-morsan* — or even flour, bran, and other finely ground plant products. *Talmud lomar be'afar* — the verse reverts to the limiting term: *afar*. *Uma ra'ita lerabbot elu u-lehotzi et elu?* — what is your principled distinction: which things do you include and which do you exclude? *Achar sheriba hakatuv u-mieat, marbeh ani et elu shehen min afar u-motzi ani et elu... min afar* — after the *Torah* both expanded and contracted, I include those that are a type of dust and exclude those that are not a type of dust. Being "a type of dust" means being granular, and presumably also being of the natural, organic world — hence flaxseed and ground stone are included, while metal and flour are not. The precise lines of distinction are not fully transparent in every case.

The *Gemara* then raises a sharp question: *eima — vekhissahu* כלל, *be'afar* פרט — why not read *vekhissahu* as the *klal* (the general statement) and *be'afar* as the *prat* (the specific term), and apply the principle of *klal u'prat*: *klal u'prat ein baklal ela mah shebaprat* — when a general statement is followed by a specific term, only the specific is included? *Afar in, midei achrini lo* — only dust itself qualifies, and nothing else.

### Rashi & Tosafot

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב מָרִי: מִשּׁוּם דְּהָוֵה כְּלָל הַצָּרִיךְ לִפְרָט, וְכׇל כְּלָל הַצָּרִיךְ לִפְרָט – אֵין דָּנִין אוֹתוֹ בִּכְלָל וּפְרָט.
> **Translation:** **Rav Mari said** in response: One should not suggest such an interpretation **because** the term “and cover it” **is a generalization that requires a detail** to clarify its nature, **and any generalization that requires a detail** to clarify its nature **is not interpreted by** the hermeneutical principle of **a generalization and a detail.** It is necessary for the verse to state that the blood must be covered with earth in order to clarify that the mitzva of covering the blood is such that the blood must be covered with a substance that will absorb the blood, and that it does not suffice to place a vessel over it.

The *Gemara* answers in the name of Rav Mari: *mishum dehavah klal hatzarich liprat* — because this is a *klal* that requires the *prat* in order to be understood at all. *V'khol klal hatzarich liprat ein danin oto b'klal u'prat* — whenever the general term cannot stand on its own without the particular to clarify it, the standard rule of *klal u'prat* does not apply. This principle is itself one of the thirteen hermeneutical rules: *miklal she'hu tzarich liprat u'miprat she'hu tzarich liklal*.

*Rashi* and *Tosafot* understand *klal hatzarich liprat* in two distinct ways, and it is important to keep them separate.

*Rashi* explains that the *klal* here requires the *prat* because even the *verb* is ambiguous. *Vekhissahu* — "and he shall cover it" — could refer to covering in the manner of placing a blanket or vessel on top; or it could refer to covering in the manner of burying something beneath granular material, so that it is submerged rather than merely overlaid. These are qualitatively different acts. The word *vekhissahu* alone does not tell us which kind of covering is meant. The *prat* — *be'afar*, with dust — is needed not merely to specify what object to use, but to tell us what kind of covering is required at all. Because the *klal* is genuinely unclear without the *prat*, it cannot function as a standard *klal u'prat* pair that limits the scope to the *prat* alone.

*Tosafot* offers a more direct explanation. The word *tzarich* here does not mean that the *klal* needs the *prat* to be understood — rather, the *prat* (*be'afar*) was *needed for a different purpose entirely*. Earlier in the discussion, we learned from *be'afar* that there must be earth both below and above the blood — the blood must be placed on earth and covered with earth. The *Torah* needed to use a specific noun (*afar*) to convey that teaching. Because *be'afar* was *needed* to teach something else (the requirement of earth below), it cannot simultaneously function as an exclusive *prat* that limits the verb *vekhissahu* to dirt alone. Since the *prat* had to appear for a separate reason, its limiting force is undermined, and we cannot apply *klal u'prat* in the standard way.

### Ribuy u'Miut Method

In principle, one might have applied the alternative hermeneutical framework of *ribuy u'miut* — inclusion and exclusion — which achieves a similar effect to *klal u'prat* but through a looser pairing of a broader and a narrower term. *Ribuy u'miut* yields whatever is similar to the narrow term, without requiring a formal *klal*/*prat* structure. The *Gemara* here, however, is clearly proceeding on the assumption that the applicable method is *klal u'prat*, since no one debates the law. The expansion and contraction achieved by *vekhissahu* and *be'afar* together are thus worked out within the framework of *klal u'prat* — with the exception that the standard rule does not apply because this is a *klal hatzarich liprat*, and instead we arrive at "anything similar to *afar*" as the operative criterion.

It is worth noting that *Tosafot* observes a subtle difference in formulation between the *baraita*'s criterion and that of the *Mishnah*. The *Mishnah* uses the language *davar shemegadel bo tzmachim* — something that grows plants — whereas the *baraita* specifies *zorin bo umatzmiach* — something that one plants in and that causes growth. These are not identical. Something like desert sand may support the spontaneous growth of plants, and in that sense falls under *megadel tzmachim*; but if one were to plant a seed in it, nothing would grow. The *baraita*'s formulation — *zorin bo umatzmiach* — thus excludes desert sand in a way the *Mishnah*'s language does not obviously do. This distinction becomes directly relevant when the *Gemara* turns to the case of one traveling in the desert, a question taken up shortly.

The conclusion of the derivation is: things that share the qualities of dust — granular form, and the capacity to nurture plant life — are included; things that lack those qualities are excluded. This accounts for the inclusion of ground stone, sawdust, and thin flax, and the exclusion of flour, metal, and thick or solid materials.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דָּרַשׁ רַב נַחְמָן בַּר רַב חִסְדָּא: אֵין מְכַסִּים אֶלָּא בְּדָבָר שֶׁזּוֹרְעִין בּוֹ וּמַצְמִיחַ. אָמַר רָבָא: הַאי בּוּרְכָא!
> **Translation:** § **Rav Naḥman bar Rav Ḥisda taught: One may not cover** the blood of an undomesticated animal or a bird **except with a substance in which** seed is **sown and sprouts. Rava said: This** is **an absurdity [*burkha*],** as the mishna and *baraita* both teach that one may use substances in which seeds do not sprout.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק לְרָבָא: מַאי בּוּרְכָתֵיהּ? אֲנָא אַמְרִיתַהּ נִיהֲלֵיהּ, וּמֵהָא מַתְנִיתָא אַמְרִיתַהּ נִיהֲלֵיהּ: הָיָה מְהַלֵּךְ בַּמִּדְבָּר, וְאֵין לוֹ אֵפֶר לְכַסּוֹת – שׁוֹחֵק דִּינַר זָהָב וּמְכַסֶּה. הָיָה מְהַלֵּךְ בִּסְפִינָה, וְאֵין לוֹ עָפָר לְכַסּוֹת – שׂוֹרֵף טַלִּיתוֹ וּמְכַסֶּה.
> **Translation:** **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said to Rava: What is the absurdity** of **his** statement? **I said this** statement to Rav Naḥman bar Rav Ḥisda **and I said it to him from this *baraita*:** If one **is traveling in the desert,** where the earth is not arable, and wishes to slaughter an undomesticated animal or a bird, **but he does not have dirt** with which **to cover** the blood, **he may grind a gold dinar** into powder **and cover** the blood with it. If one **is traveling on a ship** and wants to slaughter an undomesticated animal or a bird **but he does not have earth** with which **to cover** the blood, **he may burn his garment and cover** the blood with the ashes. It is evident from the first clause of the *baraita* that desert sand, which does not allow for the sprouting of seeds, may not be used to cover the blood of an undomesticated animal or a bird.

## Desert & Sea Cases



### Gold Coin Case


A series of extreme cases is now introduced, illustrating how one fulfills the obligation of *kissuy hadam* when conventional materials are unavailable.

The first case: *haya mehalek bamidbar ve'ein lo afar lechasot* — one was traveling in the desert and had no earth with which to cover. The implicit point, as clarified by *Tosafot* above, is that the sand of the desert does not qualify — one cannot plant a seed in desert sand and expect it to grow, so it fails the *megadel tzmachim* criterion. *Shochek dinar zahav u-mechassen* — one grinds a gold coin and uses the gold dust to cover the blood.

### Tallit Ashes

The second case: *haya mehalek bisafina ve'ein lo afar lechasot* — one was traveling by boat and had no earth. *Soref taliso u-mechassen* — one burns one's *tallit* and uses the ashes to cover.

### Ashes & Gold Dust

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא שׂוֹרֵף טַלִּיתוֹ וּמְכַסֶּה, אַשְׁכְּחַן אֵפֶר דְּאִיקְּרִי עָפָר, אֶלָּא דִּינַר זָהָב מְנָלַן? אָמַר רַבִּי זֵירָא: ״וְעַפְרוֹת זָהָב לוֹ״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara analyzes the *baraita*: **Granted,** it is understood that **one may burn his garment and cover** the blood with the ashes, since **we found** a source for the fact that **ashes are called earth [*afar*],** as the Gemara will soon prove; accordingly, the use of ashes is in accordance with the verse: “And cover it with earth.” **But** with regard to **a gold dinar, from where do we** derive that one may grind it into powder and use it to cover the blood? **Rabbi Zeira said:** The verse states: **“And it has dust of [*afrot*] gold”** (Job 28:6), indicating that gold is referred to as dust.

The *Gemara* immediately questions the basis for these rulings. *Bishlama soref taliso u-mechassen, ashkechan eifer d'ika* — the case of ashes is understandable, since we have a source demonstrating that ashes are called *afar* (dust). The verse concerning the purification waters of the red heifer uses the term *me'afar sereifat hachatat* — "from the dust of the burning of the purification-offering," calling what is in fact ash by the name *afar*. Similarly, *Avraham* in his supplication over *Sodom* declares *ve'anochi afar va'eifer* — "and I am dust and ashes" — where *afar* and *eifer* are used in parallel. Thus ashes are linguistically labeled as a type of *afar* and may be used for *kissuy hadam*.

But as for gold dust: *ela dinar zahav minalan* — how do we know gold dust is permitted? We established that ground metal is excluded. The answer comes from Rav Zeira: *ve'afrot zahav lo* — the verse speaks of "the dust of gold," using the word *afar* in connection with gold. Since the *Torah* itself labels gold dust as a form of *afar*, it falls within the category of things called dust and may be used, even though it would not otherwise qualify under the standard qualities of *afar*.

## Shammai v. Hillel



### Core Dispute

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: אֵין מְכַסִּין אֶלָּא בְּעָפָר, דִּבְרֵי בֵּית שַׁמַּאי. וּבֵית הִלֵּל אוֹמְרִים: מָצִינוּ אֵפֶר שֶׁקָּרוּי עָפָר, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ״וְלָקְחוּ לַטָּמֵא מֵעֲפַר שְׂרֵיפַת״. וּבֵית שַׁמַּאי: עֲפַר שְׂרֵיפָה אִיקְּרִי, עָפָר סְתָמָא לָא אִיקְּרִי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites a related *baraita* in which **the Sages taught: One may not cover** the blood of an undomesticated animal or a bird **except with earth;** this is **the statement of Beit Shammai. And Beit Hillel say: We find that ashes are called dust [*afar*], as it is stated** with regard to the red heifer: **“And for the impure they shall take from the ashes [*me’afar*] of the burning of the purification from sin”** (Numbers 19:17). **And Beit Shammai** respond: Ashes are **called dust of the burning [*afar sereifa*],** but **they are not called ordinary dust [*afar*].**

A *baraita* is now cited: *Tnu rabbanan: ein michasin ela be'afar, divrei Beit Shammai* — one may cover only with actual dust/earth — this is the position of *Beit Shammai*. They reject all the expanded materials we have been discussing. *U'Beit Hillel omrim: matzinu eifer shekarui afar* — *Beit Hillel* says we find that ashes are called *afar*, drawing on the verse about the red heifer (*me'afar sereifat hachatat*), and therefore ashes are also permitted.

*Beit Shammai* responds: *afar sereifah ikrai* — it is called "dust of burning," meaning the term *afar* there is qualified by *sereifah*, making it a specialized compound expression. *Afar stama lo* — it is not simply called *afar* without qualification. Therefore, only actual earth qualifies for *Beit Shammai*.

The debate thus yields two organizing categories for what is permissible. The first category consists of materials that share the *qualities* of *afar* — granular form, organic character, the capacity to support vegetation. *Rashi* emphasizes the granular aspect as defining what "covering" means; the *Mishnah* emphasizes the *megadel tzmachim* criterion. The second category consists of materials that are *labeled* as *afar* in *Torah* language, even if they do not share all of earth's properties — namely, ashes (which the verse calls *afar* by the red heifer) and gold dust (which the verse describes as *afrot zahav*). *Beit Shammai* accepts only actual earth. *Beit Hillel* accepts ashes by virtue of the linguistic labeling; and by extension, presumably, other things that have the qualities of *afar* or are labeled as such.

### Added Materials

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנָא: הוֹסִיפוּ עֲלֵיהֶן הַשְּׁחוֹר, וְהַכְּחוֹל, וְנִקְרַת פִּיסּוּלִין, וְיֵשׁ אוֹמְרִים: אַף הַזַּרְנִיךְ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes: It is **taught** that the Sages **added** the following **to the** list of substances in the mishna with which one may cover the blood: **Coal dust, stibium [*keḥol*], and shavings from chiseling. And some say** they included **even arsenic [*zarnikh*].**

The *Mishnah* or *baraita* then adds: *hosifu aleiha: hashachor vehakachol venikris pisulin* — they added to the list: *shachor* (charcoal dust — not charcoal itself, but the fine powdery residue of ground charcoal); *kachol* (a type of salve used for the eyes, evidently available in granular form); and *nikris pisulin*, which *Rashi* identifies as a type of dust that accumulates in a mill from the grinding process — though it is unclear whether this refers to the milled grain (flour being excluded) or to fine particles from the millstones themselves (ground stone having been included). The exact nature of these materials and how they fit neatly into either the *qualities-of-afar* or the *labeled-as-afar* categories is not fully transparent.

*Ve'yesh omrim: af hazarnich* — and some say even *zarnich* is permitted — which *Rashi* identifies as some kind of mineral or metallic substance, possibly antimony (used in cosmetics). This is puzzling, since the general rule excludes ground metal (*shchikat kelei mateches*). How a metallic substance enters the list under any standard is not clearly resolved, and the lines of distinction in the final details of this *halacha* remain somewhat opaque.

## Abraham's Reward



### Avraham's Statement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: בִּשְׂכַר שֶׁאָמַר אַבְרָהָם אָבִינוּ ״וְאָנֹכִי עָפָר וָאֵפֶר״, זָכוּ בָּנָיו לִשְׁתֵּי מִצְוֹת: אֵפֶר פָּרָה, וַעֲפַר סוֹטָה.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara cites aggadic accounts relating to the mitzva of covering the blood: **Rava says: As reward** for that **which our Patriarch Abraham said: “And I am but dust [*afar*] and ashes”** (Genesis 18:27), **his children merited two mitzvot: The ashes of the** red **heifer** (see Numbers, chapter 19) **and the dust of the *sota*,** i.e., dirt taken from the ground of the Tabernacle that is mixed into the water that examines whether or not a woman committed adultery (see Numbers 5:17).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִיחְשׁוֹב נָמֵי עֲפַר כִּסּוּי הַדָּם? הָתָם הֶכְשֵׁר מִצְוָה אִיכָּא, הֲנָאָה לֵיכָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **But let** Rava **also consider the earth** used in the mitzva of **covering the blood.** The Gemara responds: **There,** the earth **does** serve as an **accessory to the mitzva** of covering the blood, but **there is no benefit** imparted by it. It occurs after the animal has been slaughtered and does not itself render the meat fit for consumption. By contrast, the ashes of the red heifer and the dust of the *sota* provide benefit, as the former purifies one who became ritually impure and the latter leads to peace between husband and wife when drinking the water proves that she did not commit adultery.


The *perek* concludes with an *aggadic* passage that ties the discussion of dust and ashes to a broader theological reflection. *Amar Rava: bischar she'amar Avraham Avinu ve'anochi afar va'eifer* — Rava said: as a reward for *Avraham Avinu* having said "and I am dust and ashes" in his supplication before God over *Sodom and Amorah*, his descendants merited two *mitzvot*: *eifer parah* — the ashes of the red heifer, which purify those who are impure; *ve'afar sotah* — the earth of the *sotah* ritual, which is used in the water given to the woman suspected of infidelity.

The *mitzvah* of the red heifer involves ashes (*eifer*), which corresponds to *Avraham*'s use of *eifer*. The *sotah* ritual involves earth (*afar*) mixed into the water given to the suspected woman, which corresponds to *Avraham*'s use of *afar*.

### Why Not Kissuy

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רָבָא: בִּשְׂכַר שֶׁאָמַר אַבְרָהָם אָבִינוּ
> **Translation:** **And Rava says: As reward** for that **which our Patriarch Abraham said** to the king of Sodom:

The *Gemara* immediately asks: *velachashev nami afar kissuy hadam* — why not also count the earth used for *kissuy hadam*, which is the very subject of this entire *perek*? The answer: *hasam hechsher mitzvah ika, hana leka* — there, in the case of the red heifer and the *sotah*, there is a *mitzvah* that brings benefit. By the red heifer, ashes provide purification — a real tangible benefit. By the *sotah*, the earth mixed into the water resolves the matter: *oseh shalom bein ish le'ishto* — it makes peace between a husband and his wife, clearing the suspicion and allowing the couple to move forward. Even though performing the *sotah* process is not itself a personal merit, the outcome confers genuine benefit.

By contrast, *kissuy hadam* — while certainly a *mitzvah* — provides no such direct benefit. One can eat the meat perfectly well without covering the blood. The covering does not resolve anything, purify anything, or protect any relationship. It is an obligation whose fulfillment is its own reward, so to speak, but it offers no concrete benefit of the kind that would make it a fitting expression of divine reward for *Avraham*'s humility.

There is something beautifully paradoxical in this *Gemara*: one accumulates *mitzvah*-credit for performing *kissuy hadam*, but that very *mitzvah* is not listed among the rewards granted to *Avraham*'s descendants — precisely because it confers no benefit beyond the act itself. The rewards from *Avraham*'s humility are expressed through *mitzvot* that accomplish something concrete in the world. *Kissuy hadam*, though of the same linguistic fabric (*afar*, *eifer*), belongs to a different register of obligation.

With this, the *Gemara* concludes the *perek*. Tomorrow's *daf* opens the *aggadic* material that closes the *masechta*.
