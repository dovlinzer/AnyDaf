# Menachot 108 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Six Shofrot Sources

### Six Shofrot Setup

The daf opens in the middle of 108a, at the passage beginning *v'Shmuel amar*. The broader context is a discussion of why there were six extra *shofrot* — collection boxes — in the *Beit Hamikdash*, making a total of thirteen. Money was deposited into these boxes and directed toward the *olat keitz hamizbeach*, the communal *olah* brought on the *mizbeach* during periods when it was not otherwise occupied. This arrangement made sense because much of that money was effectively redirected from other *korbanot* that could not be brought — particularly certain *chataot* and *ashamot* — along with other categories of sanctified funds whose purpose had lapsed.

The question driving the discussion is: why specifically six? One earlier answer, associated with Bar Padda, was that the six correspond to the different types of animals one might have originally designated — a cow, a calf, a ram, a sheep, and so on — and that relates to the question of whether people donating money for an *olah* are bound, according to *Rebbi*, to use the funds for the exact species they originally specified. But now the *Gemara* turns to additional answers that approach the question from a different angle: not the variety of animals, but the variety of *halachic* categories from which such leftover money might originate.

### Shmuel: Six Categories

*Shmuel* offers his answer: the six *shofrot* correspond to six categories of leftover sanctified funds. The passage reads:

> *V'Shmuel amar: keneged motar chataot u'motar asham u'motar asham nazir u'motar asham metzora u'motar minchat chotei u'motar asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol.*

Shmuel identifies the following six sources. First, *motar chataot* — leftover money designated for a *chatat* where not all of the funds were ultimately used. The *chatat* animal itself, if it was a private *chatat* that went unused, could not be redirected and would have to be left to die; but money that had been consecrated for a *chatat* and was not fully expended does follow the rule that leftover *chatat* funds go toward a communal *olah*, specifically the *olat keitz hamizbeach*. Second, *motar asham* — leftover money for a standard *asham*, such as an *asham gezeilot* (for stealing and falsely denying it under oath) or an *asham* for *me'ilah* (misuse of *hekdesh* property).

Shmuel then distinguishes between three types of *ashamot*. The *asham nazir* and the *asham metzora* are each treated as separate categories. The *asham metzora* is brought as part of the purification process of the *metzora*, with the blood applied to the ear, thumb, and toe of the person being purified — it is intrinsically tied to a transformative status change. The *asham nazir* similarly accompanies the re-commencement of *nezirut b'taharah* after impurity, making it likewise connected to a change of personal status rather than a straightforward sin-offering. Both of these are distinct enough from the classic *asham* to warrant separate categories.

That gives four categories so far: one *chatat* and three types of *ashamot*. The fifth is *motar minchat chotei* — leftover money designated for a *minchat chotei*, the flour-offering brought by a sinner who cannot afford an animal *chatat*. This is a category of *korban olah veyored*, the graduated sin-offering described at the end of *Parashat Vayikra*, where a person of insufficient means may bring a *minchah* instead of an animal. It functions like the *motar chatat* category except that the offering is a *minchah* rather than an animal. Sixth and finally, *motar asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol* — the leftover money from the daily *minchat chavitin*, the tenth of an *eifah* brought each day by the *Kohen Gadol*. A verse in *Yechezkel* calls this offering a *chatat*, and on that basis Shmuel assumes that any leftover money from it follows the same rule as a standard *chatat*: the surplus goes toward the *olat keitz hamizbeach*. That yields exactly six categories.

### Rabbi Oshaya Alternative

Rabbi Oshaya presents an alternative list:

> *Keneged motar chatat u'motar asham u'motar asham nazir u'motar asham metzora, u'motar kinnim u'motar minchat chotei.*

Rabbi Oshaya agrees with Shmuel's first four categories — the standard *chatat*, and the three varieties of *ashamot* — and he also agrees that leftover *minchat chotei* money belongs on the list, though he places it last. The point of departure is the sixth item. Where Shmuel listed the *minchat chavitin* of the *Kohen Gadol*, Rabbi Oshaya replaces it with *motar kinnim* — leftover money from bird sacrifices. Bird sacrifices function as a type of *chatat* in multiple contexts; for instance, a *yoledet* (a woman after childbirth) bringing offerings for her purification would include bird sacrifices. Rabbi Oshaya regards the *kinnim* as the more natural sixth category, and, as we shall see, rejects the *minchat chavitin* of the *Kohen Gadol* on principled grounds.

### Competing Lists Explained

The *Gemara* asks: why did Shmuel not say like Rabbi Oshaya and include *kinnim*? The answer is that *kinnim* were already explicitly mentioned among the first seven *shofrot* listed by the *Mishnah* at the outset of the passage describing the thirteen boxes. Since one of those original seven was dedicated specifically for bird sacrifices deposited *l'chatchila* — women who did not want to wait in line would deposit their coins, and the *kohanim* would bring the appropriate bird sacrifices on their behalf — it might seem that there is no need for a separate box among the final six for *kinnim*. But the *Gemara* challenges this reasoning: did Rabbi Oshaya not also have *kinnim* in his version of the *Mishnah*? And the answer is that he did — *chad l'kinnim v'chad l'motar kinnim* — one *shofar* for the *kinnim* designated from the outset for that purpose, and a separate *shofar* for *motar kinnim*, leftover *kinnim* money being redirected to the *olah*. Since the two purposes are entirely different, they obviously require distinct boxes, and so the *motar kinnim* needs its own *shofar* among the six.

The *Gemara* then asks the reverse question: why did Rabbi Oshaya not include the *minchat chavitin* of the *Kohen Gadol* as Shmuel did? The answer is that Rabbi Oshaya holds like the position that *motar asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol yirakeiv* — the leftover money of the *Kohen Gadol*'s daily *minchah* must be left to rot. On the one hand, it is called a *chatat* in *Yechezkel*, so the leftover money cannot be redirected for another *chatat*; on the other hand, Rabbi Oshaya does not regard it as a genuine *chatat* in the halachic sense, and therefore it does not enjoy the rule that leftover *chatat* money goes toward an *olah*. Caught between these two considerations, the money can only be left to rot, and thus it cannot constitute one of the six sources feeding the *olat keitz hamizbeach*.

## Kohen Gadol Mincha Debate

### Minchat Chavitin Not Cha…

A *baraita* is cited: *mosar minchas nedavah, mosar minchah yirakeiv* — leftover money of a *minchat nedavah*, the leftover *minchah*, shall rot. This formulation is, on its face, nearly incomprehensible. The *Gemara* asks *mai kamar* — what is it saying? Rav Chisda explains: *mosar minchas chotei nedavah nedavah* — if you have leftover money of a *minchat chotei*, it goes for a *nedavah*. This is consistent with what both Shmuel and Rabbi Oshaya had said: leftover money of a *minchat chotei* goes for the *nedavat tzibbur*, meaning the communal *olos keitz hamizbeach*. That is what *nedavah* means here. The second clause — that leftover money of the other *minchah* must rot — refers to the *minchat chavitin*, the *Kohen Gadol*'s daily tenth of an *eifah*. It is called a *chatat* in *Yechezkel*, but because it is not a genuine *chatat* in the normative sense, its leftover money cannot be redirected toward an *olah* — and so it must be left to rot.

### Yechezkel Source Analysis

At this point the *Gemara* introduces the view of *Rabbah*, who explicitly realigns himself with Shmuel: *afilu mosar asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol nami nedavah* — even the leftover money of the *Kohen Gadol*'s *minchat chavitin* goes for a *nedavah*. *Rabbah* returns to Shmuel's position that this offering, having been called a *chatat* in *Yechezkel*, follows the standard *chatat* rule and its surplus goes to the *olat keitz hamizbeach*. If something must be left to rot in the *baraita*, *Rabbah* reads that as a reference to something else entirely: *motar lachmei toda yirakeiv* — leftover money from the bread accompanying a *todah* offering must be left to rot. The bread of a *todah* has no independent sacrificial status; it exists solely to accompany the *todah* itself, so any leftover money for it cannot be transferred to any independent *korban* and must simply be discarded.

The upshot is that we now have an established dispute about what is done with the leftover money of the *Kohen Gadol*'s *minchat chavitin*. This is characterized by the *Gemara* as *beplugta* — a recognized controversy.

### Yochanan vs Elazar

The *Gemara* frames the dispute explicitly: *mah na'aseh b'mosar asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol* — what is done with the leftover money of the *Kohen Gadol*'s daily *minchah*? Rabbi Yochanan says *nedavah* — it goes toward the communal *olah*, consistent with Shmuel and *Rabbah*. Rabbi Elazar says *yirakeiv* — it must be left to rot, consistent with Rabbi Oshaya's position.

### Shekalim Mishna Proof

A challenge is raised from a *Mishnah* in Shekalim: *motar shekalim chulin, u'motar asiris ha'eifah, u'motar kinei zavim v'kinei zavot v'kinei yoledot, v'chataot v'ashamot — motreihem nedavah.* The *Mishnah* states that leftover *shekalim* may go to *chulin* (non-sanctified purposes), and then lists a series of offerings whose surpluses all go to *nedavah*: the *asiris ha'eifah*, all categories of bird sacrifices (for a *zav*, a *zavah*, and a *yoledet*), *chataot*, and *ashamot*. This appears to be a straightforward proof for Rabbi Yochanan and Shmuel, since the *asiris ha'eifah* is listed among those whose leftovers go to *nedavah*.

It is worth noting that this *Mishnah* is actually a hybrid of the two lists — it includes both the *asiris ha'eifah* (which Shmuel featured) and the bird sacrifices (which Rabbi Oshaya featured), thus mentioning precisely the items over which Shmuel and Rabbi Oshaya were in disagreement regarding the six *shofrot*. In practice, the genuine *halachic* dispute was not about the bird sacrifice money — everyone agreed that went toward the *olat keitz hamizbeach* — but about the *asiris ha'eifah*, which is explicitly included here. This *Mishnah*, then, would seem to clearly favor the position that *motar asiris ha'eifah* goes for *nedavah*.

### Shekalim Source Resoluti…

The *Gemara* deflects the proof. *Mai lav motar asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol* — does this not refer to the *Kohen Gadol*'s tenth of an *eifah*? The reply is: *lo, motar minchat chotei* — no, it refers to the *minchat chotei*. Although the standard term *asiris ha'eifah* typically denotes the *Kohen Gadol*'s daily offering, since a standard *minchah* is also a tenth of an *eifah*, the *Mishnah* can be read as referring to the *minchat chotei* instead. On this reading, the *Mishnah* in Shekalim maps precisely onto Rabbi Oshaya's list of six: the *chatat*, the three types of *ashamot*, the bird sacrifices, and the *minchat chotei* — and the reference to *asiris ha'eifah* means the *minchat chotei*, not the *Kohen Gadol*'s offering. Shmuel, of course, would read the *Mishnah* straightforwardly as referring to the *Kohen Gadol*'s *minchat chavitin*. The *Mishnah* from Shekalim is thus not a clear resolution but remains susceptible to either reading.

### Rav Nachman Position

*Amar Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak: mistavera k'man d'amar mosar asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol yirakeiv* — it stands to reason like the position that the leftover money of the *Kohen Gadol*'s *minchat chavitin* must rot. Rav Nachman rules in accordance with Rabbi Elazar, the position that the surplus is left to rot.

### Minchat Chavitin Analysis

To support this ruling, a *baraita* is cited: *lo yasim aleha shemen v'lo yiten aleha levonah ki chatas hi* — this is the verse discussing the *minchat chotei*, instructing that neither oil nor frankincense be placed on it, because it is a *chatat*. Rabbi Yehuda derives from this: *hi k'ruyah chatas v'ein acheres k'ruyah chatas* — it is the *minchah* that is called a *chatat*, and no other one. The implication is that even though *Yechezkel* calls the *Kohen Gadol*'s *minchat chavitin* a *chatat*, the verse in the *Torah* is telling us that only the *minchat chotei* bears the designation of *chatat* in the full halachic sense.

From this the *baraita* draws a further inference: *limed al asiris ha'eifah shel Kohen Gadol she'einah k'ruyah chatas* — the verse teaches that the *Kohen Gadol*'s daily *minchah* is not truly called a *chatat*. And since a genuine *chatat* may not have frankincense placed on it (*lo yasim aleha levonah*), and the *Kohen Gadol*'s *minchah* is not a genuine *chatat*, it follows that *levonah* is required for it — *v'te'unah levonah*. And crucially: *u'mide'einah k'ruyah chatas mosarah yerakev* — because it is not genuinely called a *chatat*, its leftover money must rot.

This is the analytical key to the entire debate. The *minchat chavitin* of the *Kohen Gadol* occupies a peculiar intermediate status. The verse in *Yechezkel* calling it a *chatat* is sufficient to prevent the leftover money from being used for another *minchah* or *chatat*; that much carries over from the label. But it is not a genuine *chatat* in the *Torah*'s own terms, and therefore it does not benefit from the rule that leftover *chatat* money is redirected toward an *olah*. Trapped between these two positions, the money has nowhere to go and must simply be left to rot.

The one who holds that the leftover money goes to *nedavah* — Rabbi Yochanan and Shmuel — takes the view that since *Yechezkel* calls it a *chatat*, the standard *chatat* rule applies and the surplus funds the *olat keitz hamizbeach*. The one who holds it must rot — Rabbi Elazar, Rabbi Oshaya, and Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak — reasons that since it is only nominally called a *chatat* and is not a genuine one, it neither qualifies for the *chatat*-to-*olah* rule nor can it be repurposed for anything else. Rav Nachman rules that this latter reasoning is the more persuasive one.

## Blemished Animal Transfer

### One Ox to Two Oxen

With the discussion of leftover *minchah* funds concluded, the *Gemara* turns to a new *Mishnah* that continues the theme of what happens when sanctity must be transferred. The *Mishnah* opens with the case of someone who declares, "*shor zeh olah*" — "this ox is an *olah*" — thereby sanctifying the ox itself rather than undertaking a personal obligation. The distinction is critical: when one takes a personal vow (*neder*), the question is whether the ultimate offering satisfies the terms of what was vowed. Here, however, no personal obligation has been incurred; the ox itself has been endowed with sanctity. When the ox then sustains a blemish (*nistaiv*) and can no longer be offered, it must be redeemed and the funds used for a valid *korban*. The *Mishnah* rules: *im ratzah yavi bedamav shenayim* — if he wishes, he may use the redemption money to bring two animals as *olot*. The sanctity has simply been transferred; there is no designated criterion that must be satisfied, and so bringing two animals is entirely permissible.

### Two Oxen to One Ox

Conversely, if one declared "*shenei shvarim eilu olah*" — "these two oxen are an *olah*" — and both were blemished, the *Mishnah* rules that *ratzah yavi bidmeihem echad* — he may bring one large ox with the combined redemption funds. Again, the principle is simply transferring the sanctity to a valid *olah*; there is no independent requirement regarding number. *Rebbi*, however, forbids: *Rebbi oser*.

### Ram-Sheep Species Switch

The *Mishnah* continues with cases of species switching. If one declared "*ayil zeh olah*" — "this ram is an *olah*" — and it was blemished, *im ratzah yavi bedamav keves* — he may bring a sheep in its place, even though a sheep is a smaller animal. And similarly, if one declared "*keves zeh olah*" — "this sheep is an *olah*" — and it was blemished, he may bring a ram in its place, which is the larger animal. Again, *Rebbi oser* — Rebbi forbids.

### Rabbi Forbids All Changes

It is notable that *Rebbi*'s objection is appended at the conclusion of all these cases. The question is whether Rebbi is forbidding only the last case — going from a sheep to a ram, or a ram to a sheep — or whether his objection extends to all the cases in the *Mishnah*, including the transition from one ox to two and from two oxen to one.

### Gemara: Scope of Rabbi's…

The *Gemara* opens 108b and asks: *v'amarta resha shor be-maneh v'hevi shenayim be-maneh lo yatza* — but have we not established elsewhere that if someone vows to bring a single ox worth a *maneh* and brings two animals together worth a *maneh*, he has not fulfilled his obligation? Why, then, does the *Mishnah* here allow starting with one and bringing two? The *Gemara* answers immediately: *shor zeh u'nistaiv shani* — the case where one sanctifies the animal itself and it becomes blemished is different. When there is no personal vow, no obligation was assumed that needs to be fulfilled in any specific way; the sanctity merely needs a valid destination, and there is no restriction on how many animals serve as that destination.

The *Gemara* then addresses Rebbi's objection. *Mah ta'ama* — why does Rebbi forbid? The answer given is: *mishum de-havé lei gadol ve-hevei katan* — because starting with two animals and bringing one is considered going from greater to lesser. Even though the single animal may be worth more than either of the two individually, two is always regarded as a greater or more significant quantity than one. This parallels the rule for personal vows, where Rebbi does not permit one to bring a lesser offering than what was vowed. *Ve'af al gav de-nistaiv l'chatchila lo shari Rebbi* — even though the animal was blemished and the transfer is technically unavoidable, Rebbi does not permit the downgrade even under those circumstances.

The *Gemara* then presses further: *v'liflog nami be-resha* — then why does Rebbi not also argue in the first case, where one sanctified animal produces two? If Rebbi's concern is patterned after the rules of a personal vow, and Rebbi's position on personal vows is that you may not change in either direction — not from big to small, nor from small to big — then he ought to forbid going from one to two animals, not only from two to one. The *Gemara* answers: *Rebbi a'kula milta palig* — Rebbi does indeed object to all the cases. *V'natru rabbanan ad demaseik miltei v'hadar mifalg aleihu* — he let the Rabbis finish stating all their permissive rulings and then registered his blanket disagreement at the end. Thus when the *Mishnah* says *Rebbi oser*, it means he forbids all cases: one to two, two to one, and all species switches alike.

### Rabbi's Vow Pattern Logic

*Teida* — a proof that this is correct. The final case of the *Mishnah* reads: a ram is sanctified as an *olah*, it gets blemished, one may bring a sheep; a sheep is sanctified as an *olah*, it gets blemished, one may bring a ram. *Rebbi oser* — Rebbi forbids. Now the case of going from a sheep to a ram is manifestly a case of going from small to big. If Rebbi's only concern were going from big to small, he would have no objection here. Yet he forbids it. *Shma mina* — we may conclude from this that Rebbi objects to all changes without exception, in either direction, whether bigger or smaller, whether more animals or fewer. His reasoning is that when one sanctifies an animal, the transfer of its sanctity upon blemishing should be governed by the same standards as a personal vow — and by a personal vow, Rebbi does not permit going from small to big any more than from big to small. The same principle is applied here *l'chatchila*, even though the blemish was not the person's fault.

A question is then raised: *Ibayi l'hu — mimina l'mina mai?* — what is the rule when the switch is not within the same species (between small and large examples of the same type) but across entirely different species? If one sanctified a cow, may one transfer to sheep?

*Ta shema* — the *Gemara* cites a source. *Shor zeh olah v'nistav, lo yavi b'damav ayil* — if one sanctifies an ox as an *olah* and it becomes blemished, one should not bring a ram. But then: *aval mavia b'damav shenei eilim* — one may, however, bring two rams. This introduces a new consideration for the Rabbis: not only must one avoid reducing the number of sacrifices, but one must not shortchange the *Beit Hamikdash* in terms of monetary value. You may switch species, as the *baraita* permits going from an ox to rams, but you must ensure the final offering(s) are of equivalent value. The reason one brings two rams rather than one is precisely that a single ram would likely be worth less than the ox. *V'Rebbi oser — lefi she'ein bila* — and Rebbi forbids, because of the problem of *bila* — that you cannot mix the two *minchos* (the accompanying flour-offerings) that come with two animals. This seems to suggest that Rebbi's objection in this case is the *bila* problem, not his general transfer principle.

## Species Switch & Value

### Braita: Species OK, Valu…

The *baraita* under discussion rules that switching from an ox to rams is permitted in principle, but one may not shortchange the *Beit Hamikdash* by bringing an offering of lesser value. The example given is: *shor zeh olah v'nistav, lo yavi b'damav ayil, aval mavia b'damav shenei eilim* — one may not bring a single ram in place of an ox, but one may bring two rams. Rashi explains the underlying logic: transferring sanctity to a different species is acceptable because people will understand that the *kedushah* has simply been transferred. The prohibition is only on giving the *Beit Hamikdash* less value than what was originally consecrated.

### Gemara: Why Two Rams?

The *Gemara* asks: *i hachi, mai irya trei? afilu chad nami* — if the Rabbis do not care about going from big to small when an animal is blemished, why must one bring two rams? Even one should suffice, since the Rabbis hold that the blemished-animal transfer imposes no constraint on whether the replacement is larger or smaller. *D'ha b'nistav l'rabbanan lo shana lei bein gadol l'katan* — when it comes to a blemished animal, the Rabbis draw no distinction between big and small.

### Two Tannaitic Views

The *Gemara* resolves this with the observation: *trei tana'ei v'aliba d'rabbanan* — there are two *tannaim* who interpret the Rabbis' position differently. The *Mishnah* we studied reflects one *tanna*'s understanding of the Rabbinic view, according to which the Rabbis are completely indifferent to whether the replacement offering is greater or lesser in value. This *baraita* reflects a different *tanna*'s reading of the Rabbis, according to which — while the Rabbis do not follow Rebbi's strict parallel to personal vow rules — they nonetheless require that the *Beit Hamikdash* not be shortchanged in terms of value. These are thus two distinct *tannaitic* presentations of the Rabbinic position, and both must be acknowledged.

## Bila Problem & One to Two

### Bila: Two Minchas Unmixed

The *Gemara* now returns to Rebbi's position in the *baraita*, where he forbids bringing two rams in place of one ox *lefi she'ein bila* — because there is no *bila*, no mixing. When one brings two animals, each animal requires its own accompanying *minchas nesachim*, and these two *menachos* cannot be combined into a single mixed offering. The result is that rather than one *olah* with one *minchah*, one is now bringing two distinct *olos* with two distinct *menachos* — an arrangement that is conspicuously different from what was originally sanctified, in a way that goes beyond a simple transfer of *kedushah*.

### Gemara Challenges Bila

The *Gemara* immediately challenges this: *ta'ama d'ein bila* — meaning if *bila* were possible, Rebbi would permit it? *Ha yesh bila, shari* — so if you could mix the two *menachos* together, bringing two animals would be fine for Rebbi? But this seems inconsistent with what we established from the *Mishnah*. The *Mishnah* itself taught: *ayil ze olah v'nistav ratza yavi b'damav keves; keves ze olah v'nistav yavi b'damav ayil, v'Rebbi oser* — Rebbi forbids even going from ram to sheep and sheep to ram, which involves only one animal each time and no *bila* problem whatsoever. Rebbi's objection there cannot possibly be about *bila*. His fundamental concern, as established above, is that any change in type, number, or species is forbidden *l'chatchila* because it violates the structural parallel to a personal vow. The *bila* argument appears to be an entirely new and different rationale — so how can Rebbi's prohibition here be exclusively attributed to *bila*?

### Two Views on Bila Concern

The *Gemara* answers: *trei tana'ei v'aliba d'Rebbi* — there are two *tanna'im* who interpret Rebbi's position differently. Our *Mishnah* presents one *tanna*'s understanding: Rebbi's objection to transfers is absolute, grounded in the principle that any change — one to two, two to one, or any species switch — violates the parallel to personal vow rules. The *baraita* presents a different *tanna*'s understanding of Rebbi: according to that *tanna*, Rebbi only objects when going from one animal to two, and his reason is specifically the *bila* problem — the impossibility of combining the two *menachos*. On this reading, Rebbi would not object to going from two to one or to species switches, only to the one-to-two transition, and only because of *bila*.

### Vows & Blemish Parallel

The *Gemara* closes the discussion with the *baraita*'s final clause: *uv'tehorim — egel v'heivi par, keves v'heivi ayil, yatza* — when dealing with unblemished animals, meaning a personal vow, if one vowed to bring a calf and brought an ox, or vowed to bring a sheep and brought a ram, one has fulfilled the obligation. *Stam k'rabbanan* — this final clause follows the Rabbinic position, since Rebbi holds that even in the case of a personal vow one does not fulfill the obligation by bringing a larger animal than what was vowed. According to the Rabbis, however, going from small to large satisfies the vow. This brings the full discussion to a close.

To summarize the *sugyah*: when an animal is blemished after having been sanctified directly (rather than through a personal vow), the Rabbis permit a wide range of transfers — crossing species, changing number, altering size — subject only, according to one *tannaitic* reading of the Rabbinic view, to the requirement that the *Beit Hamikdash* not be shortchanged in value. According to Rebbi, two *tannaitic* traditions transmit his view: one holds that any change whatsoever is forbidden *l'chatchila* because the transfer is patterned after personal vow standards, and another holds that his sole concern is the *bila* problem when transitioning from one animal to two.
