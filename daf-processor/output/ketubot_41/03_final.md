## Goring Ox Damages



### Tam vs. Muad

The daf begins at the top of 41b, wrapping up a *sugya* that has been unfolding since the previous session. The central question is whether the half-payment (*chatzi nezek*) imposed when an unhabituated ox (*tam*) gores is classified as a *knas* — a monetary fine — or as *mammon*, an ordinary compensatory obligation. The practical difference is significant: a *knas* cannot be collected based on the tortfeasor's own admission (*al pi atzmo*), whereas a *mammon* obligation can. The debate among the *Amoraim* turns on a conceptual question: are oxen presumed to be naturally guarded and non-dangerous, such that when a *tam* gores it falls shy of actionable negligence and the half-payment is therefore a punitive *knas*? Or is a goring ox just barely over the threshold of negligence, so that the half-payment represents partial compensation — *tashlumim* — rather than a fine?

[DAF:41b]
### Muad Direct Proof

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: ״הֵמִית שׁוֹרִי אֶת פְּלוֹנִי״, אוֹ ״שׁוֹרוֹ שֶׁל פְּלוֹנִי״, הֲרֵי זֶה מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ. מַאי לָאו, בְּתָם! לָא, בְּמוּעָד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Come** and **hear** an additional proof from the mishna. One who said: **My ox killed so-and-so, or:** My ox killed **an ox** belonging to **so-and-so, this** owner **pays** based **on his own** admission. **What, is** this **not** referring **to an innocuous** ox, for which he pays half the damage, proving that it is a payment of money and not a fine? The Gemara rejects the proof: **No,** the *tanna* is referring **to a forewarned** animal.

The Gemara opens with a *ta shma* — a proof from a *braita*. The *braita* states: if someone says, "My ox gored another person," or "my ox gored another person's ox," he pays based on his own admission. The Gemara asks: does this not refer to a *tam*, thereby demonstrating that the half-payment is *mammon*, since admission suffices? The Gemara rejects this: *lo bemuad* — we are speaking of a *muad*, a habituated ox. And the implication for a *tam* would be the reverse: *eino meshaleim al pi atzmo* — he does not pay based on his own admission — precisely because the half-payment of a *tam* is a *knas*.

### Braita Slave Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲבָל בְּתָם מַאי, אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ? אַדְּתָנֵי סֵיפָא: עַבְדּוֹ שֶׁל פְּלוֹנִי אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ, נִיפְלוֹג וְנִיתְנֵי בְּדִידַהּ: בַּמֶּה דְּבָרִים אֲמוּרִים — בְּמוּעָד, אֲבָל תָּם — אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ! כּוּלַּהּ בְּמוּעָד קָמַיְירֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **However,** in the case **of an innocuous** ox, **what** is the *halakha*? If it is that **he does not pay** based **on his own** admission, then, **rather than teaching the latter clause** of the mishna: One whose ox **killed a slave** belonging to **so-and-so does not pay** based **on his own** admission, **let him distinguish and teach** the distinction **within the case itself: In what** case **is this statement said?** It is **with regard to a forewarned** ox; **however,** the owner of **an innocuous** ox **does not pay** based **on his own** admission. The Gemara rejects this proof: The **entire** mishna **is speaking of a forewarned** ox, and does not address the *halakha* of an innocuous ox at all. Therefore, no proof can be cited with regard to the nature of half the payment.

The Gemara then raises a further question on the *braita*. The *seifa* of the *braita* addresses a different case: if one's ox kills another's slave, he does not pay based on his own admission, since the thirty-shekel payment for a slave is a fixed amount — and as a fixed sum that can exceed actual damages, it is treated as a *knas*. The Gemara asks: why did the *braita* need to introduce the slave case to illustrate the *knas* exemption? It should have made the distinction within the original case itself, contrasting *muad* (where one pays *al pi atzmo*) with *tam* (where one does not). The very fact that the *braita* jumped to the slave case seems to imply that within the goring case itself — whether *muad* or *tam* — one always pays based on admission, indicating the payment is *mammon*. The Gemara answers: *kulah bemuad kamari* — the *braita* is speaking throughout of a *muad*, distinguishing between goring a free person or ox (which is *tashlumim*) and goring a slave (which is *knas*). The case of a *tam* simply was not the *braita*'s focus, and its silence tells us nothing about whether *tam* is *knas* or *mammon*.

### Exceeding Damages

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע, זֶה הַכְּלָל: כׇּל הַמְשַׁלֵּם יָתֵר עַל מַה שֶּׁהִזִּיק — אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ. הָא פָּחוֹת מִמַּה שֶּׁהִזִּיק — מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ. לָא תֵּימָא הָא פָּחוֹת מִמַּה שֶּׁהִזִּיק, אֶלָּא אֵימָא: הָא כְּמָה שֶׁהִזִּיק — מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Come** and **hear** an additional proof from the mishna: **This is the principle: Anyone who pays more than what he damaged,** the payments are fines, and therefore **he does not pay** based **on his own** admission. The Gemara infers: If he pays **less than what he damaged, he pays** based **on his own** admission. Apparently, payment of half the damage is a payment of money, not a fine. The Gemara rejects this proof: **Do not** infer and **say:** If he pays **less than what he damaged,** he pays based on his own admission. Infer and **say:** If he pays precisely **what he damaged, he pays** based **on his own** admission.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲבָל פָּחוֹת מַאי, אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ? לִיתְנֵי, זֶה הַכְּלָל: כׇּל שֶׁאֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם כְּמָה שֶׁהִזִּיק — אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ, דְּמַשְׁמַע פָּחוֹת וּמַשְׁמַע יָתֵר! תְּיוּבְתָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** according to that explanation, if he pays **less** than the damage he caused, **what** is the *halakha*? If it is that **he does not pay** based **on his own** admission, **let** the *tanna* **teach** a more general principle: **This is the principle: Anyone who does not pay** the amount **that he damaged does not pay** based **on his own** admission, **as** that formulation both **indicates** one who pays **less and indicates** one who pays **more** than the damage he inflicted. The Gemara concludes: This is **a conclusive refutation** of the opinion of Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua, that payment of half the damage is a fine.

The Gemara next turns to a *mishna* that formulates a general rule: *kol ha-meshaleim yoter al mah she-hizik eino meshaleim al pi atzmo* — anyone who pays more than the actual damages does not pay based on his own admission. Payment exceeding actual harm — whether because the amount is disproportionate (such as double payment for theft) or because it is a fixed sum like the thirty shekels for a slave — marks the payment as a *knas*. The implication seems to run in the other direction as well: if the payment is *less* than full damages, it would apparently be *tashlumim*, collected even based on one's own admission. This seems to directly support the view that half-payment of a *tam* is *mammon*.

### Less Than Damages

The Gemara presses this: the *mishna* says if one pays more than damages, he does not pay *al pi atzmo*; by contrast, if one pays less than damages, the implication is that one does pay *al pi atzmo*. This strongly suggests that partial payment is *tashlumim*, not *knas*.

### Contradiction Resolved

The Gemara, however, refuses to accept this reading of the *mishna* as definitive. It reformulates: do not read the rule as drawing the line between "more" and "less." Rather, the line is between paying *exactly* the amount of damages (which is *tashlumim*) versus anything that diverges — whether more or less — from that precise equivalence (which is *knas*). If so, why didn't the *mishna* simply say: "Whoever does not pay exactly according to the damages does not pay *al pi atzmo*"? The Gemara concedes: *tiyuvta* — this is indeed a challenge to the position that half-damages is a *knas*. The *mishna* strongly implies that only payment exceeding damages is a *knas*, and that less-than-full payment is *tashlumim*.

Yet the Gemara then delivers a striking ruling: *ve-hilkheta palga nizka knasa* — the law is that the half-payment of a goring *tam* is indeed a *knas*. The Gemara itself acknowledges the apparent paradox: you have just presented a *tiyuvta*, and yet you rule in accordance with the contradicted position? The answer: yes — *tiyuvta ve-hilkheta*. The reason the *mishna* did not formulate the rule as "anything other than exact damages is a *knas*" is that such a formulation would have been imprecise. There exists a case of half-payment that is unambiguously *mammon*, not *knas*, and that exception prevented the *mishna* from adopting the broader formulation.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהִלְכְתָא: פַּלְגָא נִיזְקָא קְנָסָא. תְּיוּבְתָּא וְהִלְכְתָא?! אִין: טַעְמָא מַאי אִיתּוֹתַב — מִשּׁוּם דְּלָא קָתָנֵי ״כְּמָה שֶׁהִזִּיק״, לָא פְּסִיקָא לֵיהּ. כֵּיוָן דְּאִיכָּא חֲצִי נֶזֶק צְרוֹרוֹת, דְּהִלְכְתָא גְּמִירִי לַהּ דְּמָמוֹנָא הוּא, מִשּׁוּם הָכִי לָא קָתָנֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara further concludes: **And the *halakha*** is that payment of **half the damage** is **a fine.** The Gemara asks: Is there **a conclusive refutation** of the opinion of Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua, **and** the ***halakha*** is in accordance with that opinion? The Gemara responds: **Yes,** the *halakha* is in accordance with his opinion, as, **what is the reason** that his opinion **was conclusively refuted?** It is **because** the *tanna* **does not teach:** This is the principle: Anyone who does not pay the amount **that he damaged.** However, the reason the *tanna* did not employ that formulation **is not clear-cut for him, since there is** the payment of **half** the **damage** caused by **pebbles** dispersed by an animal proceeding in its usual manner. **As it is a *halakha* transmitted** to Moses from Sinai **that** the payment for pebbles **is a monetary** payment, not a fine; it is **due to that** fact that the *tanna* **did not teach** the principle: Anyone who does not pay the amount that he damaged does not pay based on his own admission. In the case of pebbles, although he does not pay the amount that he damaged, he pays based on his own admission.

### Pebbles Exception


That exception is the case of *tzurot* — pebbles. When a walking animal kicks up stones that cause damage, the halacha — a *halakha le-Moshe mi-Sinai* — is that one pays half-damages. This is categorized under *regel* (trampling), and the half-payment in this case is unambiguously *mammon*, not *knas*. The *mishna* therefore could not state as an absolute rule that any sub-full payment is a *knas*, because the *tzurot* case is a counterexample: it pays half the damages and is nonetheless *mammon*. Accordingly, the *mishna* confined its stated rule to the case of payment exceeding damages, without claiming that less-than-full payment is always *tashlumim*. The real underlying principle — that anything departing from exact equivalence to actual damages is a *knas*, apart from the *tzurot* exception — remains in force. And within that framework, the half-payment of a goring *tam* is a *knas*.

### Ruling: Knas

The conclusion of this *sugya* is thus somewhat counterintuitive. All the evidence marshaled over the course of the discussion — the *mishna*'s formulation, the logical argument that paying half of something suggests partial compensation rather than a zero-based fine, and the parallel of *tzurot* where half-payment is plainly *mammon* — points in the direction of treating *chatzi nezek* as *tashlumim*. Nevertheless, the Gemara concludes with a firm ruling: *palga nizka knasa*. The tradition is that a *tam*, by its nature, does not rise to the level of actionable negligence — one cannot be truly blamed for failing to prevent an ox that had never gored before from goring — and therefore the payment imposed is punitive rather than compensatory.

It is worth pausing to appreciate what the Gemara is asserting here. The logical inference from paying half of something is that it represents partial payment toward a full debt — that is precisely the logic of the *tzurot* case. And yet the Gemara overrides that logic, seemingly on the strength of tradition alone. The Gemara had a received *halacha* that this was a *knas*, and it therefore determined that even where payment is less than full damages, it can still be a *knas* — contra the apparent implication of the *mishna* that only payments exceeding actual damages qualify as such. Sometimes less-than-full payment is *tashlumim*, as with *tzurot*, and sometimes it is a *knas*, as with the half-damages of a goring *tam*.

This ruling carries two practical consequences developed throughout the *sugya*. First, a *knas* is not collected based on the tortfeasor's own admission: *modeih bi-knas patur*. Second, a *knas* can only be imposed by a court with proper *semikhah* — the ordained authority tracing back to Moshe Rabbeinu — and therefore cannot be collected outside of Eretz Yisrael. Just as such a court is required to impose lashes, so too it is required to impose a monetary fine. An individual's admission cannot substitute for the court's imposition. The connection between these two consequences is not incidental: wherever a payment is classified as *mammon*, one is believed to obligate oneself through one's own admission — *al pi atzmo* — precisely because a monetary debt exists independently of any court ruling and can be discharged by the debtor directly. A *knas*, by contrast, does not exist as an obligation until *beis din* imposes it, just as lashes are not an objective condition that self-imposed flogging can discharge; the court's active ruling is constitutive of the obligation itself.

## Unusual Animal Damage



### Meshuneh Examples

Having concluded the discussion of *chatzi nezek*, the Gemara now extends the principles of *knas* to cases of *meshuneh* — unusual or unexpected animal behavior. The examples given are a dog that eats large lambs and a cat that eats large chickens. Such behavior is *meshuneh hu* — it is not the normal feeding behavior of these animals. Because it is anomalous and unexpected, it is treated analogously to the *keren* of an unhabituated ox, and only half-damages are assessed.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַשְׁתָּא דְּאָמְרַתְּ פַּלְגָא נִיזְקָא קְנָסָא, הַאי כַּלְבָּא דְּאָכֵל אִימְּרֵי, וְשׁוּנָּרָא דְּאָכֵיל תַּרְנְגוֹלֵי רַבְרְבֵי, מְשׁוּנֶּה הוּא, וְלָא מַגְבֵּינַן בְּבָבֶל. אֲבָל זוּטְרֵי — אוֹרְחֵיהּ הוּא וּמַגְבֵּינַן.
> **Translation:** Based on that ruling, the Gemara concludes: **And now that you said** that payment of **half the damage** is **a fine, this dog that ate lambs, and a cat that ate large roosters, is unusual** damage, for which the owner is liable to pay only half the damage if the animal was innocuous, **and** therefore, **we do not collect** it **in Babylonia.** The payment for unusual damage is a fine, and fines cannot be collected in Babylonia, as there are no ordained judges authorized to adjudicate cases involving fines. **However,** if the cat ate **small** roosters, that **is its usual manner, and we collect** the damages in Babylonia, as it is a payment of money.

### Knas Jurisdiction


Since this half-payment falls in the category of *knas*, it carries the jurisdictional limitations discussed above. *Ve-lo magbinan b'Vavel* — we do not collect this payment in Babylonia. A *knas* requires a court possessing the authentic chain of *semikhah* going back to Moshe Rabbeinu — courts that were only empowered in Eretz Yisrael. Outside of Eretz Yisrael no such court exists, and therefore a *knas* cannot be imposed there.

There is an interesting conceptual question lurking here: why should *meshuneh* eating be classified under *keren* (intentional damage) rather than *shen* (an animal's ordinary feeding behavior)? Two approaches emerge from the broader discussion in Bava Kamma. One view holds that because the eating is abnormal it gets reclassified as *keren* — willful, unexpected damage. The other approach suggests it may remain categorized as *shen*, but since the animal was behaving in a way the owner could not have guarded against — analogous to the first gorings of a *tam* — perhaps half-payment and *knas* status apply even to what is technically a *shen*-type category. Either way, for our purposes the operative rule is: unusual behavior of this kind yields half-damages assessed as a *knas*, uncollectable in the Diaspora.

### Normal Behavior

By contrast, if a cat eats small chickens or a dog eats small animals, *orchei hu* — that is simply normal behavior. In such a case, full damages are assessed and collected in the ordinary way.

### Collection Methods

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי תְּפַס — לָא מַפְּקִינַן מִינֵּיהּ. וְאִי אָמַר: אַקְבְּעוּ לִי זִימְנָא לְאֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל — מַקְבְּעִינַן לֵיהּ. וְאִי לָא אָזֵיל — מְשַׁמְּתִינַן לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: **And** in cases of fines, **if** the injured party **seized** property from the offender in the amount of the fine, even in Babylonia **we do not repossess it from him,** as according to the letter of the law he is entitled to that payment, and the party from whom he seized the property cannot claim that he does not owe that payment. **And if** the injured party **said: Set me a time** to go **to** a court **in Eretz Yisrael,** where cases of fines are adjudicated, **we set** a time **for him, and if** the other disputant **does not go** to Israel as demanded, **we excommunicate him.**

Even in cases where a Diaspora court cannot formally adjudicate a *knas*, the damaged party is not left entirely without recourse. If the damagee has already seized property equivalent to his loss — *tafas* — the court will not compel him to return it. Of course, a court proceeding would still be necessary to determine the amount of damages and whether the seized property was proportionate. Additionally, if the damagee wishes to pursue the matter formally, *ve-ee amar akvu li zimna le-Eretz Yisrael* — he may ask the Diaspora court to set an appointment for him in a court in Eretz Yisrael. If the damager refuses to appear, *meshamtinan lei* — the Diaspora court places him under *cherem* until he complies. And regardless of whether such an appointment is set, the damager may be placed in *cherem* simply for maintaining a dangerous animal, on the grounds that *salek hezekach* — you must remove your hazard.

### Rabbi Natan

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בֵּין כָּךְ וּבֵין כָּךְ מְשַׁמְּתִינַן לֵיהּ. דְּאָמְרִינַן לֵיהּ: סַלֵּיק הֶזֵּיקָךְ, מִדְּרַבִּי נָתָן. דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי נָתָן אוֹמֵר: מִנַּיִן שֶׁלֹּא יְגַדֵּל אָדָם כֶּלֶב רַע בְּתוֹךְ בֵּיתוֹ, וְלֹא יַעֲמִיד סוּלָּם רָעוּעַ בְּתוֹךְ בֵּיתוֹ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְלֹא תָשִׂים דָּמִים בְּבֵיתֶךָ״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara adds: **Either way,** whether or not he agrees to go to Eretz Yisrael, if he keeps the cause of the damage, **we** in Babylonia **excommunicate him, as we say to him: Remove your** cause of **damage,** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Natan. As it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Natan says: From where** is it derived **that a person may not raise a vicious dog in his house, and may not place an unsteady ladder in his house?** It is **as it is stated:** “And you shall make a parapet for your roof **that you shall not place blood in your house”** (Deuteronomy 22:8). It is prohibited to leave a potentially dangerous object in one’s house, and one who refuses to remove it is excommunicated.

This connects to a *braita* citing Rabbi Natan: *minayin she-lo yigadel adam kelev ra be-toch beito* — how do we know that a person may not keep a dangerous dog in his house, nor maintain a shaky ladder? *She-ne'emar: ve-lo tasim damim be-veitecha* — as it is written, "you shall not place blood in your house." The point is that the obligation to remove a hazard applies even in circumstances where the animal's behavior may be a first-time occurrence. Whether this applies universally or only once the dangerous pattern has become established remains an open question.

## Seduction & Rape Payments



### Three vs. Four

The Gemara now returns to the *mishna* before us, which opens the fourth *perek*. The *mishna* states: a seduced girl (*mefutah*) receives three payments — *boshet*, *pegam*, and *knas* — while a raped girl (*anusah*) receives four, adding *tzaar* (pain and suffering) to the three. This distinction was already known from an earlier *mishna*, as the Gemara will shortly note, but the present *mishna* goes further in specifying to whom the payments go and under what circumstances.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נַעֲרָה שֶׁנִּתְפַּתְּתָה — בּוֹשְׁתָּהּ וּפְגָמָהּ וּקְנָסָהּ שֶׁל אָבִיהָ, וְהַצַּעַר בִּתְפוּסָה. עָמְדָה בַּדִּין עַד שֶׁלֹּא מֵת הָאָב — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל אָב. מֵת הָאָב — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל אַחִין.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: In the case of **a young woman who was seduced,** the compensation for **her humiliation and her degradation and her fine** belong **to her father. And** the same applies to the compensation for **pain in** the case of a woman who was **raped.** If the young woman **stood trial** against the seducer or rapist **before the father died, these** payments belong **to her father,** as stated above. If **the father died** before he collected the money from the offender, the payments belong **to** her **brothers.** As the father’s heirs, they inherit the money to which he was entitled before he passed away.

### Payments to Father

All of these payments — the embarrassment, the injury, the fine, and in the case of rape the pain and suffering — go to the father. The *mishna* states this explicitly: *boshet u-pegam ve-knasah shel aviha*, and *ve-ha-tzaar bi-tfusah* — the *tzaar* in a case of forcible seizure likewise belongs to the father.

### Father Alive Case

The *mishna* then details the distribution of payments according to the timing of various events. The baseline rule is straightforward: if the father is alive when the court case concludes and awards the payment, the money goes to him.

### Father Dies After

If the court has concluded — the father was alive at the time of *gemar din*, the court's final ruling — and only thereafter the father dies, *harei hen shel achin*: the money passes to the brothers. The logic is that once the court has ruled, the debt is owed directly to the father; his death causes it to pass to his heirs like any other asset of the estate.

### Case Before Death

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֲדַרַן עֲלָךְ אֵלּוּ נְעָרוֹת
> **Translation:** May we return to you chapter “These are the young girls.”


Conversely, if the court case had not yet been concluded before the father died — *lo hispika la'amod ba-din ad she-met av* — then *harei hen shel atzmah*: the money goes to the daughter herself. The conceptual framework underlying this, as the Gemara explains, is that the tortfeasor's obligation fundamentally runs to the daughter; the father is entitled to collect it through her by virtue of his legal relationship to her. Once the father has died before the court case, that parental relationship no longer exists, and the daughter receives the award directly.

### Woman Emancipated

A parallel principle applies if the daughter has become a *bogeret* — a fully adult, emancipated woman — before the court case concluded. In that case as well, *hareihen shel atzmah*: the money goes to her. The father's entitlement depends on both his being alive and her being a *naarah* or *ketanah* under his authority at the time of the ruling. If either condition fails — either he dies or she becomes a *bogeret* — the money flows to her directly.

### Rabbi Shimon View

Rabbi Shimon disagrees with the *chachamim* regarding the effect of *gemar din*. He holds: *im lo hispika li-gbot ad she-met ha-av hareihen shel atzmah* — if the money was not yet actually collected before the father died, it goes to the daughter, even if the court had already ruled in the father's favor. According to Rabbi Shimon, *gemar din* alone is insufficient to convert the obligation into a direct debt owed to the father. Even after the court's ruling, the payment is still conceptually routed through the daughter — think of it as a kind of automatic transfer that activates only when the money is actually paid. If the father dies before receiving payment, the transfer never activates, and the money remains with the daughter.

The *chachamim*, by contrast, hold that *gemar din* converts the obligation into a direct debt to the father. Once he has been awarded the money by the court, it is his asset; his subsequent death causes it to pass to his sons as part of the estate.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֹא הִסְפִּיקָה לַעֲמוֹד בַּדִּין עַד שֶׁמֵּת הָאָב — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל עַצְמָהּ, עָמְדָה בַּדִּין עַד שֶׁלֹּא בָּגְרָה — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל אָב, מֵת הָאָב — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל אַחִין. לֹא הִסְפִּיקָה לַעֲמוֹד בַּדִּין עַד שֶׁבָּגְרָה — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל עַצְמָהּ. רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: אִם לֹא הִסְפִּיקָה לִגְבּוֹת עַד שֶׁמֵּת הָאָב — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל עַצְמָהּ,
> **Translation:** However, if **she did not manage to stand in judgment before the father died,** and she was subsequently awarded the money, the compensation belongs **to her,** as she is now under her own jurisdiction due to the fact that she no longer has a father. If **she stood trial before she reached majority,** the payments belong **to her father,** and if **the father died, they** belong **to** her **brothers,** who inherit the money notwithstanding the fact that she has become a grown woman since the trial. If **she did not manage to stand in judgment before she reached majority,** the money belongs **to her. Rabbi Shimon says:** Even **if** she stood trial in her father’s lifetime but **did not manage to collect** the payments **before the father died,** the brothers do not inherit this money, as it belongs **to her.**

### Earnings vs. Knas


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַעֲשֵׂה יָדֶיהָ וּמְצִיאָתָהּ, אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁלֹּא גָּבְתָה, מֵת הָאָב — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל אַחִין.
> **Translation:** By contrast, with regard to **her earnings and** the lost **items** that **she** has **found, although she has not collected** them, e.g., she had yet to receive her wages, if **the father died they** belong **to** her **brothers.** These payments are considered the property of their father, as he was entitled to them before he passed away.

This ruling of Rabbi Shimon is, however, limited to the *knas* context. The *mishna* immediately contrasts it with the daughter's earnings and found objects: *ma'aseh yadeha u-metzi'asah af al pi she-lo gavsa met av hareihen shel achin* — her wages and found property, even if not yet collected at the time of the father's death, pass to the brothers. The distinction is that wages and found property become an immediate direct debt to the father the moment they are earned or found. The *knas*, by contrast — according to Rabbi Shimon — continues to flow through the daughter even after *gemar din*, and therefore does not pass to the brothers if the father dies before collection.

## Knas & False Oath



### Gemara's Question

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ מַאי קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן? תְּנֵינָא: הַמְפַתֶּה נוֹתֵן שְׁלֹשָׁה דְּבָרִים, וְהָאוֹנֵס אַרְבָּעָה. הַמְפַתֶּה נוֹתֵן בּוֹשֶׁת וּפְגָם וּקְנָס, מוֹסִיף עָלָיו אוֹנֵס שֶׁנּוֹתֵן אֶת הַצַּעַר! לְאָבִיהָ אִיצְטְרִיךְ לֵיהּ. לְאָבִיהָ נָמֵי פְּשִׁיטָא, מִדְּקָא יָהֵיב מְפַתֶּה, דְּאִי לְעַצְמָהּ — אַמַּאי יָהֵיב מְפַתֶּה? מִדַּעְתָּהּ עֲבַד!
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara asks: **What is** the mishna **teaching us? We** already **learned** this in a mishna (*Ketubot* 39a): **The seducer pays three types of indemnity and the rapist** pays **four. The seducer pays** compensation for his victim’s **humiliation and degradation and** for the **fine** the Torah imposes on a seducer. **A rapist adds an additional** payment, **as he pays** compensation for **the pain** she suffered. The Gemara answers: It **is necessary for** the mishna to teach that the money is given **to her father.** The Gemara asks: It **is also obvious** that the money goes **to her father, from** the fact **that a seducer pays** these types of indemnity, **as, if** one claims that the money goes **to her, why does a seducer pay** her at all? After all, **he acted with her consent;** how can she then claim compensation?

The Gemara opens this section with a challenge: *mai kamashmalan?* — what is the *mishna* actually teaching us? The first part of the *mishna*, stating that the seducer pays three payments and the rapist four, was already known from an earlier *mishna*. And the fact that all payments go to the father — is that not obvious as well?

### Why Seducer Pays

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עָמְדָה בַּדִּין אִיצְטְרִיכָא לֵיהּ, פְּלוּגְתָּא דְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן וְרַבָּנַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: It **was necessary for** the mishna to mention these cases in order to address the case where she **stood for judgment** before her father died, and then he died before collecting payment. In this case, there is **a dispute between Rabbi Shimon and the Rabbis** as to whether the sons inherit these payments from the father or whether the money belongs to the young woman.

The Gemara then provides the answer: the *chiddush* that all payments go to the father is not entirely self-evident, but it can be inferred from a different principle. The fact that the seducer — whose act was consensual — still owes payment demonstrates that the payment runs to the father, not to the daughter. *De'ilei atzma, amai yahiv mefateh? Midaatah avad* — if the money were owed to the daughter herself, why would the seducer pay? She consented. The very existence of the seducer's liability proves that the injured party, in the relevant legal sense, is the father. The Gemara therefore concludes that the true *chiddush* of the *mishna* is the detailed rules about *gemar din* — the debate between the *rabanan* and Rabbi Shimon about what happens after the court rules and before the money is collected.

### Ones/Pituy Debate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּנַן הָתָם: ״אָנַסְתָּ וּפִיתִּיתָ אֶת בִּתִּי״, וְהוּא אוֹמֵר: ״לֹא אָנַסְתִּי וְלֹא פִּיתִּיתִי״. ״מַשְׁבִּיעֲךָ אֲנִי״, וְאָמַר: ״אָמֵן״, וְאַחַר כָּךְ הוֹדָה — חַיָּיב.
> **Translation:** § **We learned** in a mishna **there** (*Shevuot* 36b) that if someone said to another: **You raped** my daughter, **or: You seduced my daughter, and** the other **says: I did not rape and I did not seduce,** to which the father replied: **I administer an oath to you, and** the defendant **said: Amen, and afterward he admitted** that he had raped or seduced the man’s daughter, he is **obligated** both in the payments of a rapist or a seducer as well as an additional one-fifth, and he must bring an offering for swearing falsely that he did not owe the money.

The Gemara now cites a *mishna* from tractate Shevuot to introduce the question of *korban shevuah* — the oath-offering — in the context of *knas*. The general rule of *korban shevuah* is that when a person falsely denies under oath an obligation that, had he admitted to it, he would have been required to pay, he brings a special *korban* and pays the principal plus a fifth. The question is whether this applies when the underlying obligation is a *knas*. The *mishna* in Shevuot records the case: a father says to someone, "You raped or seduced my daughter," and the accused denies it under oath and then admits. Is he liable for a *korban shevuah*?

### No Korban for Knas

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן פּוֹטֵר, שֶׁאֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם קְנָס עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ. אָמְרוּ לוֹ: אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁאֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם קְנָס עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ, אֲבָל מְשַׁלֵּם בּוֹשֶׁת וּפְגָם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Shimon exempts** him, **as he does not pay** the **fine on his own** admission. The accused individual is not considered to have taken a false oath in denial of a monetary charge because he would not have been obligated to pay the fine on the basis of his own admission of guilt. The Rabbis **said to him: Although he does not pay** the **fine on his own** admission, **indeed he does pay** compensation for **the humiliation and degradation on his own** admission. Consequently, he has denied a monetary claim, and therefore his false oath obligates him to add one-fifth and to bring an offering. This concludes the mishna.

Rabbi Shimon holds: *she'eino meshalem knas al pi atzmo* — since you do not pay a *knas* based on your own admission, a false denial of a *knas* does not generate liability for a *korban shevuah*. The *chachamim* respond: even if that is true for the *knas* component, the accused would still pay for *boshet* and *pegam* based on his own admission — those are *mammon*, not *knas* — and therefore a false denial of those components does generate *korban* liability. The debate, as the Gemara notes, is not truly about the underlying legal principles, which both sides share. Both agree: no *korban shevuah* for *knas*, yes *korban shevuah* for *mammon*. The disagreement is about which component the father's claim primarily invokes — is the father demanding the *knas* (in which case denial is exempt), or the *boshet* and *pegam* (in which case denial obligates)?

### Abayes Question

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּעָא מִינֵּיהּ אַבָּיֵי מֵרַבָּה, הָאוֹמֵר לַחֲבֵירוֹ: ״אָנַסְתָּ וּפִיתִּיתָ אֶת בִּתִּי, וְהֶעֱמַדְתִּיךָ בַּדִּין, וְנִתְחַיַּיבְתָּ לִי מָמוֹן״, וְהוּא אוֹמֵר: ״לֹא אָנַסְתִּי וְלֹא פִּיתִּיתִי, וְלֹא הֶעֱמַדְתַּנִי בַּדִּין, וְלֹא נִתְחַיַּיבְתִּי לְךָ מָמוֹן״, וְנִשְׁבַּע וְהוֹדָה, לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן מַאי?
> **Translation:** In light of this mishna, **Abaye raised a dilemma before Rabba:** With regard to **one who says to another: You raped** my daughter, **or: You seduced my daughter, and I made you stand in judgment** for your actions, **and you were found obligated** to pay **me money** but you did not do so, **and** the defendant **says: I did not rape, or: I did not seduce, and you did not make me stand in judgment, and I was not found obligated** to pay **you money, and** the defendant **took an oath** that he was telling the truth **and** subsequently **admitted** his guilt, **according to** the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon, what** is the *halakha*?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֵּיוָן דְּעָמַד בַּדִּין — מָמוֹנָא הָוֵאי, וּמִיחַיַּיב עֲלֵיהּ קׇרְבַּן שְׁבוּעָה. אוֹ דִלְמָא, אַף עַל גַּב דְּעָמַד בַּדִּין, קְנָס הָוֵי? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מָמוֹנָא הָוֵי, וּמִיחַיַּיב עֲלֵיהּ קׇרְבַּן שְׁבוּעָה.
> **Translation:** Abaye explains the two sides of the dilemma: **Since he stood trial** and was found liable, **is** this considered a regular **monetary** obligation, **and** therefore **he is liable to** bring the **offering** for taking a false **oath** to deny a monetary claim? **Or perhaps** one can argue that **although he stood trial** and the court ordered him to pay, the payment **is** in essence **a fine.** Rabba **said to him:** Since he has already stood trial, **it is** considered a regular **monetary** payment, **and he is liable to** bring the **offering of an oath.**

This sets up the major inquiry of the *sugya*: Abaye poses a question to Rabba. The case: a father says, "You raped or seduced my daughter, and not only that — we already brought the case to court, *amadeticha ba-din*, and the court ruled that you owe me the money." The accused denies everything — the act, the court case, and the resulting obligation — and swears falsely, then admits. *Le-Rabbi Shimon mah?* — what is the law according to Rabbi Shimon?

The question is whether *gemar din* — the court's final ruling — converts a *knas* into *mammon*. The logic supporting conversion is compelling: the reason a *knas* cannot be self-incurred through admission is that it requires a court to impose it; without the court, there is no debt. But once the court has in fact imposed the debt, the obstacle is removed. The person is no longer admitting to something that could not exist through his own admission — he is admitting to a debt already created by the court. Should that admission now obligate him, and should a false denial generate *korban shevuah* liability? Or does the obligation retain its character as *knas* permanently — since it originated as a *knas*, perhaps it always remains one?

The Gemara frames this precisely: *keivan de-amad ba-din mammon havei* — perhaps once there has been a court ruling, the obligation is now simply *mammon*, and we should be liable for the *korban shevuah* since we are disputing an established monetary debt. *O dilma, af al gav de-amad ba-din, knas havei* — or perhaps even after the court has ruled it retains its identity as *knas*, having originated as one, and therefore the *modeh bi-knas* exemption persists and there is no *korban* liability.

### Ravas Answer

Rava answers Abaye: *mamona hu, mechayve lei korban shevuah* — after *gemar din*, it is *mammon*, and denying it generates *korban shevuah* liability.

### Braita Contradiction

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵיתִיבֵיהּ, רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: יָכוֹל הָאוֹמֵר לַחֲבֵירוֹ ״אָנַסְתָּ וּפִיתִּיתָ אֶת בִּתִּי״, וְהוּא אוֹמֵר: ״לֹא אָנַסְתִּי וְלֹא פִּיתִּיתִי״; ״הֵמִית שׁוֹרְךָ אֶת עַבְדִּי״, וְהוּא אוֹמֵר: ״לֹא הֵמִית״; אוֹ שֶׁאָמַר לוֹ עַבְדּוֹ ״הִפַּלְתָּ אֶת שִׁינִּי וְסִימִיתָ אֶת עֵינִי״, וְהוּא אוֹמֵר: ״לֹא הִפַּלְתִּי וְלֹא סִימִיתִי״, וְנִשְׁבַּע וְהוֹדָה, יָכוֹל יְהֵא חַיָּיב —
> **Translation:** Abaye **raised an objection to** Rabba from the following *baraita*. **Rabbi Shimon says:** One **might** have thought that in the case of **one who says to another: You raped** my daughter, **or: You seduced my daughter, and he says: I did not rape** her, **or: I did not seduce** her, or if he claimed: **Your ox killed my slave, and he says:** It **did not kill** him, **or if his slave said to him: You knocked out my tooth, or: You blinded my eye** and you are therefore obligated to emancipate me, **and he says: I did not knock** it **out, or: I did not blind** your eye, **and he takes an oath but** later **admitted** to the truth of the accusation, one **might** have thought that he **should be liable** to bring an offering for a false oath denying a monetary claim.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וְכִחֵשׁ בַּעֲמִיתוֹ בְּפִקָּדוֹן אוֹ בִתְשׂוּמֶת יָד אוֹ בְגָזֵל אוֹ עָשַׁק אֶת עֲמִיתוֹ. אוֹ מָצָא אֲבֵידָה וְכִחֶשׁ בָּהּ וְנִשְׁבַּע עַל שָׁקֶר״, מָה אֵלּוּ מְיוּחָדִין שֶׁהֵן מָמוֹן, אַף כֹּל שֶׁהֵן מָמוֹן. יָצְאוּ אֵלּוּ, שֶׁהֵן קְנָס.
> **Translation:** Therefore, with regard to the offering for a false oath in denial of a monetary claim, **the verse states:** “If anyone sin, and commit a trespass against the Lord **and deal falsely with his neighbor in a matter of a deposit or of a pledge or of a robbery or have oppressed his neighbor, or have found that which was lost and deal falsely with it, and swear to a lie”** (Leviticus 5:21–22). **Just as** all these **matters** listed in the verse **are unique** in **that they are monetary** obligations equal in value to the loss one has caused another individual, **so too,** this *halakha* applies to **all** obligations **that are monetary** claims, which **excludes these** payments of a rapist, a seducer, and the like, **as they are fines.**

The Gemara immediately challenges this from a *braita*. Rabbi Shimon states in that *braita*: if someone demands of another "you raped or seduced my daughter," or "your ox killed my slave," or "you knocked out my slave's tooth" or "blinded my slave's eye" — all *knas* cases — and the accused swears falsely and then admits, one might think he is liable for a *korban shevuah*. The *braita* teaches: *yatzu elu she-hein knas* — excluded are cases of *knas*, from which one is exempt. And the *chachamim* hold — from the verse *u-ma'alah ma'al* — that one is *chayav* even in these cases. So we have a *machlokes*: *chachamim* say *chayav*, Rabbi Shimon says *patur*.

## Rabbi Shimon Position



### Reisha/Seifa Analysis

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי לָאו בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין! לָא, בְּשֶׁלֹּא עָמַד בַּדִּין.
> **Translation:** **What, is it not** referring to a case **where he has stood trial,** and yet Rabbi Shimon does not render him liable for the oath as the payment was originally a fine? Rabba refutes this argument: **No,** that *baraita* is referring to a situation **where he has not stood trial.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא מִדְּרֵישָׁא בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין, סֵיפָא נָמֵי בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין. דְּקָתָנֵי רֵישָׁא: אֵין לִי אֶלָּא דְּבָרִים שֶׁמְּשַׁלְּמִין עֲלֵיהֶם אֶת הַקֶּרֶן. תַּשְׁלוּמֵי כֶפֶל, תַּשְׁלוּמֵי אַרְבָּעָה וַחֲמִשָּׁה, וְהָאוֹנֵס וְהַמְפַתֶּה וּמוֹצִיא שֵׁם רַע, מִנַּיִן — תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וּמָעֲלָה מַעַל״, רִיבָּה.
> **Translation:** Abaye persists: **But from** the fact **that** the **first clause** of the *baraita* deals **with** one **who has stood trial,** it follows that the **latter clause also** deals **with** one **who has stood trial. As** the *baraita* **teaches** in its **first clause: I have** derived the *halakha* **only** for **matters for which one pays the principal.** With regard to **the payments** that are **double** the principal, and **payments** that are **four and five** times the principal, and those of **the rapist, and the seducer, and the defamer, from where** is it derived that all these are included in the liability to bring an offering for falsely taking an oath on a deposit? **The verse states:** “If anyone sin **and commit a trespass [*ma’ala ma’al*]”** (Leviticus 5:21). The doubled usage of the word trespass serves to **amplify** and include any false oath taken in denial of monetary liability.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֵיכִי דָמֵי? אִי דְּלֹא עָמַד בַּדִּין, כְּפֵילָא מִי אִיכָּא? אֶלָּא פְּשִׁיטָא בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין. וּמִדְּרֵישָׁא בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין, סֵיפָא נָמֵי בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין!
> **Translation:** Abaye analyzes this statement: **What are the circumstances? If** this is referring to a situation **when he has not stood trial, is there double** payment in that case? Everyone agrees that one who admits his guilt is exempt from the double payment, and yet this obligation is mentioned in the *baraita*. **Rather,** it **is obvious** that the *baraita* is referring to a case **where** it is claimed that he has already **stood trial** and was declared liable to pay the double payment, and the accused individual denies this claim. Abaye summarizes his question: **And from** the fact **that** the **first clause** of this *baraita* deals **with** one **who has stood trial,** the **latter clause also** deals **with** one **who has stood trial,** and even so Rabbi Shimon does not deem him liable to bring an offering for his oath.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ, יָכֵילְנָא לְשַׁנּוֹיֵי לָךְ: רֵישָׁא בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין, וְסֵיפָא בְּשֶׁלֹּא עָמַד בַּדִּין, וְכוּלַּהּ רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן הִיא. וְשִׁינּוּיֵי דְּחִיקֵי לָא מְשַׁנֵּינַן לָךְ. דְּאִם כֵּן אָמְרַתְּ לִי: לִיתְנֵי רֵישָׁא ״רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר״, אוֹ לִיתְנֵי סֵיפָא ״דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן״.
> **Translation:** Rabba **said to him: I could answer you** that the **first clause** deals **with** one **who** is accused of already having **stood trial** and been deemed liable, **and** the **latter clause** deals **with** one **who has not stood trial, and** this **entire** *baraita* **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon.** According to this answer, Rabbi Shimon concedes that after one has been deemed liable in court, the double payment attains the status of a regular monetary obligation rather than a fine, and therefore in the first case in the *baraita* he is liable to bring an offering and a payment for his admission. **But I will not answer you a far-fetched answer, for if** it is **so,** that the entire *baraita* represents the opinion of Rabbi Shimon, **you** could **say to me: Let** the *tanna* of the *baraita* either **teach** explicitly in the **first clause: Rabbi Shimon says, or let him teach** in the **latter clause:** This is **the statement of Rabbi Shimon.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא: כּוּלַּהּ בְּשֶׁעָמַד בַּדִּין, וְרֵישָׁא רַבָּנַן וְסֵיפָא רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן.
> **Translation:** Rabba continued: **Rather,** I will say that the **entire** *baraita* is referring **to** one **who has stood trial, and** as for the difference in *halakha*, the **first clause** is in accordance with the opinion of **the Rabbis,** who deem one liable to bring the offering of an oath in a case where the plaintiff says that the defendant stood trial, was found liable, and swore falsely. **And** the **latter clause** represents the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon,** who exempts one who confesses from bringing the offering of an oath.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמוֹדֵינָא לָךְ לְעִנְיַן קׇרְבַּן שְׁבוּעָה דְּרַחֲמָנָא פַּטְרֵיהּ, מִ״וְּכִחֵשׁ״.
> **Translation:** **And I concede to you,** Abaye, **with regard to** the liability to bring **an offering** for falsely taking **an oath** on a deposit, **that the Merciful One exempts him** from this offering here, **based upon** the verse **“And deal falsely** with his neighbor in a matter of a deposit,” (Leviticus 5:21), which indicates that one is liable to bring an offering only if he lied about a claim that was originally a monetary obligation.

The Gemara now probes whether the *braita*'s ruling applies before or after *gemar din*. The argument proceeds as follows: the *reisha* of the *braita*, attributed to the *chachamim*, explicitly lists *ones u-mefateh* among the cases where one would be *chayav* for a *korban shevuah*. But there is a logical difficulty: if the obligation were purely *knas* with no *mammon* component whatsoever, and if one would be exempt even when admitting, how could the *chachamim* say one is *chayav* for denying? You cannot be liable for falsely denying something you would owe nothing for even if you admitted. The very fact that the *chachamim* impose *chiyuv* implies we must be speaking of a case where the *knas* has already been converted by *gemar din* — otherwise, how could denial generate liability?

And since the *reisha* (the *chachamim*'s ruling) must refer to *she-amad ba-din*, the *seifa* (Rabbi Shimon's ruling of *patur*) must also refer to *she-amad ba-din*. This would demonstrate that Rabbi Shimon exempts even after *gemar din* — the *knas* retains its identity even then, yielding no *korban shevuah* and (by extension) no obligation upon admission.

Rava responds that he could technically distinguish the *braita*: the *reisha* speaks of *amad ba-din* and the *seifa* of *lo amad ba-din*, and the entire *braita* is Rabbi Shimon's own position rather than a debate with the *chachamim*. But he immediately rejects this answer: *shinuya de-chika lo meshanina lach* — I will not give you a forced answer. For if we read the *braita* that way, Rabbi Shimon's name should have appeared either at the beginning or consistently at the end; the way the *braita* is structured, with Rabbi Shimon appearing only in the *seifa* as a dissent, clearly indicates a debate between Rabbi Shimon and the *chachamim*. And since both must be addressing the same stage — *amad ba-din* — we see that Rabbi Shimon holds the *knas* remains *knas* even after *gemar din*, at least for the purposes of *korban shevuah*.

Rava therefore concedes: *u-modina lach le-inyan korban shevuah de-Rachmana patrei* — for the specific laws of *korban shevuah*, Rabbi Shimon is correct. There is a special verse — *ve-khichesh ba-amito* — that exempts obligations originating as *knas* from the *korban shevuah* regime, even after *gemar din*. But Rava maintains his core position: in other areas of *halakha*, after *gemar din*, the *knas* does convert to *mammon*. The exemption from *korban shevuah* is a *halakha* specific to the laws of oaths, grounded in a particular verse, and does not reflect the general legal identity of the post-*gemar din* obligation.

### Inheritance Proof

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי קָאָמֵינָא מָמוֹן הָוֵי — לְהוֹרִישׁוֹ לְבָנָיו.
> **Translation:** **And when I say** that Rabbi Shimon maintains that after one is declared liable in court his obligation to pay **is** considered a regular **monetary** payment rather than a fine, that is not to say that he is liable to bring an offering for falsely denying a monetary claim, but rather **to** say that the recipient of the payment **bequeaths it to his sons.** Unlike a fine, which does not pass by inheritance to one’s heirs, this is classified as a regular monetary payment. Consequently, if the perpetrator was deemed liable in court and ordered to pay the father of the girl he raped or seduced, and the father died before receiving payment, his sons inherit the right to that payment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵיתִיבֵיהּ, רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: אִם לֹא הִסְפִּיקָה לִגְבּוֹת עַד שֶׁמֵּת הָאָב — הֲרֵי הֵן שֶׁל עַצְמָהּ. וְאִי אָמְרַתְּ מָמוֹן הָוֵי לְהוֹרִישׁוֹ לְבָנָיו, לְעַצְמָהּ אַמַּאי? דְּאַחִין בָּעֵי מִיהְוֵי!
> **Translation:** Abaye **raised an objection to** this last point from the mishna. **Rabbi Shimon says: If** the daughter **did not manage to collect** the payments **before the father died, they** belong **to her. And if you say** that this fine **is a monetary** payment **to** the extent that one can **bequeath it to his sons** after the trial, **why** does the money belong **to her?** Since the trial has taken place, it **should be** the property of **the brothers** by inheritance from their father, as it is already considered a regular monetary obligation that is owed to the father.

The Gemara next turns to our own *mishna* to challenge Rava's position. *Itivei* — we raise a contradiction. Rabbi Shimon rules in the *mishna*: if the money was not collected before the father died, it goes to the daughter. Now, if Rava is correct that after *gemar din* the obligation converts to *mammon* in general, then once the court rules it becomes a direct debt to the father, and upon his death it should pass as *yerusha* to his sons — *de-achin ba'ei le-hava*. Why then does Rabbi Shimon say she keeps it? This appears to demonstrate that according to Rabbi Shimon, *gemar din* does not convert *knas* to *mammon* — the obligation remains fundamentally routed through the daughter even after the court rules, and therefore upon the father's death it never becomes part of his estate.

### 22 Year Struggle

The Gemara then delivers a remarkable statement: *hai milta kashya ba le-Rabba ve-Rav Yosef esrin ve-tartin shanin* — this difficulty challenged Rabba and Rav Yosef for twenty-two years without resolution. The *Tosafot* raise an obvious question: if the difficulties were insurmountable, why not simply retract? All the evidence — the *korban shevuah* ruling and the *mishna* about inheritance — points toward Rabbi Shimon treating *knas* as *knas* even after *gemar din*. Why hold on to a tradition for twenty-two years if no reconciliation could be found? *Tosafot* answers: they evidently had a firm tradition that according to Rabbi Shimon, *gemar din* does in general convert *knas* to *mammon*. But they could not reconcile that tradition with the two exceptions — *korban shevuah* and the *mishna* about inheritance — for which the *knas* character clearly persists.

### Two Exceptions

After Rabba's twenty-two years as *rosh yeshiva*, he passed away and Rav Yosef assumed the position. Rashi notes beautifully that in order to honor Rav Yosef — who was the greater *baki* even if Rabba had been the sharper analyst — he was assisted *min ha-shamayim* in resolving the question that had stymied his predecessor. The answer came to him when he assumed the leadership of the *yeshiva*.

### Korban Exception

Rav Yosef explains that the tradition Rabba had — that *gemar din* converts *knas* to *mammon* according to Rabbi Shimon — is indeed correct as a general rule, but there are two exceptions. The first exception: the laws of *korban shevuah*. Even after *gemar din*, when the original obligation was a *knas*, one is exempt from the *korban shevuah*. This is because the *Torah* requires the *korban* only for obligations that originated as *mammon*. The verse *ve-khichesh ba-amito* establishes that if it started its life as *knas*, the *korban* exemption applies regardless of subsequent conversions through *gemar din*.

### Ones/Pituah Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: הַאי מִילְּתָא קְשַׁאי בַּהּ רַבָּה וְרַב יוֹסֵף עֶשְׂרִין וְתַרְתֵּין שְׁנִין וְלָא אִיפְּרַק, עַד דִּיתֵיב רַב יוֹסֵף בְּרֵישָׁא וּפָירְקַהּ: שָׁאנֵי הָתָם, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״וְנָתַן הָאִישׁ הַשּׁוֹכֵב עִמָּהּ לַאֲבִי הַנַּעֲרָה חֲמִשִּׁים כֶּסֶף״, לֹא זִיכְּתָה תּוֹרָה לָאָב אֶלָּא מִשְּׁעַת נְתִינָה.
> **Translation:** **Rava said: This matter was difficult for Rabba and Rav Yosef** for **twenty-two years without resolution, until Rav Yosef sat at the head** of the academy **and resolved it** in the following manner: **There,** in the case of a rape, **it is different, as the verse states: “And the man who laid with her shall give the young woman’s father fifty shekels of silver”** (Deuteronomy 22:29), from which it is inferred: **The Torah entitled the father** to this money **only from the time of giving.** Consequently, if the father dies before receiving the money, he does not bequeath his right to the money to his sons. Instead, the daughter is considered to take her father’s place as the plaintiff, because she was the victim, and the money is paid to her.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי קָאָמַר רַבָּה מָמוֹנָא הָוֵי לְהוֹרִישׁוֹ לְבָנָיו — בִּשְׁאָר קְנָסוֹת.
> **Translation:** **And when Rabba said** that the fine imposed by a court **is** considered a regular **monetary obligation** with regard to one’s ability **to bequeath it to his sons,** he was not referring to this particular case of a rapist or seducer, but only **to other fines,** which do have the status of regular monetary obligations after the court delivers its verdict.

The second exception: the specific case of *ones u-mefateh* — rape and seduction. Even after *gemar din*, these payments are not considered a direct debt to the father; they continue to run through the daughter. The explanation lies in the verse: *ve-natan ha-ish ha-shochev imah le-avi ha-na'arah chamishim kasef* — "the man who lay with her shall give to the father of the girl fifty pieces of silver." The word *ve-natan* — "he shall give" — connotes that the father's entitlement to the money crystallizes only at the moment of actual payment (*netinah*). *Lo zikhtah Torah le-av ella mi-sha'at netinah* — the Torah did not grant the father rights until the moment of giving. Therefore even after *gemar din*, the money remains conceptually the daughter's until it is actually handed over; if the father dies beforehand, it stays with her.

### Yiten vs. Natan

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה גַּבֵּי עֶבֶד, דִּכְתִיב: ״כֶּסֶף שְׁלֹשִׁים שְׁקָלִים יִתֵּן לַאדוֹנָיו״, הָכִי נָמֵי לֹא זִיכְּתָה תּוֹרָה לָאָדוֹן אֶלָּא מִשְּׁעַת נְתִינָה?! ״יִתֵּן״ לְחוּד, ״וְנָתַן״ לְחוּד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **However, if** that is **so,** that the verb “give” is explained in this manner, **with regard to** an ox that killed **a slave, where it is written: “He shall give to their master thirty shekels of silver”** (Exodus 21:32), **so too** will you say that **the Torah entitled the master only from the time of giving?** The Gemara answers: **“Shall give [*yiten*],” is distinct,** and **“shall give [*venatan*],” is distinct.** The first expression, which is stated with regard to an ox that killed a slave, does not indicate that the recipient acquires the right to the money only from the moment it is given, whereas the formulation employed in the case of rape does indicate that this is the case.

The Gemara then tests whether this principle extends to the case of the slave: the verse regarding a goring ox that kills a slave also uses a payment term — *yitein* — "he shall give thirty shekels of silver to the master." The Gemara distinguishes: *yitein lachud ve-natan lachud* — *yitein* (future) and *natan* (past/completed) carry different legal valences. The verse in the slave case uses *yitein* — "will give" — which does not imply the same condition of actual payment before the right vests. By contrast, the verse in *ones u-mefateh* uses *ve-natan* — connoting a completed act of giving — which establishes that the father's rights in *ones u-mefateh* arise only at the moment of actual payment.

### Final Synthesis

The final synthesis of the *sugya* can now be stated. Rabbi Shimon's position, properly understood, is as follows: as a general rule, *gemar din* converts a *knas* into *mammon* — it becomes an established debt, transferable as part of an estate, and presumably triggering admission-based liability in the ordinary case. However, there are two domains where the *knas* character persists even after *gemar din*. First, the laws of *korban shevuah*: because the *Torah* requires the *korban* only for obligations whose inception was *mammon*, a false oath about an obligation that originated as *knas* — even if since converted through *gemar din* — remains exempt. Second, the payments of *ones u-mefateh*: because the *Torah* conditions the father's rights on actual payment, the obligation remains routed through the daughter after *gemar din*, and therefore does not pass to the brothers upon the father's death. In all other cases of *knas*, after *gemar din* the obligation is *mammon* for all purposes.
