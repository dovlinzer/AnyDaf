## Opening Mishnah & Tumah



### Opening & Daf Overview

We open today with daf 47, amud alef, picking up with the *mishnah* at the very top of the page. We are concluding one *perek* and beginning the next.

### Beard Shaving Q&A

Before turning to the text, one clarification worth noting: a *nazir* is only prohibited from cutting the hair of the head. Shaving the beard presents no problem whatsoever, since the prohibition of *nezirut* concerns only the long hair of the head.

### Mishnah: Blood & Tumah

The *mishnah* reads: *mi shenizrak alav echad min hadamim venitma* — one upon whom one of the blood-sprinklings has been performed and then became *tamei*. To set the context: we have just concluded the extended discussion in which the *chachamim* hold that even a single *korban* completes one's *nezirut*. The *mishnah* now addresses the case where, after one *korban* has already been brought, the *nazir* becomes *tamei*. What is the consequence?

### Eliezer vs. Chachamim

*Rabbi Eliezer omer soter et hakol* — Rabbi Eliezer says it undoes everything. This is a natural extension of Rabbi Eliezer's position from earlier, that *achar ma'aseh kulam* — one requires all the *korbanot* to complete *nezirut*. Since the *nezirut* was not yet concluded, the subsequent *tumah* destroys whatever had been accomplished.

*Vachachamim omrim yavi shear korbanotav veyit'har* — the *chachamim* say that the *nezirut* is indeed finished. The *tumah* does not undermine anything; the *korban* already brought remains valid. All that remains is the obligation to bring the remaining *korbanot*, which she completes after becoming *tahor*.

## Tosafot: Tahara & Korban



### Tosafot: Veyit'har Order

The language of the *mishnah* — *yavi shear korbanotav veyit'har* — might seem to suggest that one brings the *korbanot* first and only afterwards becomes *tahor*. Tosafot addresses this directly, explaining that *veyit'har* in this context means: *lichsheyit'har* — only once one has become *tahor* may the remaining *korbanot* be brought. The order in the *mishnah* should not be read literally as "bring and then purify," but rather "bring [once purified]."

### Sources: Yerushalmi

Tosafot cites the *Yerushalmi*, which reads the *mishnah* straightforwardly as requiring *taharah* first and only then bringing the *korbanot*: *kach mefaresh biyerushalmi stam matnita d'tahara v'achar kach yavi korbanotav*.

The basis for this reading lies in the *pesukim* themselves. The *Torah*, when addressing a *nazir* who became *tamei meit*, describes a process in which he first achieves full *taharah* and only on the eighth day brings his *korban*. This reflects an implicit *Torah* demand: a *nazir* may not have his *korbanot* of *nezirut* brought while in a state of *tumah*. There is a broader *halachic* discussion about *tamei meshalayach korbanotav* — whether a *tamei* person may have *korbanot* brought on his behalf — and the ruling is that only certain light levels of *tumah* permit this. One who is *tamei meit*, or one who is a *metzora* or *zav*, certainly cannot be *meshalayach korbanotav*. But Tosafot's point is more specific: even if one might debate the general rule, in the particular context of a *nazir* who is *tamei meit*, the *pesukim* make clear that the *Torah* insists on full *taharah* before any *korbanot* of *nezirut* are brought.

### Tumah Can't Destroy

Once we understand this, Tosafot explains why the *tumah* cannot destroy what has already been accomplished. The *korban* that was brought while the *nazir* was still *tahor* was valid — *harei hu kodem shenitma ra'uy litiglachas*, before he became *tamei* he was fit for the *tiglachat*, and the *korban* was brought in a state of *hechsher*. There is therefore no basis to disqualify it retroactively. However, any *korban* brought after the *tumah* but before achieving *taharah* would indeed be invalid: *aval hazevachim shehikriv mishenitma yistor* — whatever he brought after becoming *tamei* and before becoming *tahor* does not count.

The principle Tosafot articulates is: *dekapid kra sheyavi korbanot neziroot betaharah* — the *Torah* insists that the *korbanot* of *nezirut* be brought in a state of *taharah*. After the *nazir* undergoes *taharah*, including the *hazayah* of days three and seven, he may then bring the remaining *korbanot*. One might ask whether this is a specific demand rooted in the *nazir*'s situation with *tumat meit* — where the *Torah* explicitly walks him through a process of *taharah* before the *korban* on day eight — or whether it reflects a more general principle about a *tamei* person having *korbanot* brought on his behalf. Tosafot appears to draw on the implicit model established by the *nazir tamei meit* case, in which the *Torah* clearly requires the *nazir* to exit that state of *tumah* entirely before any *korbanot* are brought. Even now, where the *nezirut* is effectively over and the *tumah* is no longer serving as a *stirah*, Tosafot maintains that same framework: the *korbanot* of *nezirut* cannot be brought until the *nazir* is fully *tahor*. Whether one grounds this in the specific *pesukim* of *nazir v'tumat meit* or in a broader question about *tamei meshalayach korbanotav*, both paths converge on the same practical conclusion — a conclusion that, as the previous section noted, finds clear support in the *Yerushalmi*'s reading of the *mishnah*.

## Eliezer: Soter Korbanot



### Tosafot Clarification

Tosafot, in his very first comment, makes this explicit: *v'nitma Rabbi Eliezer omer soter hakol v'lo kol hayamim k'mforesh ba-Gemara d'Rabbi Eliezer hu d'amar l'eil nitma l'achar milo soter zayin ela soter korbanot kama* — Rabbi Eliezer says *soter hakol*, but not all the days, as the *Gemara* clarifies; what is destroyed is specifically the *korbanot*. And that is indeed the context of these *mishnayot* — the very question that arose the day before regarding shaving and the first *korban* not being valid. The dispute between Rabbi Eliezer and the *chachamim* concerns whether the *korbanot* brought previously remain good or are nullified: Rabbi Eliezer holds that *tumah* before *nezirut* is complete invalidates both the prior *korban* and any subsequent ones, while the *chachamim* hold that the *korban* already brought stands, the *tumah* does not undermine the *nezirut*, and after becoming *tahor* the *nazir* may complete the remaining *korbanot*. The primary focus is thus the *korbanot*, not the days — though naturally the two are related.

## Miriam from Tarmod Story



### Miriam's Daughter Dies

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ מִי שֶׁנִּזְרַק עָלָיו אֶחָד מִן הַדָּמִים וְנִטְמָא, רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר אוֹמֵר: סוֹתֵר אֶת הַכֹּל. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: יָבִיא שְׁאָר קׇרְבְּנוֹתָיו וְיִטְהָר. אָמְרוּ לוֹ: מַעֲשֶׂה בְּמִרְיָם הַתַּרְמוֹדִית שֶׁנִּזְרַק עָלֶיהָ אֶחָד מִן הַדָּמִים, וּבָאוּ וְאָמְרוּ לָהּ עַל בִּתָּהּ שֶׁהָיְתָה מְסוּכֶּנֶת, וְהָלְכָה וּמָצְאָה שֶׁמֵּתָה. וְאָמְרוּ חֲכָמִים: תָּבִיא שְׁאָר קׇרְבְּנוֹתֶיהָ וְתִטְהָר.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: With regard to **one on whose behalf the blood of one** of his nazirite offerings **was sprinkled** on the altar, **and he became ritually impure** before bringing the rest of his offerings, **Rabbi Eliezer says:** His impurity **negates the entire** tally, and he remains a nazirite. **And the Rabbis say: Let him bring the rest of his offerings and be purified.** The Rabbis **said to** Rabbi Eliezer: **An incident** occurred **involving Miriam of Tarmod** who was a nazirite, **that the blood of one** of her offerings **was sprinkled on her behalf, and they came and told her that her daughter was mortally ill. And she went and found** that her daughter **was dead,** and thereby contracted impurity. **And the Rabbis said: Let her bring the rest of her offerings and be purified.**

The *chachamim* bolster their position with a practical precedent: *ma'aseh b'Miriam haTarmodit* — there was a story involving Miriam from Tarmod. She had taken upon herself a *neder* of *nezirut* and one of the blood-sprinklings had already been performed on her behalf — *shenizrak aleha echad min hadamim*. At that moment, she was informed that her daughter was in mortal danger. She ran to her daughter and found that she had died — *vehalcha umatza shemeta*. She thus became *tamei meit* after one *korban* had already been brought.

### Chachamim's Ruling

The *chachamim* ruled: *tavi shear korbanoteha v'tit-har* — let her bring the remaining *korbanot* once she becomes *tahor*. Her *nezirut* was complete after that first *korban*; the subsequent *tumah* did nothing to undermine it. The *korban* that had been brought stood, and her obligation was only to complete the remaining *korbanot* after achieving *taharah*.

This ruling stands as a direct practical refutation of Rabbi Eliezer's position. Rabbi Eliezer holds that the *tumah* destroys the *korbanot* and effectively nullifies what was accomplished; the *chachamim* demonstrated through the case of Miriam that this is not so.

## Eliezer: Soter Korba (II)



### Gemara: Soter Definition

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ קָתָנֵי, רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר אוֹמֵר: סוֹתֵר אֶת הַכֹּל. וְהָאָמַר רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר: כׇּל אַחַר מְלֹאת שִׁבְעָה סוֹתֵר! אָמַר רַב: מַאי ״סוֹתֵר״ נָמֵי דְּקָאָמַר רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר — סוֹתֵר קׇרְבְּנוֹתָיו.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: It **is taught** in the mishna: **Rabbi Eliezer says** that this **negates the entire** tally. The Gemara asks: **But didn’t Rabbi Eliezer say** (16b): With regard to **any** nazirite who became ritually impure **after the completion** of his term, this **negates** only **seven days. Rav said: What** does: **Negates, that Rabbi Eliezer said** in the mishna here mean? It means that his impurity **negates** all **his offerings.** Rabbi Eliezer did not mean that the nazirite must count his entire term of naziriteship afresh; rather, he must bring all his offerings a second time, including the one whose blood was sprinkled before he became impure.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכִי נָמֵי מִסְתַּבְּרָא, דְּקָתָנֵי: וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים תָּבִיא שְׁאָר קׇרְבְּנוֹתֶיהָ וְתִטְהָר, שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: **So too, it is reasonable** that this is the meaning of Rabbi Eliezer’s statement, **as** the mishna later **teaches: And the Rabbis said: Let her bring the rest of her offerings and be purified. Learn from here** that they disagree only with regard to the offerings, but not the naziriteship itself.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַעֲשֶׂה נָמֵי בְּמִרְיָם הַתַּרְמוֹדִית שֶׁנְּזָרֵק עָלֶיהָ אֶחָד מִן הַדָּמִים, וּבָאוּ וְהוֹדִיעוּהָ עַל בִּתָּהּ שֶׁהָיְתָה מְסוּכֶּנֶת, וְהָלְכָה וּמָצְאָה שֶׁמֵּתָה, וְאָמְרוּ חֲכָמִים: תָּבִיא שְׁאָר קׇרְבְּנוֹתֶיהָ וְתִטְהָר. שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites the rest of the mishna, which also indicates that the *tanna’im* disagree with regard to the offerings. **And an incident also** occurred **involving Miriam of Tarmod, that the blood of one** of her offerings **was sprinkled on her behalf, and they came and notified her that her daughter was mortally ill. And she went and found** that her daughter **was dead,** and thereby contracted impurity. **And the Rabbis said: Let her bring the rest of her offerings and be purified.** The Gemara concludes: Indeed, **learn from** the mishna that this is so.

The *Gemara* now steps back and presses the question: the *mishnah* states that Rabbi Eliezer says *soter et hakol*, and elsewhere Rabbi Eliezer ruled that *tumah* after the full thirty days requires only seven additional days — so what does *soter* mean here? *Amar Rav mai soter d'ka'amar Rabbi Eliezer soter korbanotav* — Rav states: the *soter* Rabbi Eliezer refers to is that it destroys the *korbanot*. Any *korban* previously brought is nullified, and any *korban* brought in an improper state is likewise invalid. The *chachamim*, by contrast, hold that the first *korban* remains fully valid — *hacha nami savarta d'chachamim im tavi shear korbanoteha v'tit-har, shma mina* — as the case of Miriam from Tarmod confirms: let her bring the remaining *korbanot* once she becomes *tahor*, and this resolves the question.

The focus, then, in both the *mishnah* and the story of Miriam, is on the *korbanot* — not on the number of days. Days are implicated insofar as destroying *nezirut* means more days are required, but what is immediately at stake is whether the *korbanot* already brought retain their validity.

## Nazir to Tumah & Kohanic



### Perek Shloshim: Issurim

With the declaration of *hadran alach sheloshah minim*, we conclude the *perek* of *sheloshah minim*. That *perek* was structured around the three core prohibitions of a *nazir*: wine, shaving, and *tumah*. These three areas opened broader *halachic* discussions with general relevance. The wine prohibition led into the discussion of *ta'am ke'ikar* — the principle that taste functions like substance in matters of forbidden mixtures. The prohibition on shaving led to an examination of what constitutes shaving with respect to the beard and the *pe'ot*. And the prohibition on *tumah* opened the discussion of *tumat kohanim* — whether a *kohen* or *nazir* who is already *tamei meit* transgresses again by becoming *tamei* again, and the laws of *meit mitzvah*.

### Kol Tumah Nazir

Now, as we enter the new *perek*, we continue and deepen the discussion of *tumah*, because there is much more to learn from the relationship between *nazir* and *kohen* with respect to the restrictions on *tumah*. The central organizing principle of the coming *perek* is the rule known as *kol tumah she'ein haNazir megale'ach aleha ein Kohen muzhar aleha* — any *tumah* for which a *nazir* does not shave, a *kohen* is not prohibited against.

This principle might seem to refer only to *tumat sheretz*, *tumat neveilah*, *zivah*, *tzara'at*, and the like — types of *tumah* that are simply outside the scope of what *nezirut* addresses. But in fact it reaches further: even within the domain of *tumat meit* itself, there are categories that, by received tradition, do not trigger the obligation for a *nazir* to shave or to restart his *nezirut*, and therefore a *kohen* is likewise not prohibited against them. This creates an important area of inquiry — things that are technically *tumat meit* and yet fall outside the prohibitions both of *nezirut* and of the *kohen*'s restrictions against *tumah*. This has direct practical relevance for contemporary questions involving *kohanim*.

## Kohen Gadol, Nazir & Meit



### Kohen Gadol vs Nazir

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַדְרָן עֲלָךְ שְׁלֹשָׁה מִינִין
> **Translation:** 

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל וְנָזִיר אֵין מִיטַּמְּאִין לִקְרוֹבֵיהֶן, אֲבָל מִיטַּמְּאִין לְמֵת מִצְוָה. הָיוּ מְהַלְּכִין בַּדֶּרֶךְ וּמָצְאוּ מֵת מִצְוָה — רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר אוֹמֵר: יִטַּמֵּא כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל, וְאַל יִטַּמֵּא נָזִיר. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: יִטַּמֵּא נָזִיר, וְאַל יִטַּמֵּא כֹּהֵן הֶדְיוֹט.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **A High Priest and a nazirite may not become ritually impure** even **to** bury **their** deceased **relatives. However, they become impure to** bury **a corpse with no one to bury it [*met mitzva*].** If one of them comes across the corpse of a Jew, and there is nobody else available to bury it, he must bury the body. If a High Priest and a nazirite **were walking along the way and they found a *met mitzva*,** and one of them can tend to the burial by himself, **Rabbi Eliezer says: Let** the **High Priest become impure, and** do **not let** the **nazirite become impure. And the Rabbis say: Let** the **nazirite become impure, and** do **not let** even **a common priest become impure.**

The *mishnah* states: *Kohen Gadol veNazir ein mitamim likroveihem* — a *kohen gadol* and a *nazir* do not become *tamei* even to their immediate relatives. This is a striking parallel. A normal *kohen* may become *tamei* to his seven close relatives; a *kohen gadol* cannot. Conceptually, the *kohen gadol* is always in a state of readiness to serve — *min hamikdash lo yeitzei* — he is never permitted to remove himself from that role, even for the death of an immediate family member. The *nazir*, interestingly, shares this same restriction according to the *pesukim*: he too cannot become *tamei* even to an immediate relative.

Both, however, do become *tamei* for a *meit mitzvah*: *aval mitamim lemeit mitzvah*. This is not stated explicitly in the *Torah* but is understood by the *rabbis* from the *pesukim*, as the *Gemara* will explain. The *mishnah* then addresses the question of priority: *hayu mehalchim baderech u-matzu meit mitzvah* — if a *kohen gadol* and a *nazir* are traveling together and encounter a *meit mitzvah*, who should become *tamei* first?

The underlying issue here touches on the concept of *hutra* versus *dichuya* — whether in situations like *meit mitzvah* or *pikuach nefesh*, the prohibition is entirely suspended (*hutra*), or merely overridden by a competing value (*dichuya*). The *dichuya* perspective leads naturally to the effort to minimize the degree of override: if only one person needs to become *tamei* to fulfill the obligation, we try to determine which person's *tumah* is least consequential.

### Eliezer: Kohen Gadol

Rabbi Eliezer says: *yitma Kohen she'eino meivi korban al tumato v'al yitma Nazir shemeivi korban al tumato* — let the *kohen* become *tamei*, because a *kohen*'s *tumah* (under normal circumstances) does not require him to bring a *korban* as part of the consequences. The *nazir*, on the other hand, whose becoming *tamei* destroys his *nezirut* and requires him to bring a *korban*, suffers far more grievous consequences. Even though under normal circumstances both would be violating a *lav*, the practical fallout for the *nazir* is far weightier. Therefore, protect the *nazir* and let the *kohen gadol* become *tamei*.

This reasoning reflects a *dichuya* sensibility: the prohibition is not entirely dissolved by the presence of a *meit mitzvah*, but rather overridden by a competing obligation — and precisely because the prohibition still registers, we try to minimize the degree of override by directing the *tumah* toward whichever person will suffer the lesser consequence. Rabbi Eliezer's answer is that the *kohen*'s *tumah*, however serious, is simply less consequential than the *nazir*'s, and so the *kohen gadol* is the one who should become *tamei*.

One might ask about a *nazir Shimshon* — such a *nazir* does not bring a *korban* for *tumah* to begin with, since a *nazir Shimshon mitamei lemeitim* in any case. But that would be a moot point, as the *nazir Shimshon*'s status is sui generis.

### Chachamim: Nazir Priority

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לָהֶם רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר: יִטַּמֵּא כֹּהֵן — שֶׁאֵינוֹ מֵבִיא קׇרְבָּן עַל טוּמְאָתוֹ, וְאַל יִטַּמֵּא נָזִיר — שֶׁהוּא מֵבִיא קׇרְבָּן עַל טוּמְאָתוֹ. אָמְרוּ לוֹ: יִטַּמֵּא נָזִיר — שֶׁאֵין קְדוּשָּׁתוֹ קְדוּשַּׁת עוֹלָם, וְאַל יִטַּמֵּא כֹּהֵן — שֶׁקְּדוּשָּׁתוֹ קְדוּשַּׁת עוֹלָם.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Eliezer said to** the Rabbis: It is preferable to **let** the **priest become impure, as he does not bring an offering for his impurity, and** do **not let** the **nazirite become impure, as he brings an offering for his impurity.** The Rabbis **said to him:** On the contrary, **let** the **nazirite become impure, as his sanctity is not permanent, and** do **not let a priest become impure, as his sanctity is permanent.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ בִּשְׁלָמָא כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל וְנָזִיר, הַאי סָבַר: כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל עָדִיף, וְהַאי סָבַר: נָזִיר עָדִיף.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: In light of the mishna’s dispute between Rabbi Eliezer and the Rabbis, the Gemara compares the status of various individuals. **Granted,** with regard to **a High Priest and a nazirite,** one can explain the dispute as follows. **This** Sage, the Rabbis, **holds** that it is **preferable** that **a High Priest** remain ritually pure, as his sanctity is permanent. **And this** Sage, Rabbi Eliezer, **holds** that it is **preferable** that **a nazirite** remain ritually pure, as he is obligated to bring an offering for his impurity.

The *chachamim* respond with a fundamentally different framework: *yitma Nazir she'ein kedushato kedushat olam v'al yitma Kohen shekedushato kedushat olam* — let the *nazir* become *tamei*, because the *nazir*'s *kedushah* is only a *kedushah* of time, a temporary status, while the *kohen*'s *kedushah* is permanent.

The argument is not about the practical consequences of becoming *tamei*, but about the quality of the violation. When a *kohen*'s *kedushah* is breached, something essential to his very identity is transgressed. A *nazir*'s *kedushah*, while real and meaningful, is a status he accepted upon himself, not an intrinsic identity. It may even be a temporary status — though Tosafot notes that even a *nazir olam* does not change this calculus, because the fundamental character of *nezirut* as a *kedushah sha'ah* remains. The *chachamim* go further: they maintain this principle even as against a *kohen hedyot*, not just a *kohen gadol*. For Rabbi Eliezer, even a *kohen gadol* becomes *tamei* before a *nazir*; for the *chachamim*, even a *nazir* becomes *tamei* before a *kohen hedyot*. This explains why the *girsa* shifts in the text when recording the *chachamim*'s position — they ground the priority in *kedushat olam*, which belongs to any *kohen*, not only the *kohen gadol*.

The tension between the two positions is illuminating: Rabbi Eliezer measures by *halachic* consequence, the *chachamim* by the ontological weight of the violation.

## Two Kohen Gadolim Cases



### Anointed vs. Garmented

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָשׁוּחַ בְּשֶׁמֶן הַמִּשְׁחָה
> **Translation:** Additionally, if the two walking together were a High Priest **anointed with the anointing oil,** as was performed during the First Temple period,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמְרוּבֵּה בְגָדִים — מָשׁוּחַ בְּשֶׁמֶן הַמִּשְׁחָה עָדִיף, דְּאִילּוּ מָשׁוּחַ בְּשֶׁמֶן הַמִּשְׁחָה — מֵבִיא פַּר הַבָּא עַל כׇּל הַמִּצְוֹת, וְאִילּוּ מְרוּבֵּה בְגָדִים — אֵין מֵבִיא.
> **Translation:** **and** a High Priest **of many garments,** i.e., one who was not anointed with oil but who was sanctified by wearing the eight garments of a High Priest, it is **preferable** that **the one who was anointed with the anointing oil** remain ritually pure. The Gemara explains: **As** a High Priest **anointed with the anointing oil brings** the **bull brought for all the mitzvot,** i.e., if an anointed priest ruled erroneously with regard to a prohibition that if a Jew transgressed it he would be liable to bring a sin-offering, he brings a bull for his sin-offering (see Leviticus 4:3–12), **while** a High Priest **of many garments does not bring** a bull.

The *Gemara* now proceeds through a series of thought experiments involving cases where two figures of comparable but not identical status encounter a *meit mitzvah* together. The first case: *Mashuach beshemen hamishcha u'merubeh bivgadim* — one *kohen gadol* who was anointed with the anointing oil, and another who attained the office of *kohen gadol* by having worn the eight priestly garments (*merubeh begadim*). The anointing oil was secreted away in the time of King Yoshiyahu, so the question concerns what would have been the rule had such a scenario existed.

The *Gemara* rules: *mashuach beshemen hamishcha adif* — the one anointed with the anointing oil has the higher status and is therefore protected from *tumah*. The reason: *de'illu mashuach beshemen hamishcha meivi par al kol hamitzvot* — there is a special *halacha* in Masechet Horayot that if a *kohen gadol* who was *nimshach beshemen hamishcha* issued an incorrect ruling and then followed his own ruling, he brings a special *par* as an *atonement korban*. This privilege and the elevated status it reflects belongs only to the *mashuach beshemen hamishcha*, not to the *merubeh begadim*. Therefore, the anointed one has an objectively higher status and is accordingly protected.

The question of how such a scenario could realistically arise — with both simultaneously alive and both either equally active or equally retired — is something Tosafot addresses, though the precise reconstruction is complex.

### Off-Duty vs. Active

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָשׁוּחַ שֶׁעָבַר וּמְרוּבֵּה בְגָדִים — מְרוּבֵּה בְגָדִים עָדִיף, דִּמְרוּבֵּה בְגָדִים — עָבֵיד עֲבוֹדָה, וְאִילּוּ מָשׁוּחַ שֶׁעָבַר — לָאו בַּר עֲבוֹדָה הוּא.
> **Translation:** In a case where **a former anointed** High Priest, i.e., a priest who had temporarily substituted for a High Priest, is walking together with one of **many garments,** it is **preferable** that the one who wears **many garments** remain ritually pure. The reason is **that** a High Priest of **many garments** still **performs the service, whereas a former anointed** High Priest **is no** longer **able** to perform the **service.**

The second case: *mashuach she'avar u'merubeh begadim* — a *mashuach beshemen hamishcha* who is no longer in active service, alongside one who is *merubeh begadim* and currently serving. Before arriving at the ruling, it is worth pausing on what makes this case structurally interesting: chronologically, the *mashuach beshemen hamishcha* must have come first, since the anointing oil predates the institution of *merubeh begadim*, and yet here he is the one who has been permanently put out to pasture. The natural question is how to construct a realistic scenario in which the figure of objectively higher intrinsic status ends up as the permanently retired one while the *merubeh begadim* is the active *kohen gadol*. One way to imagine it: the original *mashuach beshemen hamishcha* became *tamei* while in service, a substitute was appointed as *merubeh begadim* in the interim, and then when the first one became *tahor* and returned, it was the substitute who was permanently stood down — except that in this case we need it to work the other way, with the *mashuach* being the one retired. How precisely to reconstruct that sequence is a question Tosafot addresses, though as the *Gemara* itself will clarify, the precise mechanics are complex.

The ruling, in any case, reverses the first: *merubeh begadim adif*, *de'merubeh begadim avid avodah* — the one currently in active service takes priority and is protected. The reason is straightforward: what overrides the inherent prestige of having been anointed with the oil is the immediate practical reality that this person is actively performing *avodah*, and *tumah* would interfere with that. *Ve'ilu mashuach she'avar lav bar avodah hu* — the one who has been permanently removed from service cannot perform *avodah* regardless; there is no comparable disruption if he becomes *tamei*.

### Avar Chemat Kiryo vs Muma

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עָבַר מֵחֲמַת קֵירוּיוֹ וְעָבַר מֵחֲמַת מוּמוֹ — עָבַר מֵחֲמַת קֵירוּיוֹ עָדִיף, דְּאִילּוּ הַאי חֲזִי לַעֲבוֹדָה לְמָחָר, וְאִילּוּ עָבַר מֵחֲמַת מוּמוֹ לֹא חֲזִי לָעֲבוֹדָה.
> **Translation:** In a case where an anointed High Priest who temporarily **left** his role **due to his seminal emission** is walking with a former anointed High Priest who left his role **due to his blemish,** it is **preferable** that the former High Priest who **left due to his seminal emission** remain ritually pure, **as this one,** the High Priest who experienced an emission, **is fit for the** Temple **service the following day, while** the former anointed High Priest who **left due to his blemish is no** longer **fit for the service** at all (see Leviticus 21:16–24).

The third case: *avar machmas kiryo ve'avar machmas muma* — two *kohanim gedolim* who are both currently off duty. One was temporarily removed because he was a *ba'al keri* (a seminal emission), and the other because of a bodily blemish. The ruling: *avar machmas kiryo adif* — the one temporarily off duty due to *keri* is protected, *de'hu chazi le'avodah lemachar* — because he will be able to return to *avodah* the following day after immersing in the *mikveh*. The one removed due to a blemish, *ve'ilu avar machmas muma lo chazi le'avodah* — cannot currently serve, and Tosafot adds that even if the blemish is potentially temporary, one does not know when it will heal, whereas the *ba'al keri*'s disqualification is precisely one day. All three of these cases follow a clear logical structure: the one whose active relationship with the *avodah* is more immediate and more restorable is always the one whose *taharah* is more protected.

## Mashuach vs. Sgan



### Question Posed

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיבַּעְיָא לְהוּ: מְשׁוּחַ מִלְחָמָה וּסְגָן, הֵי מִינַּיְיהוּ עֲדִיף? מְשׁוּחַ מִלְחָמָה עֲדִיף — דַּחֲזֵי לְמִלְחָמָה, אוֹ דִּלְמָא סְגָן עֲדִיף — דַּחֲזֵי לַעֲבוֹדָה?
> **Translation:** § The above cases are all easily resolved and are mentioned merely to introduce the following inquiries, for which there are no obvious answers. **A dilemma was raised before** the Sages. For **which of these** two is it **preferable** that he remain ritually pure: A priest **anointed for war,** who was anointed with oil and appointed to admonish the troops before battle (see Deuteronomy 20:2) **or the deputy [*segan*]** High Priest? Is it **preferable** that the priest **anointed for war** remain ritually pure, **as he is fit for war? Or, perhaps** it is **preferable** that the **deputy** High Priest remain ritually pure, **as he is fit for service** in the Temple in place of the High Priest.

Now the *Gemara* raises a question that is less immediately obvious: *iba'ya lehu mashuach milchama ve'sgan hai minaihu adif* — which has higher priority, the *mashuach milchama* or the *sgan*?

The *mashuach milchama* is the *kohen* anointed to lead the people into battle — the one who addresses the people before war with the words *mi ha'ish hayarei ve'rach halevav yelech ve'yashuv leveiso*. He holds a specially anointed role of considerable stature. The *sgan* is the vice *kohen gadol*, whose role is to stand ready to substitute for the *kohen gadol* should he become *tamei*, particularly on *yom kippur* when a *kohen gadol* is absolutely indispensable.

Both are a rank below the *kohen gadol*, functioning as executive vice-presidents of sorts. The question: if they both encounter a *meit mitzvah*, which one is protected and which one must become *tamei*? The *mashuach milchama* has an active, weighty role — he is not merely waiting in the wings, he is on stage. The *sgan*, one might think, is essentially defined by his readiness to step in, which only rarely becomes relevant. So perhaps the *mashuach milchama*'s status warrants protection?

### Baraita Answer

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע, דְּתַנְיָא: אֵין בֵּין מְשׁוּחַ מִלְחָמָה לִסְגָן, אֶלָּא שֶׁאִם הָיוּ מְהַלְּכִין בְּדֶרֶךְ וּמָצְאוּ מֵת מִצְוָה — יִטַּמֵּא מְשׁוּחַ מִלְחָמָה וְאַל יִטַּמֵּא הַסְּגָן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Come** and **hear** a resolution, **as it is taught** in a *baraita*: **The difference between** a priest **anointed for war and a deputy** High Priest **is only that if they were walking along the way and found a *met mitzva*,** the ruling is: **Let the one anointed for war become impure, and** do **not let the deputy become impure.**

The *Gemara* cites a *baraita* in response: *tashma detanya, ein bein mashuach milchama lesgan ela she'im hayu mehalchim baderech u'matzu meit mitzvah, yitamei mashuach milchama ve'al yitamei sgan* — the only difference between the *mashuach milchama* and the *sgan* is this: if they are going on the way and find a *meit mitzvah*, the *mashuach milchama* becomes *tamei* and not the *sgan*. So the *sgan* is the one who is protected.

### Rationale: Sgan's Role

The rationale is precisely what makes this a striking ruling. The *sgan*'s entire job description is to remain *tahor* and ready. *Rebbi Chanina ben Antigonus omer: lama tiknu sgan lekohen gadol? she'im ye'arei bo psul harei nichnas umeshamesh tachtav* — why did they institute a *sgan* for the *kohen gadol*? So that if the *kohen gadol* becomes disqualified, the *sgan* enters and serves in his place. His primary responsibility is not some active task he is currently performing — it is the maintenance of his *taharah* and readiness. Becoming *tamei* would be a fundamental violation of the *sgan*'s core function in a way that the *mashuach milchama*'s becoming *tamei* is not. The *mashuach milchama*'s role does not require him to preserve his *taharah* as its central purpose; the *sgan*'s role does, almost by definition.

## Two Different Priorities



### Contradiction Noted

But this ruling seems to contradict another *baraita*: *mashuach milchama kodem lesgan* — the *mashuach milchama* comes before the *sgan*, apparently suggesting that the *mashuach milchama* has higher priority. Does this not contradict the rule that the *sgan* is protected from *tumah*?

### Two Contexts, Two Rules

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָתַנְיָא: מְשׁוּחַ מִלְחָמָה קוֹדֵם לִסְגָן! אָמַר מָר זוּטְרָא: לְעִנְיַן הַחֲיוֹתוֹ — מְשׁוּחַ מִלְחָמָה עֲדִיף, מַאי טַעְמָא — דִּתְלוּ בֵּיהּ רַבִּים,
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But isn’t it taught** in a different *baraita*: A priest **anointed for war takes precedence over the deputy** High Priest? **Mar Zutra said:** This is not difficult. **With regard to preserving his life** and rescuing him from captivity or from a dangerous situation, it is **preferable** to preserve **the one anointed for war. What is the reason** for this? The reason is **that the public depends on him** in a time of war.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְעִנְיַן טוּמְאָה סְגָן עֲדִיף. דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי חֲנִינָא בֶּן אַנְטִיגְנוֹס אוֹמֵר: לָמָּה תִּקְּנוּ סְגָן לְכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל — שֶׁאִם אֵירַע בּוֹ פְּסוּל הֲרֵי נִכְנָס וּמְשַׁמֵּשׁ תַּחְתָּיו.
> **Translation:** **But with regard to ritual impurity,** it is **preferable** that the **deputy** High Priest remain ritually pure, **as it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Ḥanina ben Antigonus says: Why did** the Sages **institute a deputy for the High Priest?** So **that if a disqualification befalls** the High Priest, his deputy **can enter** the Temple **and serve in his stead.** The deputy High Priest cannot fulfill this function if he is allowed to become ritually impure.

The *Gemara* resolves: *le'inyan hachayoso mashuach milchama adif* — when it comes to saving a life, the *mashuach milchama* takes priority. *Mai ta'ama? detali beih rabim* — because the welfare of many depends on him. If one must choose whose life to save, the *mashuach milchama*'s role in protecting the entire people gives him priority, much as one would prioritize saving the Secretary of Defense over a deputy official whose role is primarily one of readiness. It is worth noting, however, that this principle of prioritization does not operate in isolation. There is a *mishna* in *Horayot* that enumerates whose life takes precedence when only one can be saved, and while it might appear to resolve such questions directly, Rav Moshe Feinstein reads that *mishna* as applying only after every other conceivable factor — severity of injury, likelihood of survival, and any other relevant consideration one could imagine — has already been weighed and found perfectly equal. Only then, as an absolute last resort, does one consult the *mishna*'s hierarchy of sacred status. This effectively renders the *mishna*'s ranking almost entirely theoretical in practice. The *Gemara*'s ruling here about the *mashuach milchama* fits within that broader framework of prioritization.

*Ule'inyan tumah sgan adif* — but when it comes to *tumah*, the *sgan* is given priority and is protected. *Detanya Rabbi Chanina ben Antigonus omer: lama tiknu sgan lekohen gadol* — because the entire rationale for the *sgan*'s existence is to remain *tahor* and ready to serve if needed. These are two different questions yielding two different answers, and there is no contradiction.

### Tosafot: Mashuach Mil.

Tosafot raises an important point here. The *baraita* states *ein bein mashuach milchama lesgan* — there is no difference between them except for this matter of *tumah*. But this seems plainly incorrect: there are in fact many significant differences between the two. The *mashuach milchama* wears the garments of the *kohen gadol*, while the *sgan* wears the garments of a *kohen hedyot* — even when performing his role, and even in the context of *yom kippur*, when the *kohen gadol* himself wears *bigdei lavan*. Moreover, the five restrictions enumerated in the *parasha* for the *kohen gadol* — not disheveling his hair, not rending his garments, not becoming *tamei* to the dead, not marrying a widow, and being required to marry only a *betulah* — all of these apply to the *mashuach milchama* but not to the *sgan*.

This means the *mashuach milchama* is effectively a quasi-*kohen gadol* in a different sphere: not in the *Beit HaMikdash* but in the domain of the battlefield and the camp. So what does the *baraita* mean by *ein bein*?

Tosafot explains: the *ein bein* means that there is no area in which we give greater weight to the *sgan* over the *mashuach milchama*, except for this one matter — that the *sgan* does not become *tamei* when they encounter a *meit mitzvah* together. In every other domain, the *mashuach milchama* has equal or greater status. But in this one instance, because the *sgan*'s entire identity is bound up with maintaining *taharah*, that is the single exception where the *sgan* is protected and the *mashuach milchama* is not.

## Individual Override Tumah



### Individual Permission

Having completed the analysis of who takes priority when a *kohen gadol* and *nazir* travel together, the *Gemara* steps back to address the foundational premise. *Ad kan lo peligi ela be-Kohen Gadol ve-Nazir ki ka azli bahadi hadadi* — all the debate has been about who takes priority when they are traveling together. *Aval chad chad le-chudei bartzit meiyei inun* — but if each of them is alone, it is taken for granted that each one individually is permitted to become *tamei* to a *meit mitzvah*.

### Source Question

This prompts a fundamental question: *minani milei* — where does this come from? The *Torah* never states explicitly that a *meit mitzvah* overrides the prohibitions on *tumah* for a *kohen gadol* or a *nazir*. What is the textual source?

## Meit Mitzvah: Pesukkim



### Pesuk: Le'aviv U'le'imo

The *Gemara* cites the relevant *pesukim*. Regarding the *kohen gadol* it is written: *al kol nafshot met lo yavo* — he shall not approach any dead person, and separately: *le'aviv u'le'imo lo yitama* — not even to his father and mother shall he become *tamei*.

### Kal Va'chomer Logic

The *Gemara* asks: who is the subject of *al kol nafshot met lo yavo*? If it is addressing non-relatives, this would be redundant — *kal va-chomer hu*. A normal *kohen* who may become *tamei* to his relatives may not become *tamei* to non-relatives; how much more so a *kohen gadol* who cannot even become *tamei* to relatives — surely he cannot become *tamei* to non-relatives. There would be no need for the *Torah* to state this explicitly. Tosafot frames the same point more directly, without the circularity of a formal *kal va-chomer*: a *kohen gadol* is already a *kohen*, and as such is already prohibited against *tumah* to non-relatives in his basic capacity as a *kohen*. Therefore *al kol nafshot met* cannot be teaching us about non-relatives.

### Both Phrases Extra?

It follows that *al kol nafshot met lo yavo* must be referring to relatives — the *Torah* is telling us that a *kohen gadol* may not become *tamei* even to his own family. If that is so, then the subsequent phrase *le'aviv u'le'imo lo yitama* — not to his father and mother — appears to be entirely redundant. We have already been told he cannot become *tamei* to any relative. Why repeat it for father and mother specifically?

### Le'aviv Meit Mitzvah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עַד כָּאן לָא פְּלִיגִי אֶלָּא בְּכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל וְנָזִיר כִּי קָא אָזְלִי בַּהֲדֵי הֲדָדֵי, אֲבָל חַד חַד לְחוֹדֵיהּ בַּר אִיטַּמּוֹיֵי אִינּוּן. מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara turns its attention to the *halakha* of the mishna. Rabbi Eliezer and the Rabbis **disagree only with regard to a High Priest and a nazirite who are walking together** and find a *met mitzva*, in which case one of them must become impure. **However,** it is evident that if **each of them** is walking **separately, they are able,** i.e., they are required, **to become impure. From where are these matters** derived? From where is it learned that a High Priest and a nazirite, who are prohibited from becoming impure even to bury their relatives, must nevertheless become impure to bury a *met mitzva*?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״עַל כׇּל נַפְשׁוֹת מֵת לֹא יָבֹא״, בַּמָּה הַכָּתוּב מְדַבֵּר? אִי בִּרְחוֹקִים — קַל וָחוֹמֶר הוּא: וּמָה כֹּהֵן הֶדְיוֹט שֶׁהוּא מִיטַּמֵּא לִקְרוֹבִים אֵינוֹ מִיטַּמֵּא לִרְחוֹקִים, כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל שֶׁאֵינוֹ מִיטַּמֵּא לִקְרוֹבִים, אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁאֵינוֹ מִיטַּמֵּא לִרְחוֹקִים? אֶלָּא בִּקְרוֹבִים הַכָּתוּב מְדַבֵּר.
> **Translation:** It is **as the Sages taught:** The verse states with regard to a High Priest: **“Neither shall he go in to any dead bodies;** nor defile himself for his father or for his mother” (Leviticus 21:11). **With regard to what** bodies **is the verse speaking? If** it is referring **to distant** people, i.e., non-relatives, **that** *halakha* can be derived by **an *a fortiori*** inference: **And if a common priest, who becomes impure to** bury his **close** family members, **may not become impure to** bury **distant** people, then with regard to **a High Priest, who does not become impure** even **to** bury **close** members of his family, **is it not logical that he does not become impure to** bury **distant** people? **Rather, the verse is speaking of close** family members, and it prohibits a High Priest from becoming impure to bury any person, even his relatives.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְאָבִיו הוּא דְּאֵינוֹ מִיטַּמֵּא, הָא מִיטַּמֵּא הוּא לְמֵת מִצְוָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: With regard to the rest of the verse: “Nor defile himself for his father or for his mother” (Leviticus 21:11), each of these clauses must serve to teach a novel *halakha*. **And** the phrase “for his father” teaches: **It is to** bury **his father that he may not become ritually impure,** from which it may be inferred that **he becomes impure to** bury **a *met mitzva*.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וּלְאִמּוֹ״ — לִגְזֵירָה שָׁוָה, לְכִדְרַבִּי. דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי אוֹמֵר: גַּבֵּי נָזִיר ״בְּמוֹתָם״ אֵינוֹ מִיטַּמֵּא, אֲבָל מִיטַּמֵּא הוּא לְנִגְעָם וּלְזִיבָתָם. וְאֵין לִי אֶלָּא בְּנָזִיר, בְּכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל מִנַּיִן?
> **Translation:** The phrase **“or for his mother”** serves **as a verbal analogy, in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **as it is taught** in a *baraita*: **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **says:** It states **with regard to a nazirite:** “He shall not become defiled for them when they die” (Numbers 6:7), which indicates: It is only **when** his relatives **die** that **he may not become impure** for them. **However, he may become impure for their leprosy and for their emission of a *zav*,** i.e., a nazirite is not forbidden to contract those forms of ritual impurity. **And I have** derived **only** that this *halakha* applies **to a nazirite,** the subject of this verse. **From where** do I derive that this applies **to a High Priest** as well?

Because both phrases are extra, each can be expounded to teach something new. Before turning to what *le'aviv* teaches, however, it is worth pausing on why we are entitled to treat it as extra in the first place, since there is a subtle circularity lurking in the reasoning. The *kal va-chomer* that establishes *al kol nafshot met* as referring to relatives runs as follows: a regular *kohen* may become *tamei* to his father yet is forbidden to become *tamei* to non-relatives; therefore a *kohen gadol*, who cannot even become *tamei* to his father, certainly cannot become *tamei* to non-relatives. Since he already cannot become *tamei* to non-relatives, the Torah must be speaking of relatives when it says *al kol nafshot met lo yavo* — which in turn leaves *le'aviv u'le'imo* without a role and available for a *drasha*. But the circularity is this: the only reason we know a *kohen gadol* cannot become *tamei* to his father — which is what the *kal va-chomer* starts from — is because the verse explicitly says *le'aviv u'le'imo lo yitama*. Yet that is precisely the phrase we then want to declare extra. We seem to be using *le'aviv* as a literal prohibition in order to build the *kal va-chomer*, and then turning around and saying it is superfluous.

Tosafot resolves this by pointing out that the *kal va-chomer* is not really necessary. The simpler point is that a *kohen gadol* is, at minimum, no less than an ordinary *kohen*. In his basic standing as a *kohen*, he is already prohibited from becoming *tamei* to non-relatives — that restriction requires no new derivation. Given that, the phrase *al kol nafshot met lo yavo* cannot be coming to tell us something as obvious as that he may not become *tamei* to non-relatives; of course he cannot. It must therefore be addressing relatives. Once we arrive at that conclusion on its own terms — without leaning on *le'aviv* to get there — the phrase *le'aviv u'le'imo lo yitama* becomes genuinely extra and freely available for a *drasha*, without any circularity.

*Le'aviv* — "to his father" — is then cited as an example: *ha mitamei hu le-met mitzvah* — but he does become *tamei* to a *meit mitzvah*. The specific mention of *le'aviv* signals that there is someone to whom he may become *tamei* — and this is the *meit mitzvah*. The logic follows the principle of *miyut achar miyut ela lerabbot*: two restrictions in sequence imply that within those restrictions there is a permitted category. The *Torah* has already said he cannot become *tamei* to relatives; adding "not to the father" implies — but there is one case where he can, namely a *meit mitzvah*.

## Gezeira Shava: Le'imo



### Nazir's Le'imo Scope

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַרְתָּ: לֹא יֵאָמֵר ״אִמּוֹ״ בְּכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל, שֶׁאֵין תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר, שֶׁהֲרֵי קַל וָחוֹמֶר הוּא: וּמָה אִם בְּמָקוֹם שֶׁכֹּהֵן הֶדְיוֹט מִיטַּמֵּא לְאָחִיו מֵאָבִיו — אֵין כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל מִיטַּמֵּא לְאָבִיו, מָקוֹם שֶׁאֵין כֹּהֵן הֶדְיוֹט מִיטַּמֵּא לְאָחִיו מֵאִמּוֹ — אֵינוֹ דִּין שֶׁאֵין כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל מִיטַּמֵּא לְאִמּוֹ?
> **Translation:** **You** can **say** as follows: The verse need **not say “his mother” with regard to a High Priest, as** there is **no** need for **the verse to state** this, **since** this *halakha* **is** derived ***a fortiori*: And if in a case where a common priest becomes impure to** bury **his paternal brother, a High Priest may not become impure** even **to** bury **his father,** then **in a case where a common priest may not become impure to** bury **his maternal brother,** who is not considered a relative with regard to impurity, **is it not logical that a High Priest may not become impure to** bury **his mother** herself?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם זָכִיתָה מֵהַדִּין, מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״אִמּוֹ״ בְּכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל? מוּפְנֶה לְהַקִּישׁ וְלָדוּן הֵימֶנּוּ גְּזֵירָה שָׁוָה: נֶאֱמַר ״אִמּוֹ״ בְּנָזִיר, וְנֶאֱמַר ״אִמּוֹ״ בְּכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל,
> **Translation:** **And if you have successfully** derived this *halakha* **from the** *a fortiori* **inference, what** is the meaning when **the verse states “his mother” with regard to a High Priest?** It is **free to** teach a novel *halakha*, and one can **compare and learn a verbal analogy from it** as follows: **It is stated “his mother” with regard to a nazirite** (Numbers 6:7), **and it is stated “his mother” with regard to a High Priest** (Leviticus 21:11).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה ״אִמּוֹ״ הָאָמוּר בְּנָזִיר — בְּמוֹתָם אֵינוֹ מִיטַּמֵּא אֲבָל מִיטַּמֵּא הוּא לְנִגְעָם וּלְזִיבָתָם, אַף ״אִמּוֹ״ הָאָמוּר בְּכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל — בְּמוֹתָם אֵינוֹ מִיטַּמֵּא אֲבָל מִיטַּמֵּא לְנִגְעָם וּלְזִיבָתָם.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: **Just as** the phrase **“his mother” stated with regard to a nazirite** teaches that **in** his parents’ **death he may not become impure** to bury them **but he may become impure for their leprosy and for their emission of a *zav*,** as a nazirite is not prohibited from contracting these impurities, **so too,** the phrase **“his mother” stated with regard to a High Priest** means that **in** his parents’ **death he may not become impure, but he may become impure for their leprosy and for their emission of a *zav*.**

The *Gemara* now turns to *le'imo* — "to his mother" — and brings a *braisa* in the name of *Rebbi* that opens with the *nazir*. A *nazir* is told *lo yitama lahem bemotam* — he shall not become *tamei* to them in their deaths. The word *bemotam* — "in their deaths" — is significant: it specifies *tumat meit* as the prohibited *tumah*, thereby implying that other types of *tumah* — such as *tzara'at* or *zivah* — do not fall under the *nazir*'s prohibition. *Gabi nazir bemotam eino mitamei aval mitamei hu lenigam ulezivatam* — for a *nazir*, the prohibition is specifically against becoming *tamei* through death-related *tumah*; contact with a *metzora* or a *zav* is not prohibited.

This might seem obvious, since the *nazir*'s prohibition is textually framed around corpse-*tumah*. But it sets up the crucial *gezeira shava*. The *braisa* then asks *bekohen gadol minalan* — how do we know the same limitation applies to a *kohen gadol*? This question has bite precisely because the *kohen gadol*'s *pesukim* do not include the word *bemotam*, so we cannot derive the limitation for him directly from his own text. The *braisa* notes that one approach would be a *kal vachomer*: a *kohen hedyot* becomes *tamei* to a half-brother on his father's side but not to a half-brother on his mother's side, which demonstrates that paternal lineage carries more halachic weight than maternal lineage. If a *kohen gadol* cannot become *tamei* even to his father, then all the more so he cannot become *tamei* to his mother — and yet the *pasuk* says *imo* explicitly. The *braisa* therefore presses: *im zachito min hadin mah talmud lomar imo* — if you could already derive the prohibition regarding his mother through a *kal vachomer*, why did the *Torah* need to state *imo* at all? The answer is that the extra word *imo* is not there to establish the prohibition but to teach something new about its scope — a question the *gemara* resolves through the *gezeira shava* with the *nazir* discussed in the following section.

### Applied to Kohen Gadol

With respect to the *kohen gadol*, the question is different. The *pesukim* say *le'aviv u'le'imo lo yitama* — not to his father and mother shall he become *tamei*. These words do not specify *bemotam*; they simply say he shall not become *tamei*. One might therefore think the prohibition encompasses any *tumah* his father or mother might have — whether *tumat meit*, *tzara'at*, or *zivah*. *Bekohen gadol minalan* — how do we know that for a *kohen gadol* as well, the restriction is limited to *tumat meit*?

The answer is a *gezeira shava*: *ne'emar imo benazir vene'emar imo bekohen gadol* — the word *imo* ("his mother") appears both by the *nazir* and by the *kohen gadol*. This verbal parallel licenses a *halachic* connection: *mah imo ha'amur benazir bemotam eino mitamei aval mitamei hu lenigam ulezivatam* — just as by the *nazir*, the prohibition is limited to *tumat meit* (as established by *bemotam*), *af imo ha'amur bekohen gadol bemotam eino mitamei aval mitamei hu lenigam ulezivatam* — so too by the *kohen gadol*, the restriction is only against *tumat meit*, and contact with a *metzora* or *zav* is not prohibited.

### Redundancy Analysis

The overall structure of the exposition is now complete. Since *al kol nafshot met lo yavo* must refer to relatives (as argued above), both *le'aviv* and *le'imo* are redundant within the *pesuk*. Each extra phrase comes to teach a distinct *halacha*: *le'aviv* teaches that there is a case — *meit mitzvah* — where even a *kohen gadol* becomes *tamei*; and *le'imo*, through the *gezeira shava* with the *nazir*, teaches that the *kohen gadol*'s prohibition is restricted to *tumat meit* and does not extend to other forms of *tumah*. These are not references to the *kohen gadol*'s specific father or mother — once *al kol nafshot met* encompasses all relatives, *le'aviv* and *le'imo* become free to carry new independent *halachic* content, as extra words in the *Torah* are never without purpose.
