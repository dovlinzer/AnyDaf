## Nechasim Hamiyuchadim



### Mishnah Introduction

The *mishnah* introduces the concept of *nechasim hamiyuchadim* — designated or assigned property — as one of the conditions for liability in *nezikin*. The first order of business is to determine what this phrase excludes. Picking up approximately twelve lines down on 13b, the *gemara* asks: *lemautei mai* — what does it come to exclude?

### Two Oxen Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נְכָסִים הַמְיוּחָדִין. לְמַעוֹטֵי מַאי? אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: לְמַעוֹטֵי זֶה אוֹמֵר ״שׁוֹרְךָ הִזִּיק״, וְזֶה אוֹמֵר ״שׁוֹרְךָ הִזִּיק״.
> **Translation:** The mishna continues: One is liable only for **assigned property.** The Gemara asks: This clause serves **to exclude what? Rav Yehuda said:** This clause serves **to exclude** a case in which an animal is injured but it is unclear which of two oxen, owned by different people, caused the damage. The owner of **this** ox **says** to the owner of the other: **Your ox caused** the **damage. And** the owner of **that** ox **says** to the owner of the other: **Your ox caused** the **damage.** Since it cannot be proven which ox actually caused the damage, neither owner is liable. The intention of the mishna is that one is liable only where the belligerent ox is assigned to, i.e., is known to be the property of, a specific individual.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא תָּנֵי לְקַמַּן: הָיוּ שְׁנַיִם רוֹדְפִין אַחַר אֶחָד, זֶה אוֹמֵר: ״שׁוֹרְךָ הִזִּיק״, וְזֶה אוֹמֵר: ״שׁוֹרְךָ הִזִּיק״ – שְׁנֵיהֶם פְּטוּרִין! תָּנֵי וַהֲדַר מְפָרֵשׁ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Isn’t that** explicitly **taught** in the mishna **below** (35a): If **two** oxen **were** seen **pursuing a single** ox, and then the single ox is found to be injured, and the owner of **this** ox **says** to the owner of the other: **Your ox caused** the **damage.** And the owner of **that** ox **says** to the owner of the other: **Your ox caused** the **damage.** In such a case, **both of them are exempt** since it is uncertain which one is guilty. The Gemara answers: The *tanna* **teaches** this as a principle in the mishna here **and then explains** it in greater detail in the mishna below.

*Amar Rav Yehuda lemautei zeh omer shorcha hezik vezeh omer shorcha hezik* — Rav Yehuda explains that *nechasim hamiyuchadim* comes to exclude the case where it is not clear whose ox caused the damage. Two oxen were running after a third ox, the third was gored, and now Reuven and Shimon are each pointing the finger at the other. The *gemara* immediately challenges this reading: that case is already taught explicitly later in the *mishnah* — *hayu shenayim rodfim achar echad*, two oxen chasing one, *zeh omer shorcha hezik vezeh omer shorcha hezik*, and the ruling is *shenayim peturin*, both are exempt, by the classic principle of *hamotzi mechavero alav haraya*. The *gemara* resolves this by saying the *mishnah* first states the abstract principle and then later makes it explicit — *tani ve'ader mefaresh*. That is one reading of the *mishnah*.

### Hefker Ox Analysis

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּמַתְנִיתָא תָּנָא: פְּרָט לְנִכְסֵי הֶפְקֵר. הֵיכִי דָמֵי? אִילֵּימָא דִּנְגַח תּוֹרָא דִידַן לְתוֹרָא דְהֶפְקֵר – מַאן תָּבַע לֵיהּ? אֶלָּא דִּנְגַח תּוֹרָא דְהֶפְקֵר לְתוֹרָא דִידַן –
> **Translation:** **It was taught in a *baraita*** in explanation of the mishna that the term assigned property serves **to exclude ownerless** property. The Gemara clarifies: **What are the circumstances** in which the mishna teaches that one is exempt? **If we say** it is referring to a case **where an ox belonging to us,** i.e., to a Jew, **gored an ownerless ox,** it is obvious that the owner bears no liability, as **who is claiming** damages **from him? Rather,** it must be referring to a case **where an ownerless ox gored an ox belonging to us,** and it teaches that the injured party does not have a right to damages.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֵיזִיל וְלַיְתֵיהּ! בְּשֶׁקָּדַם וְזָכָה בּוֹ אַחֵר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: What is the point of this *halakha*? If the injured party wishes to recover his losses, **let him go and take** the belligerent ox for himself, since it is currently ownerless. The Gemara explains: The mishna teaches that the injured party does not have any right to the ox, in order to teach that in a case **where another** person **preceded him and acquired it,** the injured party has no claim to it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רָבִינָא אָמַר: לְמַעוֹטֵי נָגַח וְאַחַר כָּךְ הִקְדִּישׁ; נָגַח וְאַחַר כָּךְ הִפְקִיר.
> **Translation:** **Ravina said** an alternative interpretation of the mishna: The term assigned property serves **to exclude** a case in which an ox **gored and subsequently** its owner **consecrated** it, or where **it gored and subsequently** its owner **declared** it **ownerless.** Since the owners did not own the ox at the time the case was brought before the court, they are exempt from paying. Ravina explains that this is the intention of the mishna: That one is liable only when the property that caused the damage was owned by a responsible party both when it caused the damage and when it was tried. In this case, at the time of the court hearing it was not owned by a responsible party, as it was either consecrated or ownerless.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תַּנְיָא נָמֵי הָכִי, יָתֵר עַל כֵּן אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: אֲפִילּוּ נָגַח וְאַחַר כָּךְ הִקְדִּישׁ, נָגַח וְאַחַר כָּךְ הִפְקִיר – פָּטוּר, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְהוּעַד בִּבְעָלָיו וְהֵמִית אִישׁ וְגוֹ׳״ – עַד שֶׁתְּהֵא מִיתָה וְהַעֲמָדָה בַּדִּין שָׁוִין כְּאֶחָד.
> **Translation:** **It is also taught** in a *baraita*: The *halakha* is that an ox that kills a person is stoned to death. The mishna states (44b) that this does not apply to an ox consecrated to the Temple or an ownerless ox. **Moreover, Rabbi Yehuda said: Even** if an ox **gored and** its owner **subsequently consecrated** it to the Temple, or if an ox **gored and** its owner **subsequently declared** it **ownerless,** the owner **is exempt** from liability, **as it is stated:** “And if the ox was wont to gore in time past, **and had been forewarned with its owner,** and he had not guarded it in, **and it killed a man** or a woman; the ox shall be stoned, and also its owner shall be put to death” (Exodus 21:29). The repetition of the reference to the owner at the beginning and end of the verse indicates that the ox is not stoned **unless** the ox’s status as the owner’s property at the time of the **death** of the victim **and** at the time of the owner’s **standing trial is the same,** i.e., the ox is owned by a responsible party for both events.

A *baraita* offers a second interpretation: *prat lenichsei hefker* — *nechasim hamiyuchadim* excludes ownerless property. The *gemara* then probes what case this refers to.

If our ox gored a *hefker* ox — *denagach tora didan letora dehefker* — obviously one is exempt. There is no plaintiff; *man tavei lei*, who would even bring the matter to *beis din*? That is trivial.

The more interesting case is the reverse: an ownerless ox gored one of ours. One might think the solution is simple — just go ahead and take possession of the ox, since it is ownerless. Whatever damage it did to you, you can simply recoup by claiming the *hefker* animal. But the *gemara* refines the case: *beshekadam vezacha bo acher* — what if someone else, say Reuven, got there first and took possession of the *hefker* ox before you could? Now, one might reason that since the *halacha* in a case of a *tam* ox (one that has not gored before) is that payment comes from the body of the ox itself, perhaps you automatically acquired a partial ownership in that ox from the moment it did the damage — a lien, as it were, of one hundred dollars' worth of an ox that caused you a hundred dollars of damage.

The *chiddush* is that this is not so. Without an owner, no liability is triggered at all. You do not acquire any partial ownership in an ox that gored yours if that ox had no owner at the time of the goring. This is not merely an arbitrary exemption. The entire framework of *nezikin* presupposes a person who is liable — either because of negligence or because their property caused the damage. It does not operate as some metaphysical mechanism by which the damaging animal itself becomes partially owned by the victim. Without an owner, the process never begins.

### Ravina's Reading

Ravina offers an even more striking *chiddush* as a third reading of *nechasim hamiyuchadim*: *nagach ve'achar kach hikdish, nagach ve'achar kach hefkir* — my ox gored, and after it gored and I am already liable, I sanctify the ox to the *beis hamikdash* (*hekdesh*), or I renounce ownership (*mafkir*), and thereby escape payment. The idea that one can exit an existing liability through a post-damage transfer of ownership is quite a remarkable claim.

A *baraita* confirms this position: *af illu nagach ve'achar kach hikdish nagach ve'achar kach hefkir patur* — even if after the goring you sanctify or renounce ownership, you are exempt.

## Kofer & Ownership Trnsfer



### Rav Yehuda on Kofer

The *baraita* cited by Ravina deals specifically with the *kofer* payment — the atonement payment owed when a *shor ha-mu'ad* kills a person. Rav Yehuda teaches that if after the ox kills someone the owner sanctifies or renounces the ox, he is exempt from paying *kofer*. The *pasuk* the *gemara* invokes is *vehu'ad beva'alav vehameis ish* — the ox was warned against the owner and it killed a man or a woman — followed by *ha-shor yisakeil*, the ox shall be stoned.

### Same Status Requirement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּגְמַר הַדִּין לָא בָּעֵינַן?! הָא ״הַשּׁוֹר יִסָּקֵל״ – בִּגְמַר דִּין הוּא דִּכְתִיב!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But do we not** also **require** that the ox’s status be the same at the time of the **verdict**? **Isn’t** the phrase: **“The ox shall be stoned” written with regard to** the time of the **verdict?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אֵימָא: עַד שֶׁתְּהֵא מִיתָה וְהַעֲמָדָה בְּדִין וּגְמַר דִּין שָׁוִין כְּאֶחָד.
> **Translation:** **Rather, say** that the owner of the ox is exempt **unless** the ox’s status as the owner’s property at the time of the **death** of the victim and at the time of the owner’s **standing trial and at the verdict is the same,** i.e., the ox is owned by a responsible party for all three events.

The *derasha* is *ad she-tehei misa ve-ha'amada ba-din shavin* — the killing and the adjudication must occur in the same legal context, with the same status of ownership. More precisely, the *gemara* clarifies: from the moment of the killing through the *gmar din* — the issuance of the final verdict — the ox must remain in the same ownership status. *Shavin ke-echad*: the two events must be aligned. If I transfer the ox to *hefker* or *hekdesh* before the *ha'amada ba-din*, I have transferred it into a status that would have exempted me from liability had the ox been in that status at the time of the goring. And so I am off the hook.

The *gemara* raises an obvious question: does it suffice to transfer to *hefker* or *hekdesh* and then reclaim? That is left as an open question. The exemption appears to apply when the ox is in that exempt status at the moment of *ha'amada ba-din*, but whether reclaiming it afterwards reimposes liability is not resolved.

### Extension Beyond Kofer

What is striking is that the *gemara* does not explicitly derive from any *pasuk* how the *kofer* rule — with its requirement that ownership status remain consistent from goring through adjudication — extends to ordinary cases of property damage. There is no textual bridge. And the extension produces a profound injustice: my ox causes a thousand dollars of damage; the ox is worth a hundred dollars; I simply *mafkir* it and escape paying a thousand dollars in damages.

Some *acharonim* suggest, and this resolution is compelling even if the *gemara* does not make it explicit, that the *gemara* may intend to limit this rule to *keren tam* — the case of an unhabituated ox goring for the first three times, where only half-damages are paid.

### Knas vs. Monetary Obliga

Two reasons converge to make this rule work specifically for *keren tam*. First, there is a debate — to be addressed later — as to whether half-damages for *tam* is a *knas* (a fine) rather than genuine compensation (*mamon*). If it is a *knas*, then the liability does not arise automatically at the moment of the event. In ordinary cases of damage — where my ox tramples your produce or falls into your pit — I owe you money from the instant the damage occurs; *beis din* merely clarifies what already exists. A *knas*, by contrast, is imposed by *beis din*; I do not owe anything until the court imposes it. Accordingly, if the ox is in a legally exempt status — *hefker* or *hekdesh* — at the time *beis din* adjudicates, *beis din* cannot impose the fine because the ox's status has changed.

Second, and just as importantly, this limitation dissolves the injustice problem. When my ox gores for the first three times, my liability is capped at half-damages and is further limited by the value of the ox itself. I never pay more than what the ox is worth. So *mafkir*-ing the ox does not save me any money — indeed, I may lose by renouncing it. The perverse incentive to escape a large damage obligation by abandoning a small ox does not arise if my liability was already capped at the ox's value.

By contrast, applying this rule to full compensatory damages — where an ox worth a hundred dollars could shield me from a thousand-dollar judgment — would be both technically incoherent and deeply unjust. The *gemara* leaves this gap unexplained, and this is one of those moments where the reasoning works compellingly only when we confine the principle to its natural domain. As noted, *ein danin dinei kenasos be-Vavel* — courts in Babylonia do not adjudicate *kenas* matters at all — which adds another dimension to why the *beis din* must be involved for the fine to take effect.

## Own Domain Exemption



### Tosafot's Principle

The *mishnah* states *chutz me-reshut ha-meyuchedet la-mazik* — the damager is exempt when the damage occurs in his own domain. Tosafot articulates this as a broad principle: there is no liability for damage that occurs within the damager's own property when the victim's animal or produce entered without permission.

### Risk & Freedom Logic

The logic is straightforward. If your ox wanders into my property without permission, I can say to you: *torcha bi-reshuti mai ba'ei* — what is your ox doing on my property? You came here of your own accord. I have the right to do whatever I wish on my own property. I am not obligated to police my own animals in my own space to protect a trespasser. You assumed the risk by entering.

This is worth pausing over. The exemption is not merely technical — it reflects a deep principle that a property owner retains freedom of action within his domain, and that a victim who enters without authorization cannot impose liability on the property owner for events that occur there. The *rishonim* make explicit what the *gemara* does not spell out: this applies only when the victim entered without permission. If the damager invited the victim in, the calculus shifts entirely — the invitation removes the predicate for the exemption.

## Shared Domain Debate



### Mishnah Reading

The *mishnah* then presents what turns out to be a genuinely ambiguous line. After establishing the exemption for the damager's own domain, the *mishnah* says *ve-reshut ha-nizak ve-ha-mazik* — the domain of both the damagee and the damager — and then concludes *ke-she-hizik chav ha-mazik le-shalem* — when damage occurs, the damager is obligated to pay.

The question is how to parse this. Is *ve-reshut ha-nizak ve-ha-mazik* part of the *chutz* clause — meaning one is also exempt in a shared domain — and the final phrase is simply restating the general principle of liability? Or does the sentence structure mean that in a shared domain, unlike in the damager's own domain, one is liable to pay?

This ambiguity generates a significant *machloket* among the *amora'im*.

### Rav Chisda's View

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חוּץ מֵרְשׁוּת הַמְיוּחֶדֶת לַמַּזִּיק. דְּאָמַר לֵיהּ: תּוֹרָךְ בִּרְשׁוּתִי מַאי בָּעֵי?
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: One is liable for damage caused in any place **except for a domain designated** exclusively **for** the use of **the one responsible for the damage.** The Gemara explains the reason for this: This is **because** the one liable for the damage **can say to** the injured party: **What did your ox want in my domain?** One does not have to guard his ox from causing damage within his own property, since another person’s animal has no right to be there.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּרְשׁוּת הַנִּיזָּק וְהַמַּזִּיק.
> **Translation:** The mishna continues: **And** one is liable for damage caused in **a domain** designated for the joint use **of the injured** party **and the one liable for the damage.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא אָמַר אֲבִימִי: חֲצַר הַשּׁוּתָּפִין – חַיָּיב בָּהּ עַל הַשֵּׁן וְעַל הָרֶגֶל. וְהָכִי קָאָמַר: ״חוּץ מֵרְשׁוּת הַמְיוּחֶדֶת לַמַּזִּיק״ – דְּפָטוּר; ״וּרְשׁוּת הַנִּיזָּק וְהַמַּזִּיק – כְּשֶׁהִזִּיק חָב הַמַּזִּיק״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites a dispute concerning this case that results in two different ways of interpreting the mishna: **Rav Ḥisda says** that **Avimi says:** With regard to **a courtyard** designated for **the** joint use of two **partners,** either one of them is **liable** for damage caused by the ox of one partner to the property of the other partner while **in it,** both **for the** category of **Eating and for the** category of **Trampling. And** accordingly, **this is** what the mishna **is saying:** One is liable for damage caused in any place **except for a domain designated** exclusively **for** the use of **the one responsible for the damage, as** he is **exempt** there; **but** in **a domain** designated for the joint use **of the injured** party **and the one responsible for the damage, when he causes damage, the one who is responsible for the damage is obligated** to pay damages. The statement in the mishna concerning a domain designated for joint use is the beginning of the following clause and introduces a case in which one is liable for damage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר אָמַר: פָּטוּר עַל הַשֵּׁן וְעַל הָרֶגֶל. וְהָכִי קָאָמַר: ״חוּץ מֵרְשׁוּת הַמְיוּחֶדֶת לַמַּזִּיק, וּרְשׁוּת הַנִּיזָּק וְהַמַּזִּיק״ נָמֵי – פָּטוּר; ״וּכְשֶׁהִזִּיק חָב הַמַּזִּיק״ – לְאֵתוֹיֵי קֶרֶן.
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Elazar says:** In a courtyard designated for joint use, one is **exempt for** one’s ox causing damage to the property of his partner in **the** category of **Eating, and for** one’s ox causing damage to the property of his partner in **the** category of **Trampling. And** accordingly, **this is** what the mishna **is saying:** One is liable for damage caused in any place **except for a domain designated** exclusively **for** the use of **the one responsible for the damage, and** one is **also exempt** for damage done in **a domain** designated for the joint use **of the injured** party **and the one liable for the damage.** The statement in the mishna about a domain designated for joint use is a continuation of the previous clause and adds an additional case in which one is exempt. **And** the following clause: **When one causes damage, the one liable for the damage is obligated** to pay damages, serves **to add** the fact that one is liable for damage in the framework of the category of **Goring,** which was not previously mentioned in the mishna.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנִיחָא לִשְׁמוּאֵל, אֶלָּא לְרַב דְּאָמַר: תְּנָא שׁוֹר וְכֹל מִילֵּי דְשׁוֹר, ״חָב הַמַּזִּיק״ לְאֵתוֹיֵי מַאי?
> **Translation:** This interpretation of the concluding clause of the mishna, as a reference to Goring, **works out well according to Shmuel,** in his dispute with Rav (3b) concerning the meaning of Ox and *Maveh* in the first mishna of this chapter. According to Shmuel they mean Trampling and Eating, respectively, and Goring has yet to be addressed. **But according to Rav, who says:** The term ox in the mishna includes damage caused by the ox **and all matters** involving damaging actions **that** are completed by **an ox,** including Goring, Eating, and Trampling, Goring is already addressed in the mishna. **What is added** by the clause: The **one liable for the damage is obligated** to pay damages?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְאֵתוֹיֵי הָא דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״כְּשֶׁהִזִּיק חָב הַמַּזִּיק״ – לְהָבִיא שׁוֹמֵר חִנָּם וְהַשּׁוֹאֵל נוֹשֵׂא שָׂכָר וְהַשּׂוֹכֵר, שֶׁהִזִּיקָה בְּהֵמָה בִּרְשׁוּתָן; תָּם – מְשַׁלֵּם חֲצִי נֶזֶק, וּמוּעָד – מְשַׁלֵּם נֶזֶק שָׁלֵם. נִפְרְצָה בַּלַּיְלָה אוֹ שֶׁפְּרָצוּהָ לִסְטִין, וְיָצְתָה וְהִזִּיקָה – פָּטוּר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: It serves **to add that which the Sages taught** in a *baraita* that elucidates the mishna’s statement. The mishna states: **When** an animal or item one is responsible to safeguard **causes damage, the one liable for the damage** caused by insufficiently safeguarding it **is obligated** to pay payments of restitution for damage. This serves **to include** the cases of **an unpaid bailee, a borrower, a paid bailee, and a renter,** in **which an animal,** e.g., an ox, **caused damage** while **in their possession.** If the ox was **innocuous,** he **pays half** of **the damages, and** if it was **forewarned,** he **pays** the **full damages.** If the ox was safely enclosed and a wall **broke open at night, or** if **bandits [*listin*] broke it open and** the ox **went out and caused damage,** he is **exempt** from liability.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר: כְּשֶׁהִזִּיק חָב הַמַּזִּיק – לְהָבִיא שׁוֹמֵר חִנָּם וְהַשּׁוֹאֵל נוֹשֵׂא שָׂכָר וְהַשּׂוֹכֵר. הֵיכִי דָּמֵי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara analyzes the *baraita*: **The Master said** that the mishna states: **When** an animal or item one is responsible to safeguard **causes damage, the one liable for the damage** caused by insufficiently safeguarding it **is obligated** to pay payments of restitution for damage. This serves **to include** the cases of **an unpaid bailee, a borrower, a paid bailee, and a renter.** The Gemara asks: **What are the circumstances?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִילֵימָא דְּאַזְּקֵיהּ תּוֹרָא דְמַשְׁאִיל לְתוֹרָא דְשׁוֹאֵל – לֵימָא לֵיהּ: אִילּוּ אַזֵּיק בְּעָלְמָא – בָּעֵית לְשַׁלּוֹמֵי אַתְּ; הַשְׁתָּא דְּאַזְּקֵיהּ לְתוֹרָא דִידָךְ, בָּעֵינָא לְשַׁלּוֹמֵי?!
> **Translation:** **If we say** that **the lender’s ox,** which was under the watch of the borrower, **injured the borrower’s ox,** and the *baraita* is teaching that the lender is liable to pay the borrower, why is this so? **Let** the lender **say to** the borrower: **If** my ox **had injured** an ox **in the world** at large, i.e., someone else’s ox, **you,** the borrower, **would be required to pay,** as you were entrusted with safeguarding it. **Now that** my ox **has injured your ox, should I be required to pay?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא דְּאַזְּקֵיהּ תּוֹרָא דְשׁוֹאֵל לְתוֹרָא דְמַשְׁאִיל – לֵימָא לֵיהּ: אִילּוּ אִיתַּזַּק מֵעָלְמָא – בָּעֵית לְשַׁלּוֹמֵי כּוּלֵּיהּ תּוֹרָא; הַשְׁתָּא דְּאַזְּקֵיהּ תּוֹרָא דִידָךְ, פַּלְגָא נִיזְקָא הוּא דִּמְשַׁלְּמַתְּ לִי?!
> **Translation:** **Rather,** the case must be **that the borrower’s ox injured the lender’s ox,** and the *baraita* is teaching that the borrower is liable to pay. The Gemara asks: According to this interpretation, the *baraita* teaches that if the belligerent ox was innocuous, the borrower is liable to pay only half of the damages. But why? **Let** the lender **say to** the borrower: By borrowing my ox, you undertook full responsibility for it such that **if** my ox **had been injured** by an ox **from the world** at large, **you would be required to pay** me **for** the **entire** value of my **ox,** irrespective of whether the belligerent ox was considered innocuous or forewarned. **Now that** it is **your ox that injured** my ox, **should you** have to **pay** only **half** of **the damages?** This interpretation of the *baraita* is also difficult.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְעוֹלָם דְּאַזְּקֵיהּ תּוֹרָא דְמַשְׁאִיל לְתוֹרָא דְשׁוֹאֵל, וְהָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן – שֶׁקִּבֵּל עָלָיו שְׁמִירַת גּוּפוֹ,
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: **Actually,** the case is **where the lender’s ox injured the borrower’s ox, and with what are we dealing here?** This is a case **where** the borrower **undertook** the responsibility of **safeguarding the body of** the lender’s ox from being injured,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלֹא קִבֵּל עָלָיו שְׁמִירַת נְזָקָיו.
> **Translation:** **but he did not undertake** the responsibility of **safeguarding** the lender’s ox to prevent it **from causing damage.** Accordingly, the lender remains responsible for his ox, and if it caused damage to the borrower’s ox the lender is liable, as in any other case in which his ox caused damage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, אֵימָא סֵיפָא: נִפְרְצָה בַּלַּיְלָה אוֹ שֶׁפְּרָצוּהָ לִסְטִים, וְיָצְתָה וְהִזִּיקָה – פָּטוּר. הָא בַּיּוֹם – חַיָּיב? הָא לֹא קִבֵּל עָלָיו שְׁמִירַת נְזָקָיו!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If that is so, say,** and try to explain accordingly, **the latter clause** of the *baraita*: If the ox was safely enclosed and a wall **broke open at night, or** if **bandits broke it open and** the ox **went out and caused damage,** the bailee is **exempt** from liability. The Gemara infers: If it broke open **during the day** and then caused damage, he is **liable.** The Gemara asks: According to the interpretation offered in explanation of the previous clause, the *baraita* concerns a case in which one borrowed an ox but did not undertake the responsibility to prevent it from causing damage. If so, how can the latter clause imply that there are cases in which he is liable? If **he did not undertake** the responsibility of **safeguarding** the lender’s ox to prevent it **from causing damage,** he should never be liable for the damage that it caused.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכִי קָאָמַר: אִם קִבֵּל עָלָיו שְׁמִירַת נְזָקָיו – חַיָּיב. נִפְרְצָה בַּלַּיְלָה אוֹ שֶׁפְּרָצוּהָ לִסְטִים, וְיָצְתָה וְהִזִּיקָה – פָּטוּר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers that the latter clause of the *baraita* concerns a different case than that of the previous clause. **This** is what the latter clause of the *baraita* **is saying: If,** by contrast, the borrower **did undertake** the responsibility of **safeguarding** the lender’s ox to prevent it **from causing damage,** if it causes damage, the borrower is **liable.** But if the ox was safely enclosed and the wall enclosing it **broke open at night, or** if **bandits broke it open and** the ox **went out and caused damage,** he is **exempt.**

Rav Chisda citing Avimi says: *chatzar hashtufin chayav bah al ha-shen ve-al ha-regel* — in a shared domain, one is liable for *shen* and *regel*. It is worth noting why the debate about a shared domain is confined to *shen* and *regel* and does not extend to *keren*. By *keren*, one is liable in *reshut ha-rabim*; the only exemption is when the victim's animal entered the damager's exclusive domain without permission. A shared domain by definition is one where the victim has a right to be — so there is no question one is liable for *keren* in a shared domain. The interesting question is only *shen* and *regel*.

For *shen* and *regel*, the Torah establishes liability in *sadeh acher* — the other person's field — and exemption in *reshut ha-rabim*. A shared domain sits between these poles. From one angle: the exemption in *reshut ha-rabim* is a special case driven by the need to preserve freedom of use of public thoroughfares and by *pshiat ha-nizak* (the victim's own negligence in placing property in a high-traffic area); anything that is not a *reshut ha-rabim* should generate liability, including a shared domain. From the other angle: liability in *shen* and *regel* is specifically tethered to *sadeh acher*; without the quality of "another's domain," there is no liability, and a shared domain where both parties have rights is not *sadeh acher*.

Rav Chisda adopts the first reading: a shared domain is not a *reshut ha-rabim*, so one is liable. The *mishnah*'s final phrase — *ke-she-hizik chav ha-mazik le-shalem* — according to Rav Chisda is the *mishnah*'s affirmative ruling about a shared domain. According to this reading, *ve-reshut ha-nizak ve-ha-mazik* establishes that in a shared domain the damager pays. And the general principle at the end — *ke-she-hizik chav ha-mazik* — is read, according to Shmuel, as coming to include *keren*, while according to Rav, who understands *shor* in the first *mishnah* to already include *keren*, it comes to include the case of a *shomer* — a watchman — whose animal causes damage, a topic the *gemara* develops in a substantial digression.

The *gemara* addresses the *shomer* case at length. The *baraita* states that when an animal under a *shomer*'s care does damage in his domain, a *tam* pays half and a *mu'ad* pays full; but if the animal broke out at night or bandits breached the fence — cases of *onnes* — the *shomer* is exempt. The *chiddush* is not merely that a *shomer* bears responsibility for damage the animal he is watching causes to third parties, which is intuitive enough. The *gemara* pushes into harder cases.

If the ox the *shomer* was watching gored the *shomer*'s own ox, why should the original owner pay? The *shomer* would ordinarily pay for damage the ox causes to third parties, so why should the original owner compensate the *shomer* for damage his own watched ox inflicted on the *shomer*? The reverse case is equally puzzling: if the *shomer*'s own ox gored the ox he was watching, one might think he owes full compensation as a *shomer* — since a *shomer* bears full liability for damage to the item he is guarding, regardless of the cause — not merely half as a *mazik*. The *gemara* resolves these difficulties by introducing a case of stipulation: the *shomer* accepted responsibility for guarding the animal from harm (*shmiras gufo*) but explicitly did not accept responsibility for damage the animal causes (*shmiras nizko*). In such a case, if the watched ox — with no liability resting on the *shomer* for its actions — gores the *shomer*'s own ox, the original owner remains liable. And conversely, the *shomer*'s ox goring the watched ox is addressed by distinguishing between *shomer* liability and *mazik* liability based on what was stipulated.

### Rabbi Elazar's View

Rabbi Elazar takes the opposite position: *patur al ha-shen ve-al ha-regel* — in a shared domain, one is exempt from *shen* and *regel*. According to Rabbi Elazar's reading, *chutz me-reshut ha-meyuchedet la-mazik ve-reshut ha-nizak ve-ha-mazik nami patur* — the exemption clause encompasses both the damager's exclusive domain and a shared domain. The only context in which one is liable for *shen* and *regel* is *sadeh acher* — the victim's domain exclusively. The final phrase *ke-she-hizik chav ha-mazik* then, according to Rabbi Elazar, does not refer to a shared domain at all but is a separate clause.

Now, this raises an immediate question: what is that final clause actually adding? If, according to Rabbi Elazar, the *mishnah* has already told us that liability for *shen* and *regel* is confined to *sadeh acher*, and we already know from elsewhere that one is liable for *keren*, why does the *mishnah* need to append the general statement that when one's animal causes damage the damager must pay? The *gemara* explains that it comes to include *keren* — that is, to make explicit that even in a shared domain, where one is exempt from *shen* and *regel*, liability for *keren* nonetheless applies. This works neatly with Shmuel's reading, since Shmuel holds that *shor* in the opening *mishnah* referred only to *shen* and *regel*, so the final line is needed to bring *keren* into the picture. According to Rav, however, who holds that *shor* in the first *mishnah* already encompassed *keren* as well, the final line cannot be adding *keren* — it is already there. The *gemara* therefore explains that according to Rav, the final line comes to include the case of a *shomer* — a watchman whose animal causes damage — a topic the *gemara* then develops at length before returning to the central debate about a shared domain.

### Baraitot Contradiction

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִינִי?! וְהָא תָּנֵי רַב יוֹסֵף: חֲצַר הַשּׁוּתָּפִים, וְהַפּוּנְדָּק – חַיָּיב בָּהֶן עַל הַשֵּׁן וְעַל הָרֶגֶל. תְּיוּבְתָּא דְּרַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר!
> **Translation:** The Gemara returns to its prior discussion of the mishna’s statement concerning property designated for the joint use of the injured party and the one liable for the damage. The Gemara cited Rabbi Elazar’s opinion that each partner is exempt from liability for any damage that falls within the categories of Trampling or Eating that their ox caused to the other partner’s property. The Gemara challenges his opinion: **Is that so? But didn’t Rav Yosef teach** in a *baraita*: **In a courtyard** designated for the joint use **of partners, or** in **an inn [*pundak*],** one is **liable for Eating and for Trampling** caused by an ox of one partner to the property of the other. It would appear that this *baraita* is **a conclusive refutation of** the opinion of **Rabbi Elazar.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לָךְ רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: וְתִסְבְּרַאּ?! מַתְנְיָתָא מִי לָא פְּלִיגִי? וְהָתַנְיָא, אַרְבָּעָה כְּלָלוֹת הָיָה רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן אֶלְעָזָר אוֹמֵר בְּנִזָּקִין: כֹּל שֶׁהוּא רְשׁוּת לַנִּיזָּק וְלֹא לַמַּזִּיק – חַיָּיב בַּכֹּל. דְּמַזִּיק וְלֹא לַנִּיזָּק – פָּטוּר מִכֹּל.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Rabbi Elazar** could have **said to you: But** how can **you understand** that this *baraita* provides a refutation of my opinion? **Are there not *baraitot* that disagree** with regard to this issue? **And it is taught** in accordance with my opinion in a *baraita* in the *Tosefta* (1:9): **Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar would state four principles with regard to** the liability incurred based on where the **damage** occurs. With regard to **any** location **that is** the **property of the injured** party **and** is **not** the property **of the one liable for the damage,** the owner of an animal that causes damage is **liable for all** the damage caused there. If a place is the property **of the one liable for the damage and** is **not** the property **of the injured** party, the owner of an animal that causes damage is **exempt for all** the damage caused there.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָזֶה וְלָזֶה, כְּגוֹן חֲצַר הַשּׁוּתָּפִים וְהַבִּקְעָה – פָּטוּר בָּהּ עַל הַשֵּׁן וְעַל הָרֶגֶל; עַל הַנְּגִיחָה וְעַל הַנְּגִיפָה וְעַל הַנְּשִׁיכָה וְעַל הָרְבִיצָה וְעַל הַבְּעִיטָה – תָּם מְשַׁלֵּם חֲצִי נֶזֶק, מוּעָד מְשַׁלֵּם נֶזֶק שָׁלֵם.
> **Translation:** If a place is the property **of** both **this** one **and that** one, **e.g., a courtyard** designated for the joint use **of partners or** a field in **the valley** that is used by the public, the owner of an animal that causes damage is **exempt for Eating and for Trampling** performed by his animal **in** that place. But **for goring, and for pushing, and for biting, and for crouching** upon items in order to damage them, **and for kicking,** which are all subcategories of the primary category of Goring, if the ox is **innocuous,** the owner **pays half** the value of **the damage, and** if it is **forewarned, he pays** the **full** cost of the **damage.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֹא לָזֶה וְלֹא לָזֶה, כְּגוֹן חָצֵר שֶׁאֵינוֹ שֶׁל שְׁנֵיהֶם – חַיָּיב בּוֹ עַל הַשֵּׁן וְעַל הָרֶגֶל; עַל הַנְּגִיחָה וְעַל הַנְּשִׁיכָה וְעַל הַנְּגִיפָה וְעַל הָרְבִיצָה וְעַל הַבְּעִיטָה – תָּם מְשַׁלֵּם חֲצִי נֶזֶק, מוּעָד מְשַׁלֵּם נֶזֶק שָׁלֵם.
> **Translation:** If a place where the damage occurs is the property **of neither this** one **nor that** one, **e.g., a courtyard that is not** owned by either **of the two of them,** the owner of an animal that causes damage is **liable for Eating and for Trampling** performed by his animal **in** that place. But **for goring, and for pushing, and for biting, and for crouching** upon items in order to damage them, **and for kicking,** which are all subcategories of the primary category of Goring, if the ox is **innocuous,** the owner **pays half** the value of **the damage, and** if it is **forewarned, he pays** the **full** cost of the **damage.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָתָנֵי מִיהַת, חֲצַר הַשּׁוּתָּפִין וְהַבִּקְעָה – פָּטוּר בָּהּ עַל הַשֵּׁן וְעַל הָרֶגֶל;
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes that **in any event** the *baraita* **teaches** that in **a courtyard** designated for the joint use **of partners or** a field in **the valley** that is jointly owned, the owner of an animal that causes damage is **exempt for Eating and for Trampling** performed by his animal **in** that place. This *baraita* supports Rabbi Elazar’s opinion.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קַשְׁיָא אַהֲדָדֵי!
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: These *baraitot* **are difficult,** as they seem to contradict **one another.**

The *gemara* now confronts a direct conflict between two *baraitot*. Rav Yosef cites a *baraita*: *chatzar hashtufin ve-hapundak chayav bahen al ha-shen ve-al ha-regel* — a shared domain and an inn, one is liable for *shen* and *regel*. This appears to be a flat refutation of Rabbi Elazar.

Rabbi Elazar responds that there is an equal and opposite *baraita*: the four categories articulated by Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar in *nezikin*. First category: it is exclusively the damagee's domain (*reshut le-nizak ve-lo le-mazik*) — liable for everything, *chayav bakol*, including *keren*. Second: exclusively the damager's domain (*le-mazik ve-lo le-nizak*) — fully exempt, *patur mikol*, because *torcha bi-reshusi mai ba'ei*. Third: a shared domain (*laze u-laze*), exemplified by *chatzar hashtufin* or a *bika* (valley) — *patur bahen al ha-shen ve-al ha-regel*, exempt from *shen* and *regel*, but liable for *keren* (half-damages for *tam*, full for *mu'ad*). Fourth: neither party's domain (*lo laze ve-lo laze*), described as a domain belonging to neither of them — *chayav al ha-shen ve-al ha-regel*, liable for *shen* and *regel*. It is worth pausing on this fourth category: at first glance it sounds like a third party's domain entirely — my ox enters Shimon's courtyard and damages Reuven's fruit, such that neither the damager nor the victim had any right to be there. And the ruling makes sense on that basis: from the ox's perspective, it has made an incursion into a space it has no right to occupy, making it functionally equivalent to *sadeh acher*, and liability for *shen* and *regel* follows. The *gemara* will eventually redefine this fourth case, but that is the natural reading at this stage.

It is also worth noting the inclusion of the *bika*, the valley, alongside *chatzar hashtufin* in the third category. A valley may not be technically owned by either party — it may be entirely ownerless — but the operative principle is that both parties have an equal right to be there. It is not a *reshut ha-rabim*, a high-traffic public thoroughfare; it is more like a *chatzer shel shnayim*, a jointly shared space where both have a legitimate claim to bring their animals, even if formal ownership is absent. That shared entitlement is what places it in the same category as the jointly owned courtyard.

This *baraita* says precisely the opposite of Rav Yosef's *baraita* regarding a shared domain: one is liable according to Rav Yosef's source, exempt according to Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar. We have an outright contradiction between *baraitot*.

## Reconciling Baraitot



### Rav Yosef's Distinction

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי תַּנְיָא הָהִיא – בְּחָצֵר מְיוּחֶדֶת לָזֶה וְלָזֶה בֵּין לְפֵירוֹת בֵּין לִשְׁווֹרִים. הָהִיא דְּרַב יוֹסֵף – בְּחָצֵר מְיוּחֶדֶת לְפֵירוֹת, וְאֵינָהּ מְיוּחֶדֶת לִשְׁווֹרִים; דִּלְגַבֵּי שֵׁן – הָוְיָא לַהּ חֲצַר הַנִּיזָּק.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: **When that** *baraita* **is taught,** it is **with regard to** a jointly owned **courtyard,** which under the terms of their partnership is **designated for** the use of both **this** one **and that** one, i.e., as partners, **for** bringing **both produce and oxen** there. Since both partners have a right to keep their oxen there, they are not liable for any damage their oxen cause in the jointly owned space. **That** *baraita* **of Rav Yosef** is taught **with regard to** a jointly owned **courtyard,** which under the terms of their partnership is **designated for** the purpose of bringing their **produce** there, **but it is not designated for** bringing **oxen** there. Accordingly, **with regard to** damage of the category of **Eating** caused by one partner’s ox to the other’s produce, **it is** equivalent to **a courtyard** owned exclusively by **the injured** party, and therefore he is liable.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דַּיְקָא נָמֵי – דְּקָתָנֵי הָכָא דּוּמְיָא דְפוּנְדָּק, וְקָתָנֵי הָתָם דּוּמְיָא דְבִקְעָה; שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes: In accordance with this explanation, the language of each of these *baraitot* is **also precise, as it teaches here,** in the *baraita* of Rav Yosef, the case of a courtyard that is **similar to an inn,** where oxen are usually not allowed to be brought, **and it teaches there,** in the second *baraita*, the case of a courtyard that is **similar to** a field in the **valley,** where oxen are usually allowed to enter. **Conclude from it** that the Gemara’s distinction between the two *baraitot* is correct.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְקֵיף לַהּ רַבִּי זֵירָא: כֵּיוָן דִּמְיוּחֶדֶת לְפֵירוֹת?! הָא בָּעֵינַן ״וּבִעֵר בִּשְׂדֵה אַחֵר״, וְלֵיכָּא!
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira objects to this** interpretation of Rav Yosef’s *baraita*: **Since** the courtyard is **designated for** both of them to keep their **produce** there, they should not be liable for damage caused by their oxen there, as in order for one to be liable **don’t we need** a case that fits the depiction of the verse: **“And it consumed in a field of another”** (Exodus 22:4), **and** since they both have the right to use the courtyard it **does not** fulfill this condition.

The *gemara* resolves the contradiction by introducing a crucial distinction: *ki tanya hi be-chatzer meyuchedet laze u-laze bein la-peiros bein li-shvarim* — the *baraita* that exempts applies to a shared domain in which both parties have full rights: the right to bring their oxen *and* the right to keep their fruit there. *Hahi de-Rav Yosef be-chatzer meyuchedet la-peiros ve-eina meyuchedet li-shvarim* — the *baraita* that creates liability applies to a domain where one has the right to bring fruit but not to bring oxen.

This is the key: what does it mean to have a "right" to be in a given domain? The *gemara* is drawing a distinction between the right to deposit fruit and the right to bring cattle. In the case of Rav Yosef, the victim has a right to have their fruit in this shared courtyard, but the damager has no right to bring their ox there. From the perspective of the ox, it has entered a space it has no right to occupy — and that is functionally equivalent to *sadeh acher*. In the case of the *baraita* that exempts, both parties have the right to bring their oxen, so the ox's presence is entirely legitimate, and there is no basis for liability in *shen* and *regel*.

The underlying principle the *gemara* is drawing out is this: the operative question is never merely whether some form of shared access exists, but specifically whether the damager's ox had a right to be there. When the ox had no right of entry — *eina meyuchedet li-shvarim* — it is *sadeh acher* in every meaningful sense, and liability follows. When the ox did have a right to be there, the damager is exempt, regardless of how the domain is otherwise characterized. A *chatzer hashutafin* that permits oxen is in this respect no different from a *reshut ha-rabim*; what matters is not ownership but the legitimacy of the animal's presence. One way to think about it: a shared courtyard can have, so to speak, bylaws — an agreement that certain things may be kept there and others may not — and it is those terms of access, not the bare fact of co-ownership, that determine liability.

The *gemara* observes that this reading is confirmed by the language of the two sources themselves: the *baraita* that rules liability spoke of a case comparable to an inn (*pundak*) — one brings luggage to an inn, not oxen; the inn grants rights over fruit and belongings, not over animals. The *baraita* that rules exempt spoke of a *bika*, a valley — a space where one has an unquestioned right to bring one's animal to graze. It is worth noting that this also explains why the *bika* was placed in the exempt category to begin with: even without formal ownership of a valley, one's ox is entirely permitted to be there, and wherever the ox is permitted, there is no liability. One is liable only in spaces the ox had no right to enter.

What the *gemara* has not yet resolved — and what becomes a *machlokes Rashi ve-Tosfos* — is the inverse case: a domain designated for oxen but not for fruit. That question the *gemara* leaves for a later point. For now, Rav Zevid presses on the reconciliation itself.

### Zevid-Abaye Exchange

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי: כֵּיוָן דְּאֵינָהּ מְיוּחֶדֶת לִשְׁווֹרִים, ״שְׂדֵה אַחֵר״ קָרֵינָא בֵּיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said to him: Since it is not designated for** them to keep **oxen** there, that is sufficient **for it to be termed “a field of another.”**

Rav Zevid challenges this reconciliation: if the victim has rights to the domain for fruit, can one really say it is *sadeh acher*? The *pasuk* requires *u-vi'er bi-sdei acher*, and here the victim has some rights. Abaye responds: *keivan de-eina meyuchedet li-shvarim, sdeh acher karinan beih* — since the domain is not designated for oxen, we call it *sadeh acher* from the ox's perspective. The victim may have a right to bring fruit there; the ox had no right to be there at all. The ox's right of entry — or lack thereof — is the operative question.

### Mixed Rights Scenario

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב אַחָא מִדִּיפְתִּי לְרָבִינָא: לֵימָא מִדְּמַתְנְיָתָא לָא פְּלִיגִי, אָמוֹרָאֵי נָמֵי לָא פְּלִיגִי?
> **Translation:** Given the distinction between the cases discussed in the *baraitot*, the Gemara reconsiders the dispute between Rav Ḥisda and Rabbi Elazar concerning the mishna’s statement about property whose use is designated for both the injured party and the one liable for the damage. **Rav Aḥa of Difti said to Ravina: Let us say** that **from** the fact **that the *baraitot* do not disagree,** as they are referring to different cases, so too, the ***amora’im*,** i.e., Rav Ḥisda and Rabbi Elazar, **also do not disagree,** as they are similarly referring to these different cases.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אִין. וְאִם תִּמְצָא לוֹמַר פְּלִיגִי – בְּקוּשְׁיָא דְּרַבִּי זֵירָא וּבְפֵירוּקָא דְאַבָּיֵי פְּלִיגִי.
> **Translation:** Ravina **said to him: Yes,** that is correct. **And** even **if you say** that **they disagree,** their dispute does not concern every case of a jointly owned courtyard, as the Gemara had assumed; rather, their dispute concerns specifically a courtyard designated for the use of their produce, but not the use of their oxen. And **they disagree whether** to accept **the difficulty** raised **by Rabbi Zeira,** that such a courtyard cannot be considered “a field of another,” **or** whether **to** accept **the resolution of Abaye,** that it can be considered “a field of another.”

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוּפָא – אַרְבָּעָה כְּלָלוֹת הָיָה רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן אֶלְעָזָר אוֹמֵר בְּנִזָּקִין: כֹּל שֶׁהוּא רְשׁוּת לַנִּיזָּק וְלֹא לַמַּזִּיק – חַיָּיב בַּכֹּל.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara returns to **the** matter **itself** and analyzes the *baraita* in the *Tosefta* cited above. It states: **Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar would state four principles with regard to** the liability incurred based on where the **damage** was caused. With regard to **any** location **that is** the **property of the injured** party **and** is **not** the property **of the one liable for the damage,** the owner of an animal that causes damage is **liable for all** the damage caused there.

With the *baraitot* reconciled, the *gemara* asks whether the *amora'im* — Rav Chisda and Rabbi Elazar — were also just talking about different scenarios and do not in fact disagree. The *gemara* answers: possibly. If we are generous, Rav Chisda was speaking about a case where only fruit rights exist (no ox rights), which is functionally *sadeh acher*, and Rabbi Elazar was speaking about a case where both ox rights and fruit rights exist, which is not *sadeh acher* — and both would agree on both cases.

Or, says the *gemara*, if we say they do genuinely disagree, their disagreement is confined to the *kushya* of Rav Zevid and the resolution of Abaye: the case where one has rights for oxen but not for fruit. One position says this is *sadeh acher* because the ox had no right there; the other says you cannot call it *sadeh acher* when the victim has some right in the domain, even if it is only a fruit right.

### Core Principle Extraction

What the *gemara* is doing conceptually throughout this entire discussion is decisive and important. The operative question is never whether a domain resembles *reshut ha-rabim* closely enough to generate an exemption. The *pasuk* does not say: exempt in *reshut ha-rabim*, liable elsewhere. The *pasuk* says *u-vi'er bi-sdei acher* — liable specifically in *sadeh acher*. Accordingly, you are liable in *shen* and *regel* only when you can define the location as *sadeh acher*. And *sadeh acher* requires, at minimum, that the ox had no right to be in that domain. Any time the ox had a right to be there, we cannot call it *sadeh acher*, and liability in *shen* and *regel* is off the table.

It is worth pausing to appreciate how narrow this definition turns out to be. The exemption is not about the character of the space in the abstract — it is about whether this ox had a right to be there. And that means even a space that is partly yours, like a shared orchard, can still be *sadeh acher* with respect to your ox if the arrangement never included grazing rights. The scenario the *gemara* flagged as genuinely disputed — where one has fruit rights but not ox rights — is strange precisely because one could say: at the end of the day, it is partly my orchard too. Yet the logic of *sadeh acher* cuts the other way. What matters is whether the ox had a right to enter, not whether the owner had any right at all. So if your ox had no right to graze there, even in a jointly held orchard, the location qualifies as *sadeh acher* with respect to that animal, and liability follows. The whole discussion is ultimately about drawing a principled line between a shared pasture, where an ox has a right to roam, and a shared orchard, where the ox may have no such right even if the owner has a stake in the property.

The *bika* is in the exempt category precisely because, even if it is technically ownerless, one's ox has a right to be there — it is the kind of space where animals graze freely. The *pundak* is in the liable category because, even though one may have rights to store property there, one does not have a right to bring an ox into an inn.

## Four Categories Analysis



### Damagee's Domain Only

Returning to the *baraita* of Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar, the *gemara* now unpacks each of the four categories in detail. The first category — *kol she-reshut le-nizak ve-lo le-mazik*, the domain is exclusively the damagee's — generates liability for everything: *chayav bakol*. The *gemara* emphasizes that the *baraita* does not say *al ha-kol* (for all the types of damages) but *bakol* (fully, completely). This reads as: one pays full damages, even for *keren*. This follows Rabbi Tarfon's position: *meshuneh keren be-chatzer ha-nizak nezek shalem meshalem* — when an unusual act of *keren* occurs in the damagee's domain, full damages are owed even if the ox is a *tam*. When the ox invaded a space it had absolutely no right to enter and committed an act of damage there, the elevated circumstance justifies full payment.

### Damager's Domain Only

The second category — *le-mazik ve-lo le-nizak*, exclusively the damager's domain — is *patur mikol*, fully exempt. The victim's property entered without right; *torcha bi-reshusi mai ba'ei*, the damager is under no duty of care.

### Shared Domain Breakdown

The third category — *laze u-laze*, shared domain, exemplified by *chatzar hashtufin* and a *bika* — is *patur* for *shen* and *regel* but liable for *keren*: *tam* pays half, *mu'ad* pays full. As established above, in a shared domain where both parties have rights to bring their oxen, the ox's presence is legitimate and *sadeh acher* cannot be invoked. But *keren* — which is liable even in *reshut ha-rabim* — applies in any domain that is not exclusively the damager's, so liability for *keren* persists.

### Neither's Domain

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״עַל הַכֹּל״ לָא קָתָנֵי, אֶלָּא ״חַיָּיב בַּכֹּל״ – בְּכוּלֵּי[הּ] נֶזֶק;
> **Translation:** The Gemara infers: The *baraita* **does not teach:** One is liable **for any [*al hakkol*]** damage caused there, which would suggest that one is liable for all types of damage. **Rather,** it teaches: **One is liable for all [*bakkol*]** the damage caused, which can be translated as: **For** the **full** cost of the **damage.** This indicates that even were one’s ox considered to be innocuous and caused damage of the category of Goring, which would generally incur liability for half the value of the damage, in this case the owner is liable for the full cost of the damage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַנִּי – רַבִּי טַרְפוֹן הִיא, דְּאָמַר: מְשׁוּנֶּה קֶרֶן בַּחֲצַר הַנִּיזָּק, נֶזֶק שָׁלֵם מְשַׁלֵּם.
> **Translation:** **Whose** opinion is this? **It is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Tarfon, who says:** The *halakha* of cases of **Goring** performed by an innocuous animal, which is **atypical,** done **in the courtyard of the injured** party, is that the owner of the ox **pays** the **full** cost of the **damage** even if the ox is innocuous.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵימָא סֵיפָא: לֹא לָזֶה וְלֹא לָזֶה, כְּגוֹן חָצֵר שֶׁאֵינוֹ שֶׁל שְׁנֵיהֶם – חַיָּיב בָּהּ עַל הַשֵּׁן וְעַל הָרֶגֶל.
> **Translation:** If this is so, **say,** and try to explain accordingly, **the latter clause** of the *baraita*: If a place where the damage occurs is the property **of neither this** one **nor that** one, **e.g., a courtyard that is not** owned by either **of the two of them,** the owner of an animal that causes damage is **liable for Eating and for Trampling** performed by his animal **in** that place.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי ״לֹא לָזֶה וְלֹא לָזֶה״? אִילֵּימָא לֹא לָזֶה וְלֹא לָזֶה כְּלָל – אֶלָּא דְּאַחֵר; וְהָא בָּעֵינָא ״וּבִעֵר בִּשְׂדֵה אַחֵר״, וְלֵיכָּא!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What** does the *baraita* mean by saying the property is **of neither this** one **nor that** one? **If we say** the intention of the *baraita* is that the property is **of neither this** one **nor that** one **at all,** but **rather** it belongs **to another** person, why should either partner be liable? **But don’t I need** a case that fits the depiction of the verse: **“And it consumed in a field of another”** (Exodus 22:4), which is understood to mean that the consumed produce belongs to the owner of the field in which the act of consumption occurred; **and** since the injured party does not have any rights to this place it **does not** fulfill this condition?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא פְּשִׁיטָא, לֹא לָזֶה וְלֹא לָזֶה – אֶלָּא דְּחַד; וְקָתָנֵי סֵיפָא: תָּם מְשַׁלֵּם חֲצִי נֶזֶק, וּמוּעָד מְשַׁלֵּם נֶזֶק שָׁלֵם;
> **Translation:** **Rather, it is obvious** that the intention of the *baraita* is that the property is **of neither this** one **nor that** one together; **rather,** it belongs **to** only **one** of them, i.e., the injured party, **and the latter clause teaches:** If the ox is **innocuous,** the owner **pays half** the value of **the damage, and** if it is **forewarned, he pays** the **full** cost of the **damage.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲתָאן לְרַבָּנַן, דְּאָמְרִי: מְשׁוּנֶּה קֶרֶן בַּחֲצַר הַנִּיזָּק, חֲצִי נֶזֶק הוּא דִּמְשַׁלֵּם!
> **Translation:** If so, in the latter clause **we arrive at** the opinion of **the Rabbis, who say:** The *halakha* of cases of **Goring** performed by an innocuous animal, which is **atypical,** occurring **in the courtyard of the injured** party, is that the owner of the ox **pays half** the value of **the damage.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רֵישָׁא רַבִּי טַרְפוֹן, וְסֵיפָא רַבָּנַן?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: Could it be that **the first clause** of the *baraita* is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Tarfon and the last clause is** in accordance with the opinion of **the Rabbis?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִין; דְּהָא אֲמַר לֵיהּ שְׁמוּאֵל לְרַב יְהוּדָה: שִׁינָּנָא, שְׁבוֹק מַתְנִיתִין וְתָא אַבַּתְרַאי – רֵישָׁא רַבִּי טַרְפוֹן, וְסֵיפָא רַבָּנַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Yes,** that is **as Shmuel said to Rav Yehuda** with regard to another mishna (15b): ***Shinnana*, leave the mishna and follow after me** and my interpretation that **the first clause** of the mishna is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Tarfon and the last clause is** in accordance with the opinion of **the Rabbis.** In this *baraita* as well, the different clauses are taught in accordance with different Sages’ opinions.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רָבִינָא מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא אָמַר: כּוּלַּהּ רַבִּי טַרְפוֹן הִיא; וּמַאי לֹא לָזֶה וְלֹא לָזֶה – לֹא לָזֶה וְלֹא לָזֶה לְפֵירוֹת, אֶלָּא דְּחַד; לָזֶה וְלָזֶה לִשְׁווֹרִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara presents a different interpretation of the *baraita*: **Ravina said in the name of Rava: The entire** *baraita* **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Tarfon, and what** does it mean by saying that the property is **of neither this** one **nor that** one? The property does **not** belong both **to this** one **and that** one; **rather,** it is designated **for** the use of only **one** of them, **for** him to keep his **produce** there. Only the injured party has the right to keep his produce there. But it is designated both **for this** one **and for that** one, **for** them to keep their **oxen** there, as both have this right.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּלְגַבֵּי שֵׁן – הָוְיָא לַהּ חֲצַר הַנִּיזָּק, לְגַבֵּי קֶרֶן – הָוְיָא לַהּ רְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים.
> **Translation:** Accordingly, **with regard to** damage of the category of **Eating,** the property **is** considered to be the **courtyard of the injured** party; therefore, the one responsible for the damage is liable to pay for the damage. By contrast, **with regard to** damage of the category of **Goring,** since they both have the right to keep their oxen there, the property **is** considered to be like **a public domain,** and therefore even Rabbi Tarfon agrees that if one’s innocuous ox causes damage there one is liable for only half the value of the damage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, אַרְבָּעָה?! שְׁלֹשָׁה הָווּ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If that is so,** does the *baraita* list **four** principles? **There are** only **three.** In other words, according to the way Ravina explains the latter clause, i.e., the fourth principle, it does not teach anything that is not already known from the first three principles, as the fact that one is liable for Eating and Trampling in property designated for the use of the injured party is explicitly stated in the first principle, and the fact that one is liable for only half the cost of the damage in a case of Goring in a jointly owned courtyard is expressed in the third principle.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק:
> **Translation:** **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak said:**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שְׁלֹשָׁה כְּלָלוֹת – בְּאַרְבָּעָ[ה] מְקוֹמוֹת.
> **Translation:** There are only **three** distinct **principles,** but they are listed as four cases because they apply **in four** different **places.**

The fourth category — *lo laze ve-lo laze*, neither party's domain — initially appears to refer to a third party's domain: my ox enters Reuven's yard and eats Shimon's fruit. But the *gemara* rejects this reading: the *pasuk* of *u-vi'er bi-sdei acher* would not be satisfied, because the fruit consumed must belong to the person whose domain was entered. If my ox is in Reuven's yard eating Shimon's fruit, Shimon's property is not in Shimon's domain.

The *gemara* therefore reinterprets *lo laze ve-lo laze* not as "neither party," but as "not both" — meaning it is one person's domain, namely the damagee's. It is *lo laze* (not the damager's) *ve-lo laze* (and not both of theirs) — it is only the damagee's. And therefore one is liable for *shen* and *regel* as in the first category.

This creates an apparent redundancy: the first and fourth categories seem identical. The *gemara* resolves this by noting that the final category carries a different ruling for *keren* — *tam* pays half and *mu'ad* pays full — which follows *rabbanan*, who hold that even in *chatzer ha-nizak* a *tam* pays only half for *keren*. The beginning of the *baraita* followed Rabbi Tarfon (who rules full payment for *keren* in *chatzer ha-nizak*), and the end follows *rabbanan*. Shmuel advised Rav Yehuda: *shevok matnitin*, do not try to reconcile the *baraita* as a unified position — the *reisha* is Rabbi Tarfon and the *seifa* is *rabbanan*.

Ravina citing Rav offers an alternative: the entire *baraita* follows Rabbi Tarfon. The fourth category is not the damagee's domain but rather a domain that is *lo laze ve-lo laze la-peiros ela de-chad, lazeh ve-lazeh li-shvarim* — neither party has exclusive rights to the fruit (only one of them does), but both have rights to bring their oxen. According to this reading, both are permitted to bring oxen, so from the perspective of *keren* it resembles *reshut ha-rabim* (the ox had every right to be there), and the *tam* pays only half. But since only one party has the right to keep fruit there, from the perspective of *shen* it constitutes *chatzer ha-nizak*, and the damager is fully liable.

## Fruit vs. Ox Rights



### Mixed Rights Setup

This brings us to the final and most complex scenario — the one the *gemara* did not explicitly adjudicate but which generates a *machloket* between Rashi and Tosafot. The case, as read by Rashi from the *girsa* of Ravina citing Rav: both parties have rights to bring their oxen to this domain, but only the damagee (not the damager) has the right to keep fruit there. My ox therefore enters a space where it has every right to be — but it eats your fruit, which is in a space where you have a right to keep it and I do not.

### Rashi's Approach

According to Rashi, this scenario splits the liability. With respect to *shen* — the eating of the fruit — it is considered *chatzer ha-nizak*, the damagee's domain. Even though my ox had a right to be there, the absence of my right to keep fruit there means it is, to that degree, *sadeh acher*: the domain is "another's" with respect to what was consumed. Rashi thus holds that *sadeh acher* is satisfied whenever any element of the domain is exclusively the other person's — it need not be that the ox had no right to be there; it is enough that I had no right to the fruit that was consumed. Accordingly, the damager pays fully for *shen* and *regel*.

With respect to *keren*, however — since both parties have the right to bring their oxen — it resembles *reshut ha-rabim*, and a *tam* pays only half.

### Tosafot's Objection

Tosafot rejects this. Rabbeinu Tam's reading reverses the critical variable: the relevant question for *sadeh acher* is always and only whether the ox had a right to be in the domain. If my ox had a right to be there, no element of "another's field" is present, and *shen* and *regel* cannot generate liability. For Tosafot, *sadeh acher* requires that the ox itself had no right of entry — the fruit rights of either party are secondary. Accordingly, Rabbeinu Tam reads *lo laze ve-lo laze* as: neither party has rights to bring their ox there — and then, with respect to *shen*, it is *chatzer ha-nizak*; with respect to *keren*, because both ox rights are absent, it resembles *reshut ha-rabim*.

The divide is thus fundamental. For Rashi, *sadeh acher* is a function of the rights to the property consumed: as long as the victim has a right to the fruit and the damager does not, it is sufficiently "another's domain" to generate liability in *shen*. For Tosafot, *sadeh acher* is solely a function of the ox's right of entry: if my ox had a right to be there at all, the domain cannot be called *sadeh acher*, whatever my rights to the fruit. This sharp disagreement about the precise definition of *sadeh acher* will carry forward into subsequent discussions.
