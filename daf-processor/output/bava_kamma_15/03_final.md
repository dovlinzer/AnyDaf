[DAF:14b]
## Mishna Overview



### Archaic Mishna Language

The daf begins at the top of 14b, where we continue with a series of *mishnayot* that are notably cryptic, written in what one can only describe as an archaic layer of Tannaitic language. One wonders about the scholarly consensus: the assumption is that these *mishnayot* are among the earliest in the *mishna*. It is, in a way, a testament to the remarkable editorial achievement of Rebbe in organizing and standardizing *Shas* that most *mishnayot* do not read like this. The opacity here is striking precisely because it stands apart from the more discursive style that characterizes most of the *mishna*.

### Shum Kesef Mishnah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ שׁוּם כֶּסֶף, שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף. בִּפְנֵי בֵּית דִּין, וְעַל פִּי עֵדִים – בְּנֵי חוֹרִין, בְּנֵי בְרִית. וְהַנָּשִׁים בִּכְלַל הַנֶּזֶק. וְהַנִּיזָּק וְהַמַּזִּיק בְּתַשְׁלוּמִין.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: The determination of payment of damages is made by **monetary appraisal.** One pays with items **worth money.** This *halakha* applies **before a court. And** it is **based upon** the testimony of **witnesses** who are **free men,** i.e., men who are not Canaanite slaves, and who are **members of the covenant,** i.e., Jews. **And women are included in the** *halakhot* of **damages** in the same way as men. **And** both **the injured** party **and the one liable for the damage** are involved **in** the **payment.** The Gemara will explicate each of these principles.


The *mishna* reads: *shum kesef, shaveh kesef, bifnei beis din, ve-al pi eidim, bnei chorin bnei bris, ve-nashim bikhlal ha-nezek, ve-hanizak ve-hamazik betashlumin.* Assessment of money, monetary equivalent, before a court, through witnesses, free persons, members of the covenant, women are included in damages, and both the damaged party and the damager participate in the payment. It reads almost as a legal poem, and the *gemara* will now work through each phrase methodically to determine what it actually means.

## Para and Talit



### Rav Yehuda's Read

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ מַאי ״שׁוּם כֶּסֶף״?
> **Translation:** GEMARA: **What** is the meaning of: The determination of payment of damages is made by **monetary appraisal?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: שׁוּם זֶה לֹא יְהֵא אֶלָּא בְּכֶסֶף.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yehuda says: This appraisal** of the damage caused **should be** made **only in terms of** the **monetary** value of the damage.

The *gemara* opens with the first phrase: *mai shum kesef?* What does "assessment of money" mean? Rav Yehuda explains: *shum zeh lo yehei ela bekesef* — when you assess the loss, you do so in terms of the monetary value, the dollar amount. The *gemara* then notes that we have already learned this teaching, since a *braita* elaborates on it more explicitly.

### Cow/Garment Case

The *braita* presents the following scenario: *para she-hizika talit ve-talit she-hizika para* — a cow and a garment got into a kind of altercation and damaged one another. Tosafot works out precisely how this could occur: if the *talit* caused the *para* to slip, that would function like a *bor birshut harabim*; if the *para* ate the *talit*, that would need to occur in *reshut hanizak* and would be a form of *shen*. Perhaps the cow gored the garment, or consumed it in an unusual manner. Regardless of the precise mechanism, the *braita* rules: *ein omrim tze para be-talit ve-talit be-para* — we do not simply declare a swap, saying that the cow covers the garment's damage and the garment covers the cow's damage and the whole matter is settled. Rather, *shamnin otan bidamim* — you assess each loss in monetary terms, determine the difference, and whoever holds the balance pays that difference.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּנֵינָא לְהָא דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: פָּרָה שֶׁהִזִּיקָה טַלִּית, וְטַלִּית שֶׁהִזִּיקָה פָּרָה – אֵין אוֹמְרִים: תֵּצֵא פָּרָה בְּטַלִּית וְטַלִּית בְּפָרָה, אֶלָּא שָׁמִין אוֹתָהּ בְּדָמִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes: **We learn,** in this mishna, **this** *halakha* **that the Sages taught** explicitly: In a case in which **a cow damaged a cloak,** and the **cloak injured** the **cow,** e.g., the cow tread on it, thereby damaging it, and it became entangled in the cow’s legs, causing the cow to trip, **one does not say** that it may be presumed that the damage to the **cow is offset by** the damage to the **cloak and** the damage to the **cloak is offset by** the damage to the **cow,** and therefore the owners of both are exempt from any payment. **Rather,** the court **appraises** separately the damage caused to each side **in terms of** its **monetary** value and only then calculates to what extent the liabilities offset each other.

### Tosafot Chidushim


This ruling may seem self-evident, yet Tosafot identifies two distinct *chidushim* embedded here. The first concerns the case where the *para* damaged the *talit*. If the ox gored and was a *tam* — an animal that has not yet established a pattern of goring — the well-known rule is that the owner pays half the damage, capped at the value of the goring animal itself. One might have thought that rather than merely owing money, the injured party automatically acquires a proprietary interest in the goring ox itself. The second *chidush* addresses the reverse scenario: the *talit* functioned as a *bor* and caused the cow to slip. One might have thought that even though the owner of the *talit* owes money for the damage to the cow, the injured party of the cow should at least be able to seize the *talit* itself that caused the harm — especially in a case where the owner of the *talit* has since died and left no landed property from which a lien could be exercised. The *braita* teaches that this too is incorrect. What is owed in both cases is a monetary obligation, not a proprietary claim on the damaging object.

### Rabbi Akiva's Ownership


The first *chidush* speaks directly to a dispute between Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Yishmael. Rabbi Akiva holds the position of *yuchlaf hashor*: as soon as your ox gores my animal, I automatically become a part-owner in your ox. It is not merely that you owe me money; I now own a percentage of your animal as of the moment of the goring. This *mishna*, according to Tosafot, rules against Rabbi Akiva. When the *braita* says we do not simply declare a swap — *ein omrim tze para be-talit* — it is teaching that you do not acquire ownership of the goring ox. The owner of the ox owes you money, limited to the ox's value, but your claim does not translate into direct ownership of the animal.

The second *chidush* is perhaps less intuitive. By ordinary principles, one can only collect debts from landed property on which there is a lien. If the owner of the *talit* that caused the slip has died and left no land, the injured party might argue: surely I should at least have rights to the *talit* itself, the very object that caused my loss, even if I cannot collect from the orphans' other assets. The *braita* says no. The obligation here is purely monetary. How one pays is a separate question — one can pay in chattel, not necessarily in cash — but the nature of the obligation is defined as an amount of money owed, and not as any proprietary claim on the damaging object itself.

## Shaveh Kesef



### Chattel Payment

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף״ –
> **Translation:** § The mishna continues: With items **worth money;** to what does this refer?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאֵין בֵּית דִּין נִזְקָקִין אֶלָּא לִנְכָסִים שֶׁיֵּשׁ לָהֶן אַחְרָיוּת. אֲבָל אִם קָדַם נִיזָּק וְתָפַס מִטַּלְטְלִין – בֵּית דִּין גּוֹבִין לוֹ מֵהֶן.
> **Translation:** This is **as the Sages taught** in a *baraita*: Payment is made with items **worth money.** This **teaches that** the **court attends to** the collection of damages **only** in order to collect from **property that serves as a guarantee,** i.e., land. **But if the injured** party **proceeded,** of his own accord, **to seize movable property** from the one liable for the damage, the **court collects** damages **for him from those** items he seized. According to the *baraita*, the phrase: Items worth money, is referring to land.

The *gemara* now turns to the next phrase: *shaveh kesef*, monetary equivalent. The natural reading would seem to be that one need not pay in actual cash; any chattel equivalent in value will suffice — even *subin*, bran, as the *gemara* uses as its example. This is how the term is most classically understood.

### Braita's Land Read

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר: ״שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאֵין בֵּית דִּין נִזְקָקִין אֶלָּא לִנְכָסִים שֶׁיֵּשׁ לָהֶן אַחְרָיוּת. מַאי מַשְׁמַע?
> **Translation:** The Gemara analyzes this: **The Master said** in the *baraita*: Payment is made with items **worth money.** This **teaches that** the **court attends to** the collection of damages **only** in order to collect from **property that serves as a guarantee,** i.e., land. The phrase: Items worth money, therefore, is used as a term for land. The Gemara asks: **From where** is this **inferred?**

What the *gemara* actually does with this phrase is, however, surprising. A *braita* interprets *shaveh kesef* in a completely unexpected way: *shaveh kesef melamed she-ein beis din nizkakin ela le-nekhasim she-yesh lahem achrayus* — *shaveh kesef* teaches that *beis din* will only deal with, that is to say seize or levy, property that bears liens, which means land. According to this reading, *shaveh kesef* refers not to chattel as a payment medium but specifically to real property.

This is genuinely puzzling on two levels. First, why should *shaveh kesef* mean land, when it most naturally means chattel equivalent in monetary value? Second, why would *beis din* be limited to seizing land when the *mazik* is alive and directly obligated? The *braita* adds a further clause: if the *nizak* went ahead and physically seized chattel from the *mazik* on his own, *beis din* will allow him to keep it. Why? If *beis din* cannot officially levy chattel, why would a unilateral seizure by the *nizak* be validated?

The *gemara* resolves the second question: the rule applies specifically *be-yasmei*, where the *mazik* has died and we are dealing with orphans. The heirs are not directly obligated in their father's debt; collection from the estate is limited to landed property on which liens had been established. And liens, the *gemara* clarifies, are created automatically — the moment one causes damage, the injured party holds a lien on the *mazik*'s land. *Beis din* does not create the lien; it assists in exercising it.

The case of unauthorized seizure during the *mazik*'s lifetime is resolved by reference to the principle of *shetafas mechayim*: if the *nizak* seized chattel while the *mazik* was still alive, at a moment when the debt was directly owed by a living obligor, *beis din* will validate that seizure retroactively as payment. One might note, as was raised in the course of the *sugya*, that this bears resemblance to the principle of *avid inish dina le-nafshei* — taking the law into one's own hands — a topic that receives fuller treatment later in the *gemara* at 27b.

### No Fixed Price

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר עוּלָּא: דָּבָר הַשָּׁוֶה כֹּל כֶּסֶף. מַאי נִיהוּ? דָּבָר שֶׁאֵין לוֹ אוֹנָאָה. עֲבָדִים וּשְׁטָרוֹת נָמֵי אֵין לָהֶן אוֹנָאָה!
> **Translation:** **Rabba bar Ulla says** that the phrase: Items worth money, is referring to **something that is worth any** amount of **money,** and **what is that?** It is **something that is not subject to** price **fraud,** which is the *halakha* with regard to land. Unlike the sale of movable property, the sale of land is valid irrespective of the sum for which the land is sold. The Gemara challenges this explanation: **But slaves and documents are also not subject to** price **fraud,** and yet these are not included in the phrase: Items worth money, as the court does not collect payments of damages from them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר עוּלָּא: דָּבָר הַנִּקְנֶה בְּכֶסֶף. עֲבָדִים וּשְׁטָרוֹת נָמֵי נִקְנִין בְּכֶסֶף!
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rabba bar Ulla said:** The phrase is referring to **something that is acquired with money,** which is the *halakha* with regard to land but not with regard to movable property (see *Kiddushin* 26a). The Gemara rejects this explanation: But **slaves and documents are also acquired with money.**

The *gemara* then circles back to the primary puzzle: since when does *shaveh kesef* mean land? Rabba bar Ulla offers an explanation: *davar ha-shaveh ke-kesef* means something that can be worth an unlimited sum, with no fixed market price — and that is land, because land is formally excluded from the *parasha* of *ona'ah*, the prohibition of overcharging. Since there is no fixed price for land, it can be said to be equivalent to any amount of money, and hence is called *shaveh kesef*.

The *gemara* objects: slaves and documents of debt also lack the prohibition of *ona'ah*. Rabba bar Ulla then reformulates: *davar ha-nikneh be-kesef* — something acquired with money. Land is acquired through a monetary transaction, whereas chattel is acquired through *meshikha* and *hagbahah*, physical acts of acquisition. The *gemara* presses again: slaves are also acquired with money. Rashi and Tosafot note that the inclusion of *shtaros* — loan documents — in this category is itself not straightforward, since transferring debt obligations does not clearly follow the same rules. Regardless, the objection stands at least for *avadim*.

### Like Money Not Money

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַב אָשֵׁי: ״שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף״ – וְלֹא כֶּסֶף, וְהָנֵי כּוּלְּהוּ כֶּסֶף נִינְהוּ.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rav Ashi said:** The phrase is referring to something that is **worth money, but is not** actually **money, and all these** things, i.e., movable property, slaves, and documents, **are** considered like actual **money** because they have the defining characteristics of money in that they are valuable and portable.


Rav Ashi then proposes an altogether different reading: *shaveh kesef ve-lo kesef* — something that is *like* money but is not money itself. And who is *like* money? *Ve-hani kulu kesef ninhu* — all chattel is considered money. This follows from a point established earlier in the *gemara* regarding *meitav*: when one pays with chattel, even low-quality chattel, it can serve as payment, because movable property is presumed to be readily liquidable and therefore functionally equivalent to cash. What is genuinely *like* money but not quite money is land. Hence *shaveh kesef* designates land, because land is the one category that approximates but does not equal cash in terms of its acquisition and transfer mechanisms.

As the *gemara* itself acknowledges, this is not the straightforward reading of *shaveh kesef*. The most natural interpretation — and indeed the one that governs its use throughout *halakha* — is simply that payment can be made in any chattel equivalent in value to money, even *subin*, low-grade bran or refuse. But the *braita* reads it as land, and for that reason the *gemara* now must explain how an interpretation that inverts the ordinary usage of the term can be made to cohere as a legal ruling.

## Bifnei Beis Din



### Court Requirement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רָמֵי לֵיהּ רַב יְהוּדָה בַּר חִינָּנָא לְרַב הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יְהוֹשֻׁעַ, תָּנָא: ״שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁאֵין בֵּית דִּין נִזְקָקִין אֶלָּא לִנְכָסִים שֶׁיֵּשׁ לָהֶן אַחְרָיוּת. וְהָתַנְיָא: ״יָשִׁיב״ – לְרַבּוֹת שָׁוֶה כֶּסֶף, וַאֲפִילּוּ סוּבִּין!
> **Translation:** **Rav Yehuda bar Ḥinnana raises a contradiction to** the opinion of **Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua:** In the *baraita* cited earlier **it is taught:** The mishna’s statement that payment is made with items **worth money teaches that** the **court attends to** the collection of damages **only** in order to collect from **property that serves as a guarantee,** i.e., land. **But it is taught** also in another *baraita*: The verse states the superfluous phrase: **“He shall recompense”** (Exodus 21:34), **to include** items **worth money, and even bran,** a relatively inferior commodity, is accepted as a valid form of payment. The first *baraita* teaches that only land is a valid form of payment, whereas the second *baraita* teaches that one may pay even with movable property.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן – בְּיַתְמֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **With what are we dealing here** in the *baraita* that teaches that the court collects damages only from land? We are dealing **with** a case where damages are collected from **orphans** whose father was liable to pay damages. The *baraita* teaches that the liability inherited by the orphans applies only to the land they inherited from their father as it is liened to their father’s debts.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי בְּיַתְמֵי, אֵימָא סֵיפָא: אִם קָדַם נִיזָּק וְתָפַס מִטַּלְטְלִין – בֵּית דִּין גּוֹבִין לוֹ מֵהֶן. אִי בְּיַתְמֵי, אַמַּאי בֵּית דִּין גּוֹבִין לוֹ מֵהֶן?
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If** the *baraita* is referring to collection **from orphans, say,** and try to explain accordingly, **the latter clause** of the *baraita*: But **if the injured** party **proceeded,** of his own accord, **to seize movable property** from the one liable for the damage, the **court collects** damages **for him from those** items he seized. The Gemara asks: **If,** as suggested, the *baraita* is referring to collection **from orphans, why does the court collect** damages **for him from those** items he seized, as the father’s movable property is not liened to his debts?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּדְאָמַר רָבָא אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן: שֶׁתָּפַס מֵחַיִּים; הָכָא נָמֵי – שֶׁתָּפַס מֵחַיִּים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: The case is **like that which Rava says** that **Rav Naḥman says** with regard to a mishna (*Ketubot* 84a), which teaches that creditors who seize movable property as payment for a debt may keep those items: That is referring to a case in **which he seized** the property **while** the debtor was still **alive. Here also,** the *baraita* is referring to a case in **which he seized** the property **while** the one liable for the damage was still **alive.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״בִּפְנֵי בֵּית דִּין״ –
> **Translation:** § The mishna continues: **Before a court.** What does this phrase mean?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פְּרָט לְמוֹכֵר נְכָסָיו וְאַחַר כָּךְ הוֹלֵךְ לְבֵית דִּין.
> **Translation:** This phrase should be understood as a qualification of the previous phrase in the mishna that teaches, as the Gemara explained, that a court collects damages only from land. The mishna then teaches that the court collects only from land that is still in the possession of the one liable for the damage when he appears in court. This **excludes** a case where the one liable for the damage **sells his** landed **property and afterward goes to court.** Since at the time of the hearing he no longer owns land, the court does not collect damages from it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ – לָוָה וּמָכַר נְכָסָיו, וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּא לְבֵית דִּין, אֵין בֵּית דִּין גּוֹבִין לוֹ מֵהֶן?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges this: Should we **conclude from** the mishna that if one **borrows** money **and** then **sells his** landed **property, and afterward** he **goes to the court, the court does not collect** the loan **from those** landed properties that he sold? This is certainly incorrect, as the creditor has a lien on the debtor’s property from the time that he borrows the money.

The *gemara* next addresses *bifnei beis din* — assessment and payment must occur in the presence of a court. One might have thought that this refers to the situation where one sold off all one's property before going to court, leaving the plaintiff with nothing to collect. But the *gemara* immediately objects: if any of the sold property was land, the injured party should still be able to collect based on the existing lien. *Beis din* does not create liens; liens arise automatically at the moment of damage or borrowing. The court's role is to assist in enforcing them, not to generate them. Selling one's land before appearing in court should therefore make no difference to the plaintiff's ability to levy on those properties.

### Semicha Authority

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא פְּרָט לְבֵית דִּין הֶדְיוֹטוֹת.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** the phrase: Before a court, should be understood as an independent clause that **excludes a court of non-ordained judges [*hedyotot*]** from judging all types of damages, as the payment for certain types of damage is considered to be a fine, and only expert judges who have been ordained are able to impose fines.

The *gemara* therefore reinterprets *bifnei beis din*: *prat le-beis din hedyotos* — it excludes a court of commoners, a *beis din* that lacks the ordained authority traced back to Moshe Rabbeinu. This *semikhah* — distinct from the modern rabbinical ordination that bears the same name — empowers judges to adjudicate capital cases and *knasot*, monetary fines. A court empowered only to handle standard monetary litigation (*dinei mammonot*) cannot adjudicate the types of claims present here. The reason is that some of the obligations arising from the laws of damages — as the *gemara* is about to elaborate — are classified as *knas*, punitive fines rather than compensatory payments. Imposing a punishment requires a court that holds a level of divine authority, conceptually linked to the verse *ad ha-Elohim yavo devar shneihem*. A court of *hedyotos* lacks that standing.

### Valid Witnesses

The *mishna* continues: *al pi eidim* — through witnesses. This seems obvious; of course adjudication requires witnesses. The *chidush*, the *gemara* explains, lies in the phrase that follows: *prat le-modeh be-knas ve-achar kach ba'u eidim she-hu patur* — to exclude the case where one admitted to a fine and witnesses subsequently arrived. Since we are dealing with cases of *knas*, the rule of self-admission — *modeh be-knas patur* — comes into play.

## Modeh Beknas



### Self-Admission Exclusion

The underlying principle here is that self-admission cannot create an obligation to pay a fine. This distinguishes a *knas* fundamentally from *mamon*, ordinary civil liability. In matters of compensation, self-admission suffices and even obligates. In matters of fine, it does not.

### Fines as Punishment

The reason for this distinction is structural. A fine is conceptually a punishment imposed through monetary means, analogous to lashes — a punishment imposed through the body. Just as lashes require objective testimony and cannot be self-imposed, so too a monetary fine requires objective testimony. The principle of *ein adam meisim atzmo rasha* — a person cannot render himself wicked — functions here not merely as a procedural protection but as a substantive rule: the court's authority to punish depends on externally established guilt. Self-incrimination does not supply that foundation. Moreover, the court imposing the fine must possess the requisite *semikhah*, since the fine represents not compensation but a divinely authorized sanction.

### Witnesses After Admission

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״עַל פִּי עֵדִים״ –
> **Translation:** § The mishna continues: Payment is made **based upon** the testimony of **witnesses.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פְּרָט לְמוֹדֶה בִּקְנָס וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּאוּ עֵדִים, שֶׁהוּא פָּטוּר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: This clause is stated to **exclude** from liability one who **admits** he is liable to pay **a fine,** even if **witnesses come afterward** and testify that he is liable to pay it. The mishna teaches **that he is** nevertheless **exempt,** due to his admission.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנִיחָא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר: מוֹדֶה בִּקְנָס וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּאוּ עֵדִים – פָּטוּר. אֶלָּא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר: מוֹדֶה בִּקְנָס וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּאוּ עֵדִים – חַיָּיב, מַאי אִיכָּא לְמֵימַר?
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **This works out well according to the one who says: One who admits** he is liable to pay **a fine** is **exempt,** even if **witnesses come afterward** and testify to his liability. **But according to the one who says:** With regard to **one who admits** he is liable to pay **a fine and** then **witnesses come afterward** and testify that he is guilty, he is **liable** to pay the fine, **what is there to say** in explanation of this clause of the mishna?

There is a further debate referenced in the *gemara*: if one admits to a fine and witnesses subsequently arrive, is one obligated? One opinion holds that subsequent witnesses do render one liable — the initial admission is simply irrelevant but does not permanently foreclose the obligation. A second opinion holds that having admitted first, the person is permanently exempt, even if witnesses later corroborate the event. The *gemara* notes that if one follows the second view, the *chidush* of *al pi eidim* is that witnesses alone create the fine obligation. If one follows the first view — that subsequent witnesses do create liability — then *al pi eidim* is needed for a different purpose, which leads into the next clause: *bnei chorin bnei bris*.

> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: This clause does not serve to exclude some other case; rather, **it is needed** to preface **the latter clause** that teaches that the only people who are accepted as witnesses in cases of damages are both

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״בְּנֵי חוֹרִין וּבְנֵי בְרִית״. ״בְּנֵי חוֹרִין״ – לְמַעוֹטֵי עֲבָדִים, ״בְּנֵי בְרִית״ – לְמַעוֹטֵי גּוֹיִם.
> **Translation:** **free men and members of the covenant.** The mishna states: **Free men, to exclude** Canaanite **slaves** from being valid witnesses, and it states: **Members of the covenant, to exclude gentiles.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּצְרִיכָא; דְּאִי אַשְׁמְעִינַן עֶבֶד – מִשּׁוּם דְּאֵין לוֹ חַיִיס, אֲבָל גּוֹי – דְּיֵשׁ לוֹ חַיִיס, אֵימָא לָא; וְאִי אַשְׁמְעִינַן גּוֹי – מִשּׁוּם דְּלָא שָׁיֵיךְ בְּמִצְוֹת, אֲבָל עֶבֶד – דְּשָׁיֵיךְ בְּמִצְוֹת, אֵימָא לָא; צְרִיכָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes: **And** it is **necessary** to teach both exceptions, **as had** the Torah **taught us** only about **a slave,** I might have presumed that he is disqualified from serving as a witness **because he has no lineage,** i.e., the child of a Canaanite slave is not legally considered to be his child in any sense; **but a** free **gentile, who has lineage, I might say no,** he is qualified. **And** similarly, **had** the Torah **taught us** only about **a gentile,** I might have presumed that he is disqualified from serving as a witness **because he has no connection to** the **mitzvot** of the Torah; **but** with regard to a Canaanite **slave, who does have a connection to** the **mitzvot,** as he is obligated to keep the mitzvot that a woman is obligated to keep, **I might say no,** he is qualified. Therefore, it is **necessary** to teach both exceptions.

Valid witnesses must be *bnei chorin* — free persons — and *bnei bris* — members of the covenant. *Bnei chorin le-mautei avadim*: free persons to exclude non-Jewish slaves, who are not valid witnesses, even though they bear a partial obligation in *mitzvot*. *Bnei bris le-mautei ovdei kochavim*: members of the covenant to exclude non-Jews. The *gemara* establishes that both exclusions are necessary. One might have thought that excluding slaves — who occupy a diminished societal standing because *halakha* does not recognize their family relationships — is straightforward, whereas non-Jews, who do possess recognized family relationships and are of equal standing in society, might be includable. Conversely, one might have excluded non-Jews on account of their greater distance from the obligations of *mitzvot*, while thinking slaves, who are partly obligated in *mitzvot*, might qualify. Each exclusion is therefore necessary to convey that neither category is valid.

One notable observation arises here: the list of valid witnesses mentions *bnei chorin* and *bnei bris* but not women — even though the *mishna* explicitly mentions women in its subsequent clause. Women are in practice excluded from serving as witnesses, yet the *braita* does not include that exclusion in this list. A possible explanation is that it is simply taken for granted, though the asymmetry is noteworthy.

This raises the question, engaged in medieval practice, of how women's testimony might be handled in *dinei mammonot*. In medieval Ashkenaz, where women were frequently active participants in commerce, the *rishonim* developed a framework of accepting women's testimony not as formal *edut* but as *birur davar* — a form of circumstantial evidentiary weight that operates outside the technical framework of witness law. This approach sidestepped the formal disqualification while addressing the practical needs of a community where women were often central economic actors.

The related question of women serving as *dayanim* — judges — is taken up by Tosafot in the context of the verse *asher tasim lifneihem*, which the *gemara* elsewhere reads as referring to the judges before whom cases are brought, rather than the litigants. If *lifneihem* there includes women as parties to litigation, could it also be read to include women as judges? Tosafot notes that the general principle — *kol ha-kasher ladun kasher le-ha'id* — would seem to foreclose this, since women are disqualified as witnesses and therefore disqualified as judges. But Tosafot reframes the principle: it applies to men who are qualified to judge being qualified to testify. A woman might, on this reading, be a qualified judge even though she cannot serve as a witness. The case of Devorah is raised and then somewhat set aside: *shema hayu mekabelim otah aleihem mipnei Shekhinah* — perhaps they accepted her authority on account of her prophetic status, which constitutes a form of consensual arbitration rather than a demonstration of women's formal judicial standing. Tosafot leaves the question of whether a woman can serve as a *kasher dayan* as genuinely open.

## Women in Damages



### Women Included

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַנָּשִׁים בִּכְלַל הַנֶּזֶק. מְנָהָנֵי מִילֵּי?
> **Translation:** § The mishna continues: **And women are included in the** *halakhot* of **damages** in the same way as men. The Gemara asks: **From where is this matter** derived?

The *mishna* states: *ve-nashim bikhlal ha-nezek* — women are included in the laws of damages. The *gemara* asks: *mena hani mili?* From where is this derived?

### Masculine Presumption


Rav Yehuda in the name of Rav, and similarly the school of Rabbi Yishmael, derive it from the verse *ish o isha ki ya'asu mikol chatot ha-adam* — if a man or woman commits any of the sins of a person. From this, *hishvah ha-katuv isha le-ish le-khol onashim she-ba-Torah* — the Torah equates women to men with regard to all punishments in the Torah. The reason such a verse is needed, as Tosafot explains, is that the entire Torah is written *be-lashon zachar*, in the masculine gender. As in classical legal drafting, the masculine formulation is presumed to function as a gender-neutral default unless explicitly stated otherwise. But this raises the question of whether women are automatically included or whether explicit inclusion is required in each domain.

### Textual Proofs

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה אָמַר רַב, וְכֵן תָּנָא דְּבֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל, אָמַר קְרָא: ״אִישׁ אוֹ אִשָּׁה כִּי יַעֲשׂוּ מִכׇּל חַטֹּאת״ – הִשְׁוָה הַכָּתוּב אִשָּׁה לְאִישׁ לְכׇל עוֹנָשִׁין שֶׁבַּתּוֹרָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara presents three sources for this *halakha*. **Rav Yehuda says** that **Rav says, and similarly, the school of Rabbi Yishmael taught:** The **verse states** with regard to the liability of one who takes a false oath that he had not stolen: **“When a man or woman shall commit any of the sins** of a person” (Numbers 5:6). **The verse equates a woman with a man with regard to all punishments in the Torah.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּבֵי רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר תָּנָא: ״וְאֵלֶּה הַמִּשְׁפָּטִים אֲשֶׁר תָּשִׂים לִפְנֵיהֶם״ – הִשְׁוָה הַכָּתוּב אִשָּׁה לְאִישׁ לְכׇל דִּינִין שֶׁבַּתּוֹרָה.
> **Translation:** **The school of Rabbi Elazar taught:** The verse states with regard to the civil laws given following the revelation at Sinai: **“And these are the civil laws that you shall set before them”** (Exodus 21:1). The reference to “them” in the verse is referring to all those who stood at the revelation, both men and women. **The verse** thereby **equates a woman with a man with regard to all civil laws in the Torah.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּבֵי חִזְקִיָּה וְרַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי תָּנָא, אָמַר קְרָא: ״וְהֵמִית אִישׁ אוֹ אִשָּׁה״ – הִשְׁוָה הַכָּתוּב אִשָּׁה לְאִישׁ לְכׇל מִיתוֹת שֶׁבַּתּוֹרָה.
> **Translation:** **The school of Ḥizkiyya and Rabbi Yosei HaGelili taught:** The **verse states** with regard to liability in a case where one’s ox kills a person: **“And it killed a man or a woman”** (Exodus 21:29). **The verse** thereby **equates a woman with a man with regard to all killings in the Torah,** i.e., the liability incurred is the same whether the person killed was a man or a woman.

Multiple verses are brought to cover different domains. The school of Rabbi Yishmael derives from *ish o isha* that women are included in all punishments. The school of Rabbi Elazar derives from *ve-eileh ha-mishpatim asher tasim lifneihem* — the word *lifneihem* is not specifically masculine, and from it we learn *hishvah ha-katuv ish le-isha le-khol dinim she-ba-Torah*: civil law applies equally to men and women. The school of Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yose Ha-Gelili derive from *ve-nigach ish o isha* — an ox that gores a man or a woman — that with respect to being the injured party, the victim of a goring, men and women are treated identically: *hishvah ha-katuv ish le-isha le-khol mitot she-ba-Torah*.

All three are necessary.

### Women Judges Question

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּצְרִיכִי; דְּאִי אַשְׁמְעִינַן קַמַּיְיתָא – הָתָם הוּא דְּחָס רַחֲמָנָא עֲלַהּ, כִּי הֵיכִי דְּתֶהְוֵי לַהּ כַּפָּרָה; אֲבָל דִּינִין – אִישׁ, דְּבַר מַשָּׂא וּמַתָּן – אִין, אִשָּׁה – לָא.

> **Translation:** **And had** the Torah **taught us** only that men and women are equated in **civil law,** one might have said that this is **in order that she should** be able to **sustain** herself by engaging in business dealings. If civil laws did not apply to a woman, one would be wary about conducting business with her. **But** with regard to obligating a woman to achieve **atonement,** one might have said that **a man, who is obligated in mitzvot, yes,** he is similarly obligated to achieve atonement; but **a woman, who is not obligated in** all the **mitzvot** to the same extent as a man, no, the obligation does **not** apply to her.

> **Translation:** **And had** the Torah **taught us** only the equivalence of a man and woman with regard to **a ransom,** one might have thought that this is **because** it is a very serious matter as **there is a loss of life, but** in **these** first **two** sources, **where there is not a loss of life, I would say no,** a woman is not included. Accordingly, it is **necessary** to have all three sources.

Had only the first verse been stated — that women bring *korbanot* for sins — one might have said the Torah showed *rachmanus* on women so that they could achieve *kaparah*, but that this does not extend to civil law, where women, presumed not to be primary actors in commerce (*ish de-var masa u-matan*), might not be subject to the full framework of litigation. Had only the civil-law inclusion been stated, one might have inferred it was necessary only to ensure that women could function in economic life (*ki heichi de-tehavi lah chiyusa*), but that when it comes to *kaparah* — atonement — a woman, *de-lav bat mitzvah* in the sense of being less comprehensively obligated in *mitzvot*, perhaps does not fully access the atonement mechanism in the same way as a man who is more completely obligated. Had only these two been stated without the verse about the gored victim, one might — and here the *hava amina* becomes genuinely disturbing — have thought that if an ox kills a woman, the owner need not pay *kofer*, because *kofer* is linked to the gravity of the life taken, and a life with fewer *mitzvot* obligations might be seen as less fully within that calculus. The *gemara* closes this *hava amina* by pointing out that *kofer* is paid because *ika ibbud neshama* — there is a loss of life — and therefore of course it applies to a woman equally. All three verses are necessary, and the *maskana* is that women are included in all domains of Torah law — as agents, as litigants, and as victims — unless explicitly excluded.

The *gemara* here is, as the *hava amina* stage reveals, working with certain presumptions about women's status that are difficult for the modern reader. The conclusion is unequivocal in equalizing women across the board in tort law, but the very structure of the *tzricha* — needing proofs in each domain — reflects underlying assumptions that the rabbis themselves were working to address through the exegetical process. It is worth pausing over just how much weight the *gemara* places on the distinction of *mitzvot* obligation. Women are assumed to be *chayav* in the *lo ta'aseh* prohibitions — that much is not in dispute — and yet the *hava amina* treats their lesser obligation in *mitzvot aseh* as potentially diminishing both the significance of their transgressions and the *kofer*-value of their lives. *Rav Moshe* addressed this tension directly in a well-known *teshuva*, insisting that exemption from certain *mitzvot* carries no implication of lower *kedushah* or status — but a *gemara* like this one makes clear why the question presses so hard, because the *hava amina* does not merely raise a technical distinction in obligation; it appears to draw from that distinction a conclusion about the relative weight of a woman's life and the moral seriousness of her *aveirot*.

Passing from the *sugya* itself, *Tosafot* here raises a striking secondary question about women and *dayanus*. The word *lifneihem* in *asher tasim lifneihem* is read elsewhere in the *gemara* as referring not to those who come before the law as litigants but to those who adjudicate it — the *dayanim* — yielding the ruling *lifneihem v'lo lifnei hedyotot*, that cases must be brought before qualified judges and not before lay arbiters. If the same word *lifneihem* in our passage includes women as subjects of the law, does it not follow that women could also serve among those who administer it? *Tosafot* notes that *d'isha psula ladun* — a woman is disqualified from serving as a judge — on the grounds that whoever is fit to judge is fit to testify, and a woman is not fit to testify. But *Tosafot* then offers a striking rejoinder: perhaps the principle *kol ha-kasher ladun kasher le-haid* applies only to men — *kol ish ha-kasher ladun kasher le-haid* — meaning the inference runs only within the category of men, and does not rule out a woman being a *kasher dayan* even though she cannot be a *kasher ed*. The case of *Devorah* judging Israel is raised and then set aside — either because prophetic authority is a category apart, or because the people accepted her through a form of binding arbitration — leaving *Tosafot* without a definitive resolution. The question of whether a woman could in principle hold *smicha* and stand within the *parasha* of *Elohim* as a qualified *dayan* is, on this *Tosafot*, genuinely open.

## Half Damages Debate



### Rav Papa Compensation

*Palga nizka* — half damages in the case of a *tam* ox that gores — is the subject of a famous *itra'mar* between Rav Papa and Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua. Rav Papa holds: *mamona* — it is genuine compensation. His reasoning: *kasvar stam shvarim lav be-chezkat shimur kaimi* — ordinary oxen are not presumed to be inherently self-restrained; even an ox that has never gored still requires supervision. If an ox has not been watched and it goes out and gores, there is an element of negligence on the owner's part. Strictly speaking, therefore, the owner *should* pay full damages. *U-ve-din hu de-ba'ei le-shlumai kulei* — by strict logic he should pay everything. But the Torah has *rachmanus* on him — *de-akhti lav ya'ad*, the ox has not yet established a pattern of goring, has not gone through the *muad* process, and so the owner stands right at the edge of negligence. Because the negligence is borderline, the Torah goes easy on him and requires only half. But it remains genuine *mamon*: the owner was genuinely negligent, and what he pays is real compensation for real damages attributable to his failure.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִתְּמַר: פַּלְגָא נִזְקָא – רַב פָּפָּא אָמַר: מָמוֹנָא, רַב הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יְהוֹשֻׁעַ אָמַר: קְנָסָא.
> **Translation:** An amoraic dispute **was stated** with regard to the payment of **half** the cost of **the damage** made when one’s innocuous ox gores another’s animal. **Rav Pappa says:** It is **monetary restitution** for the injured party’s loss. **Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua, says:** It is **a fine.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב פָּפָּא אָמַר מָמוֹנָא – קָסָבַר: סְתָם שְׁווֹרִים לָאו בְּחֶזְקַת שִׁימּוּר קָיְימִי, וּבְדִין הוּא דְּבָעֵי לְשַׁלּוֹמֵי כּוּלֵּיהּ; וְרַחֲמָנָא הוּא דְּחָס עֲלֵיהּ, דְּאַכַּתִּי לָא אִיַּיעַד תּוֹרֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elucidates: **Rav Pappa says:** The payment is **monetary restitution,** as **he holds** that even **ordinary oxen,** which have not been forewarned with regard to Goring, are **not presumed to be safeguarded,** as their nature does not prevent them from acting in a belligerent manner. Therefore, the owner is responsible to safeguard them, to prevent them from doing so. Accordingly, if an ox causes damage by goring, **by right it** should have been **that** the owner **needs to pay** the **full** cost of the damage. Nevertheless, **the Merciful One had pity upon him, as his ox had not yet been forewarned** and he was not fully aware of the possibility that it might gore, and accordingly, the Torah reduced the extent of his liability.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יְהוֹשֻׁעַ אָמַר קְנָסָא – קָסָבַר: סְתָם שְׁווֹרִים בְּחֶזְקַת שִׁימּוּר קָיְימִי, וּבְדִין הוּא דְּלָא לְשַׁלֵּם כְּלָל; וְרַחֲמָנָא הוּא דְּקַנְסֵיהּ, כִּי הֵיכִי דְּלִנְטְרֵיהּ לְתוֹרֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua, says:** It is **a fine,** as **he holds** that **ordinary oxen** are **presumed to be safeguarded,** as their nature prevents them from acting in a belligerent manner. Therefore, the owner is not responsible for safeguarding them to prevent them from doing so. Accordingly, if an ox causes damage by goring, **by right it** should have been **that** the owner **does not pay at all.** Nevertheless, **the Merciful One penalized him in order that he will safeguard his ox,** even before it is forewarned, and decreed that the fine should be given to the injured party even though he is not really entitled to be compensated for his loss.

### Rav Huna Fine


Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua holds: *knasa* — it is a fine. *Kasvar stam shvarim be-chezkat shimur kaimi* — ordinary oxen *are* presumed to be naturally restrained and not in need of close supervision. Therefore the owner was *not* negligent. *U-be-dino de-lo le-shalem klal* — by strict logic he should pay nothing at all. But the Torah fined him anyway — *ki heikhi de-lintrei le-torei* — to incentivize a higher level of care. By knowing that even a *tam* goring will cost half the damage, every ox owner is pushed to maintain vigilance beyond what strict negligence liability would demand. The half payment is not compensation for actual fault; it is a punitive incentive to prevent harm.

### Borderline Agreement

Both positions, when placed side by side, agree on one thing: the *tam* goring occupies a genuine borderline. That is precisely why the Torah set the payment at half, not full and not zero. Rav Papa reads the halved payment as the Torah moderating a genuine obligation downward; Rav Huna reads it as the Torah imposing a liability where none strictly existed. One says it is slightly above the threshold of negligence with the Torah being lenient; the other says it is slightly below that threshold with the Torah being strict. The disagreement is about which side of the line the *tam* goring falls on.

It is worth pausing to note that the authority behind this half-payment is the Torah itself, not rabbinic enactment. When we call it a *knas*, we mean a Torah-level *knas* — something like *shloshim shel eved*, the thirty-shekel payment for a slave, or *kefel*, the double payment imposed on a thief. The Rabbis can and do legislate their own *knasot*, but that is an entirely different category. Here we are asking what the Torah's own ruling reflects about the underlying obligation, and it is striking that the Torah chose to intervene at exactly this gray zone rather than leaving the outcome to ordinary negligence analysis.

A related question lurks beneath both positions: does the framework of *bava kamma* begin from strict liability, with exemption available only to one who can demonstrate *oness* — genuine compulsion — or does it begin from the premise that one is liable only upon proof of negligence? That foundational tension, which runs throughout the tractate, is especially acute in a borderline case like *tam* goring, where the answer to that prior question would naturally determine which side of the line one lands on even before the Torah's intervention enters the picture.

## Mamona or Knasa



### Incomplete List

The *gemara* now attempts to resolve the *machloket* between Rav Papa and Rav Huna from Tannaitic sources. The first attempted proof: the *mishna* that enumerates the differences between *tam* and *muad* does not include *modeh be-knas* — self-admission — among those differences. If *palga nizka* were a *knas*, one would expect the list to mention that a *tam* cannot be paid on self-admission while a *muad* can. The omission suggests it is *mamona*. The *gemara* deflects: *amar tana ve-shayer* — the *tanna* taught an incomplete list.

## Both Parties Pay (II)



### Compensation Model

Returning to the *mishna*'s phrase *ha-nizak ve-ha-mazik be-tashlumin*: if *palga nizka* is *mamona*, the meaning is elegant and precise. The *mazik* genuinely owes full compensation, but pays only half; the *nizak* effectively absorbs the other half of his own loss. Both parties thus participate in the payment: the *mazik* pays half, and the *nizak* bears half. They each lose an equal portion of the damages.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּנַן: הַנִּיזָּק וְהַמַּזִּיק בְּתַשְׁלוּמִין. בִּשְׁלָמָא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר פַּלְגָא נִזְקָא מָמוֹנָא – הַיְינוּ דְּשָׁיֵיךְ נִיזָּק בְּתַשְׁלוּמִין; אֶלָּא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר פַּלְגָא נִזְקָא קְנָסָא – הַשְׁתָּא דְּלָאו דִּידֵיהּ שָׁקֵיל, בְּתַשְׁלוּמִין אִיתֵיהּ?!
> **Translation:** **We learned** in the mishna: Both **the injured** party **and the one liable for the damage** are involved **in** the **payment. Granted, according to the one who says** that **the** payment of **half the** cost of the **damage** is **monetary restitution,** this assumes that the injured party is theoretically entitled to receive the full value of his damaged animal but in practice receives only half. **This is** what the mishna means by saying **that the injured** party is also **involved in** the **payment,** because it is as though he is compelled to forgo half of the cost of the damage that by right he should be paid. **But according to the one who says** that **the** payment of **half the** cost of the **damage** is **a fine,** which assumes that the injured party is not really entitled to be compensated for his loss, **now that** even the money that **he takes is not his** by right, is it accurate to describe him **as being** involved **in the payment?**

### Fine Model


If *palga nizka* is *knasa*, the framing is different. The *mazik* technically owes nothing, yet receives a windfall of half the damage's value. The *nizak*, meanwhile, is out the full loss of his animal. In what sense can one say the *nizak* "participates in payment"? What *tashlumin* is the *nizak* paying when the entire amount is a fine that the *mazik* did not truly owe?

### Carcass Depreciation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֹא נִצְרְכָא אֶלָּא לִפְחַת נְבֵילָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: The mishna’s statement **is necessary only with regard to** the fact that the injured party is the one who absorbs the loss due to the **diminishing** value **of the carcass** between the animal’s death and the time that the case is brought before the court. Since he must absorb this financial loss, it may be said that he is involved in the payment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פְּחַת נְבֵילָה – הָא תְּנָא לֵיהּ רֵישָׁא: ״תַּשְׁלוּמֵי נֶזֶק״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁהַבְּעָלִים מִטַּפְּלִין בִּנְבֵילָה!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: How can one claim that the mishna is referring to the **diminishing** value **of the carcass? Isn’t it** already **taught** in **the first clause** of the mishna, which by using the term: **Payments** of restitution **for damage, teaches that the owner** of the injured animal **attends to,** i.e., retains ownership of, **the animal carcass?** And therefore it follows that he bears any depreciation in its value, as the Gemara (10b) explained above.

The *gemara* answers: *lo nitzrecha ela le-pichut neveila* — the phrase teaches that the *nizak* bears the depreciation of the carcass. When the animal is gored and killed, the carcass immediately begins to lose value. The *nizak* suffers that ongoing loss and cannot pass it to the *mazik*. That is how the *nizak* "participates in the payment" even under the *knasa* model: not by paying for half of what is owed, but by absorbing the diminishing value of what he was left with.

## Mamona or Knasa (II)



### Counter Incomplete

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חֲדָא בְּתָם, וַחֲדָא בְּמוּעָד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains why the mishna needs to teach this principle twice: **One** mention is made **with regard to an innocuous** ox **and** the other **one** is made **with regard to a forewarned** ox.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּצְרִיכָא, דְּאִי אַשְׁמְעִינַן תָּם – מִשּׁוּם דְּאַכַּתִּי לָא אִיַּיעַד, אֲבָל מוּעָד אֵימָא לָא. וְאִי אַשְׁמְעִינַן מוּעָד – מִשּׁוּם דְּקָא מְשַׁלֵּם כּוּלֵּיהּ, אֲבָל תָּם אֵימָא לָא; צְרִיכָא.
> **Translation:** **And** it is **necessary** to state both cases, **because if it would teach us** the principle only with regard to **an innocuous** ox, I might say that only there does the Torah limit the liability of the owner of the ox, **because it has not yet been forewarned. But** with regard to **a forewarned** ox, **I would say** the Torah does **not** limit the owner’s liability at all and the owner must also bear the loss due to the diminishing value of the carcass. **And** similarly, **if it would teach us** the principle only with regard to **a forewarned** ox, I might say that only there does the Torah limit the owner’s liability **because he is paying the full** cost **of** the damage, so the Torah does not overly burden him. **But** with regard to **an innocuous** ox, where he is liable for only half of the damage, **I would say** the Torah does **not** limit his liability any further, so he must bear the loss of the diminishing value of the carcass. Therefore, it is **necessary** to state the principle in both cases.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: מָה בֵּין תָּם לְמוּעָד? שֶׁהַתָּם מְשַׁלֵּם חֲצִי נֶזֶק – מִגּוּפוֹ, וּמוּעָד מְשַׁלֵּם נֶזֶק שָׁלֵם – מִן הָעֲלִיָּיה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara attempts to prove whether the payment of half the cost of the damage is considered to be monetary restitution or a fine: **Come** and **hear** a proof from a mishna (16b): **What** is the difference **between** the liability incurred for damage caused by **an innocuous** ox **and** that caused by **a forewarned** ox? The difference is **that** the owner of **an innocuous** ox **pays half the** cost of the **damage** exclusively **from** proceeds of the sale of **its body, and** the owner of **a forewarned** ox **pays** the **full** cost of the **damage from** his **superior-quality** property.

The *gemara* notes that the teaching about carcass depreciation appeared already in an earlier *mishna* — *tashlumei nezek melamed she-ha-bealim metaplin be-neveila* — and asks whether the earlier *mishna* and the current one overlap or address different cases. The answer: *chada be-tam ve-chada be-muad* — one addresses goring by a *tam* and the other goring by a *muad*. Both cases need to be stated: in the *tam* case one might have thought the *nizak* is partly spared since the *mazik* pays only half; in the *muad* case one might have thought the *mazik*, paying full, should also absorb the carcass depreciation. Each case requires explicit teaching.

Returning to the first proof attempt: the *mishna* listing differences between *tam* and *muad* omitted *modeh be-knas*. The *gemara* says this does not prove the list is complete, since another difference was also omitted: *kofer*. A *muad* pays full *kofer* and a *tam* pays none. The *gemara* responds: *ha mani Rabbi Yosei Ha-Gelili hi de-amar tam meshalem chatzi kofer* — this *mishna* may follow Rabbi Yosei Ha-Gelili, who holds that a *tam* does pay half *kofer*. If so, the distinction between *tam* and *muad* on *kofer* does not exist according to that opinion, and there is nothing left off the list with respect to *kofer*. So the first proof remains inconclusive; the list may simply be incomplete.

[DAF:15b]
### Self-Admission Proof

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִם אִיתָא, לִיתְנֵי נָמֵי הָא – תָּם אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ, מוּעָד מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ!
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the challenge posed by the mishna: **And if it is so,** that payment for damage caused by an innocuous ox is considered a fine, **let** the mishna **also teach this** additional distinction: The owner of **an innocuous** ox **does not pay based on his own** admission, in accordance with the *halakha* that one does not pay a fine based on one’s own admission, whereas the owner of **a forewarned** ox **pays based on his own** admission.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּנָא וְשַׁיַּיר. מַאי שַׁיַּיר דְּהַאי שַׁיַּיר?
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: The *tanna* could have taught this distinction; however, **he taught** only certain distinctions **and omitted** others, including this one. The Gemara asks: **What** else **did he omit that he omitted this?** A *tanna* would never omit just a single case, perforce there must be another *halakha* he omitted.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שַׁיַּיר חֲצִי כוֹפֶר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **He omitted** the *halakha* that if an innocuous ox kills someone, its owner does not pay even **half of the ransom** payment, whereas if the ox is forewarned, he pays the full ransom.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי מִשּׁוּם חֲצִי כוֹפֶר – לָאו שִׁיּוּרָא הוּא; הָא מַנִּי – רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי הִיא, דְּאָמַר: תָּם מְשַׁלֵּם חֲצִי כוֹפֶר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this: **If** one claims that the mishna omits the *halakha* of one who admits his liability **due to** the fact that it also omits the *halakha* that the owner of an innocuous ox does not pay even **half of the ransom,** that is a flawed claim, as **this is not an omission,** because one could say: In accordance with **whose** opinion **is this** mishna? It **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili, who says** that when **an innocuous** ox kills a person, one **pays half the ransom,** and therefore the distinction between an innocuous and forewarned ox is included in the mishna’s ruling that the owner of an innocuous ox pays half the cost of the damage. Accordingly, no proof can be adduced from the mishna.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע:
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests another proof: **Come** and **hear** a proof from a mishna (*Ketubot* 41a):

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״הֵמִית שׁוֹרִי אֶת פְּלוֹנִי״ אוֹ ״שׁוֹרוֹ שֶׁל פְּלוֹנִי״ – הֲרֵי זֶה מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ.
> **Translation:** If one admits: **My ox killed so-and-so, or** my ox killed **so-and-so’s ox,** then **this** person **pays based on his own** admission.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי, לָאו בְּתָם?
> **Translation:** The Gemara analyzes the mishna: **What, is** the *halakha* of the mishna **not** stated **with regard to an innocuous ox?** If so, this mishna proves that one is required to pay for half the cost of the damage even based on one’s own admission, which demonstrates that the payment is monetary restitution and not a fine.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא, בְּמוּעָד. אֲבָל תָּם מַאי – הָכִי נָמֵי דְּאֵין מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ? אִי הָכִי, אַדְּתָנֵי סֵיפָא ״הֵמִית שׁוֹרִי אֶת עַבְדּוֹ שֶׁל פְּלוֹנִי״ – אֵין מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ, לִפְלוֹג וְלִיתְנֵי בְּדִידֵיהּ:
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects the proof: **No,** the *halakha* of the mishna is stated **with regard to a forewarned** ox. The Gemara asks: **But** according to this explanation, if it had been an **innocuous** ox that gored, **what** would be the *halakha*? Would one say that **indeed, he does not pay based on his own** admission? But **if so, rather than teaching** in **the latter clause** of the mishna in *Ketubot*: If one admits: **My ox killed so-and-so’s** Canaanite **slave, he does not pay based on his own** admission, **let** the mishna **differentiate and teach** the distinction **with regard to** the case raised in the first clause **itself.** The reason for the distinction between the cases when one’s ox kills a Jew or a Canaanite slave is that in the first one pays monetary restitution, while in the second one pays a fine. If the mishna wishes to demonstrate the difference between a fine and monetary restitution with regard to admission, then instead of introducing a new case, the mishna would have modified the case of the previous clause.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בַּמֶּה דְּבָרִים אֲמוּרִים – בְּמוּעָד, אֲבָל בְּתָם – אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ!
> **Translation:** The mishna would have continued: **In what** case **is this statement** in the first clause **said?** It is said **with regard to a forewarned** ox, **but with regard to** an admission that one’s **innocuous** ox gored, **he does not pay based on his own** admission. The fact that the mishna does not do so suggests that in fact one is required to pay for half the cost of the damage based on one’s own admission that his innocuous ox gored. Evidently, the payment is considered to be monetary restitution.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כּוּלָּהּ בְּמוּעָד קָמַיְירֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this: The reason the mishna did not raise the distinction between an innocuous and a forewarned ox is not because that distinction is not a valid one, but because the **entire** mishna **speaks** only **of** cases of **a forewarned** ox. Accordingly, no proof can be adduced from the mishna.

The *gemara* then brings a *braita*: *heimit shori et ploni o shoro shel ploni, harei zeh meshalem al pi atzmo* — if I admit that my ox gored and killed a person or gored another's ox, I pay based on my own admission. The *gemara* asks: is this not speaking even of a *tam*? If so, it shows that half damages by a *tam* is *mamona*, since the owner pays on his own admission. The *gemara* deflects: perhaps the *braita* speaks only of a *muad*. But then, the *gemara* responds, what would the ruling be for a *tam*? If *palga nizka* is *knasa*, then by a *tam* one should not pay on self-admission. Why does the *braita* not tell us that explicitly — contrasting *muad* with *tam* — rather than contrasting the case of goring a person or animal with the case of goring a slave? *Liflog ve-litni bididah* — let the *braita* divide the *tam/muad* distinction internally. The *gemara* answers: the *braita* is speaking entirely of *muad* cases and has no occasion to address *tam* goring at all.

### The Key Rule

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: זֶה הַכְּלָל, כׇּל הַמְשַׁלֵּם יוֹתֵר עַל מַה שֶּׁהִזִּיק – אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ. מַאי, לָאו הָא פָּחוֹת מִמַּה שֶׁהִזִּיק – מְשַׁלֵּם?
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests further: **Come** and **hear** a proof from the concluding clause of the mishna just cited: **This is the principle: Anyone who pays more than** the cost of **that which he damaged does not pay based on his own** admission. The Gemara infers: **What, is it not** that were he liable to pay **less than** the cost of **that which he damaged, he would pay** based on his own admission? Since when an innocuous ox gores, its owner is liable to pay more than the cost of the damage, the payment is clearly not monetary restitution, and this is why it is not payable based on one’s own admission.

The *gemara* advances what appears to be a decisive *braita*: *ze ha-kelal, kol ha-meshalem yoter al mah she-hizik eino meshalem al pi atzmo* — here is the general rule: anyone who pays *more* than the actual damage does not pay on self-admission. Payment in excess of actual damage is the hallmark of *knas*. The implicit counterpart: if you pay *less* than the damage, it would seem to be *mamon*, compensation. And half damages is less than full — so it should be *mamona*.

### Resolving Exception

The *gemara* immediately presses: does paying *less* than the damage always mean it is *mamon*? The *braita*'s formulation says if you pay *more* you cannot pay on self-admission — implying that if you pay less, you can. But should one say that if you pay less it is *always* compensation? That would mean one should have stated the rule differently: *kol she-eino meshalem kamah she-hizik* — anyone who does not pay the *exact* amount of the damage does not pay on self-admission. That broader formulation would capture both more and less than exact damage as potential *knas*. The fact that the *braita* said only "more" seems to prove that paying less is *mamon*. This appears to be a strong proof — *tiyuvta*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא, הָא כְּמָה שֶׁהִזִּיק – מְשַׁלֵּם.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects the inference: **No,** one should infer only that were he liable to pay **as much as** the cost of **that which he damaged, he pays** based on his own admission. If the sum to be paid is more or less than the cost of that which he damaged, he would not pay based on his own admission.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲבָל פָּחוֹת מַאי – הָכִי נָמֵי דְּלָא מְשַׁלֵּם? אִי הָכִי, אַדְּתָנֵי: זֶה הַכְּלָל, כׇּל הַמְשַׁלֵּם יוֹתֵר עַל מַה שֶּׁהִזִּיק – אֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם עַל פִּי עַצְמוֹ; לִיתְנֵי: זֶה הַכְּלָל, כֹּל שֶׁאֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם כְּמָה שֶׁהִזִּיק – דְּמַשְׁמַע פָּחוֹת, וּמַשְׁמַע יוֹתֵר! תְּיוּבְתָּא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks. **But** according to that opinion, where one is liable to pay **less** than the cost of the damage, **what** would be the *halakha*? Would one say that, **indeed, he does not pay** based on his own admission? **If so, instead of teaching: This is the principle: Anyone who pays more than** the cost of **that which he damaged does not pay based on his own** admission, **let** the mishna **teach** instead: **This is the principle: Anyone who does not pay as much as** the cost of **that which he damaged** does not pay based on his own admission. **As** that statement **indicates** cases where one is liable to pay **more** than the cost of that which he damaged **and it** also **indicates** cases where one is liable to pay **less** than the cost of that which he damaged. Evidently, the mishna holds that where one’s liability is for less than the cost of the damage, it is payable based on one’s own admission. This therefore provides **a conclusive refutation** of the opinion that the payment for half the cost of the damage is a fine.

### Tzerorot Example



The *gemara* responds: *lo psika lei* — the rule cannot be stated in that broader form, because there are cases where one pays *less* than the full damage and it is nonetheless *mamona*, genuine compensation. The example: *chatzi nezek tzerorot* — the half damages paid for pebbles kicked by an animal in the public domain. In that case, the animal's owner was fully negligent; the indirect causation through the pebbles simply means the law reduces the payment to half as a matter of *halakha*, not as a punitive measure. It is *mamona*, but it is less than full damage. Because this exception exists, the *braita* could not formulate the rule as "any amount other than exact damages is *knas*" — that would have incorrectly classified *chatzi nezek tzerorot* as a fine. Hence the rule was stated only for the "more than" case, leaving "less than" ambiguous. The attempted proof from this *braita* therefore fails to establish definitively that *chatzi nezek keren* is *mamona*.

## Final Ruling Knasa



### Ruled as Fine

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהִלְכְתָא: פַּלְגָא נִזְקָא – קְנָסָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara adds: **And the *halakha*** is that the payment for **half** the cost of **the damage is a fine.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּיוּבְתָּא וְהִלְכְתָא?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes the obvious inconsistency: How can it be that the Gemara offers **a conclusive refutation** of the opinion that the payment for half the cost of the damage is a fine **and** then state that **the *halakha*** is in accordance with that opinion?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִין, טַעְמָא מַאי הָוְיָא תְּיוּבְתָּא – מִשּׁוּם דְּלָא קָתָנֵי כְּמוֹ שֶׁהִזִּיק;
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: **Yes,** the *halakha* may be in accordance with that opinion because of the following resolution of the refutation. **What is the reason** that the Gemara held that **there is a conclusive refutation** of the opinion that the payment for half the cost of the damage is a fine? **Because** the mishna **does not teach:** Anyone who does not pay **as much as** the cost of **that which he damaged** does not pay based on his own admission. This is not, in fact, a conclusive refutation, as even if one holds that the payment of half the cost of the damage for an innocuous ox is considered monetary restitution, one can still explain why the mishna doesn’t discuss cases where one is liable for less than the cost of the damage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא פְּסִיקָא לֵיהּ. כֵּיוָן דְּאִיכָּא חֲצִי נֶזֶק צְרוֹרוֹת, דְּהִלְכְתָא גְּמִירִי לַהּ דְּמָמוֹנָא הוּא; מִשּׁוּם הָכִי לָא קָתָנֵי.
> **Translation:** This is because the *tanna* **cannot make an absolute** statement about such cases, that one never pays based on one’s own admission. **Since there is** an obligation for one to pay for **half** the cost of **the damage** resulting from **pebbles** inadvertently propelled by the foot of a walking animal, **which, through tradition it is learned that** this payment **is monetary restitution,** and it is **due to that** reason that the mishna **does not teach** cases where one’s liability is valued at less than the cost of the damage.

After systematically deflecting every Tannaitic proof that would have established *palga nizka* as *mamona*, the *gemara* delivers its ruling: *ve-hilkheta palga nizka knasa* — the *halakha* is that half damages of a *tam* goring is a *knas*, a fine. This is remarkable. The *gemara* just concluded *tiyuvta* — a definitive refutation — of the *mamona* position, and then immediately rules as *knasa*. The *gemara* acknowledges the tension: *tiyuvta ve-hilkheta?* It responds: *ein* — yes. The reason the *tiyuvta* was considered so powerful was because the *braita* said "more than the damage" — implying less than the damage is *mamona*. But in fact that implication does not hold universally, since *chatzi nezek tzerorot* is *mamona* while paying less than full. The *braita*'s formulation was not meant to be exhaustive in the other direction. With that resolved, the *gemara* is free to rule that *chatzi nezek keren* is *knasa*, in accordance with Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua.

### Implications Pending

The practical consequences of classifying *chatzi nezek* of a *tam* as *knasa* are substantial and will be taken up in the *gemara* that follows. Among the most significant: from what source does one pay the *knas*? The *gemara*'s resolution of that question — touching on the rule of payment from the body of the animal, the principle of *modeh be-knas patur*, and related issues — will be the subject of the continuation.
