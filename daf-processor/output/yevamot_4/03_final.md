[DAF:3b]
## Arayot Exemption



### Mana Hani Milei

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְפִי שֶׁאֵינָהּ בְּצָרַת צָרָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates: This is **because they do not** involve the case **of a rival wife of a rival wife.** With regard to the fifteen women listed, the discussion of the mishna concerning rival wives and rival wives of rival wives is appropriate. However, the two cases cited by Rav and Rav Asi do not leave room for such deliberations, as both a *sota* and an *aylonit* are exempt and forbidden equally to all of the brothers, because their prohibition does not result from a familial relation to one of the living brothers but from a personal issue relating to the women themselves. Since none of the brothers may marry her rival wife, there is no possibility of a rival wife of a rival wife, and consequently these cases were omitted from the mishna’s list of fifteen women.


The *Gemara* on 3b opens with a foundational question: *Mana hani milei?* — where do all these laws come from? Specifically, the question targets two distinct principles established in the *mishna*: first, that there is no *mitzvah* of *yibbum* when one of the women is an *ervah* to the *yavam*; and second, that the *tzara* — the co-wife — of such a woman is equally exempt. The *Gemara* signals that it will now provide the scriptural and exegetical basis for everything discussed thus far.

### Isha El Achota

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי — דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״אִשָּׁה אֶל אֲחוֹתָהּ לֹא תִקָּח לִצְרוֹר לְגַלּוֹת עֶרְוָתָהּ עָלֶיהָ בְּחַיֶּיהָ״. ״עָלֶיהָ״, מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר?
> **Translation:** § After analyzing the order and language of the mishna, the Gemara discusses the *halakhot* themselves. **From where are these matters,** that if one’s forbidden relative comes before him for levirate marriage he is prohibited from marrying her or her rival wife, derived? It is **as the Sages taught** with regard to the verse: **“And you shall not take a woman to her sister, to be a rival to her, to uncover her nakedness, with her in her lifetime”** (Leviticus 18:18). **What** is the meaning when **the verse states** the apparently superfluous phrase: **“With her”?**

*Detanu rabanan* — the Rabbis taught in a *baraita*: *Isha el achota lo tikach* — do not take a woman and her sister, *litzror* — to create friction or agitation between them — *legalot ervata aleha bechayeha* — to uncover her nakedness upon her, in her lifetime. The qualification *bechayeha* — in her lifetime — is significant: the prohibition of a woman and her sister applies specifically while the original wife is still alive. The verse thus establishes the general prohibition. The *baraita* then asks: *Ma talmud lomar?* — what does the verse come to teach? *Lefi shene'emar* — because the verse says *yevama yavo aleha* — the brother-in-law shall come upon her — one might have thought even in the case of *yibbum*, the *mitzvah* could override the prohibition of *arayot*. That is the *hava amina*.

### Aleha Drasha

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְפִי שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״יְבָמָהּ יָבֹא עָלֶיהָ״, שׁוֹמֵעַ אֲנִי אֲפִילּוּ בְּאַחַת מִכׇּל עֲרָיוֹת הָאֲמוּרוֹת בַּתּוֹרָה הַכָּתוּב מְדַבֵּר, נֶאֱמַר כָּאן ״עָלֶיהָ״ וְנֶאֱמַר לְהַלָּן ״עָלֶיהָ״,
> **Translation:** The *baraita* explains: **Since it is stated** with regard to the wife of a deceased brother: **“Her brother-in-law will have intercourse with her”** (Deuteronomy 25:5), **I** would **derive** that when **the verse speaks of** the mitzva of levirate marriage, it includes **even any one of those with whom relations are forbidden,** as **mentioned in the Torah.** Therefore, one derives a verbal analogy: **It is stated here,** with regard to a wife’s sister: **“With her,” and it is stated there,** with regard to a levirate marriage: **“With her.”**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָה לְהַלָּן בִּמְקוֹם מִצְוָה, אַף כָּאן בִּמְקוֹם מִצְוָה, וְאָמַר רַחֲמָנָא: ״לֹא תִקָּח״.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* explains the verbal analogy. **Just as there,** a levirate marriage involves **the performance of a mitzva, so too, here,** the statement “uncover her nakedness with her” includes **the performance of a mitzva, and the Merciful One states** in the Torah: **“You shall not take.”** The phrase “with her” teaches that even in a case where there is an obligation of levirate marriage, the Torah prohibition proscribing forbidden relatives remains in force.

The *baraita* resolves this through a *gezeirah shavah*-like verbal link on the word *aleha*. *Ne'emar kan aleha* — it says here, in the case of the wife's sister: *legalot ervata aleha* — to uncover her nakedness *aleha*. *Vene'emar lehalan aleha* — and it says there, in the case of *yibbum*: *yevama yavo aleha*. The word *aleha* appears in both contexts.

The method of inference works as follows: since in the context of *yibbum* the word *aleha* refers to a case in which there is a *mitzvah* of *yibbum*, so too when the verse forbidding a woman and her sister uses the word *aleha*, it means: even in a case of *aleha* — even in a case of *yibbum*. *Ma lehalan bemakom mitzvah* — just as *aleha* there denotes the *mitzvah* of *yibbum*, *af kan bemakom mitzvah* — so too here it means even in a context of *yibbum*. The Torah then says *ve'amar rachmana lo tikach* — even in that case, you may not proceed. The verse thus teaches unambiguously that the prohibition of a wife's sister is not displaced by the *mitzvah* of *yibbum*.

### Wife's Sister

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵין לִי אֶלָּא הִיא, צָרָתָהּ מִנַּיִן? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״לִצְרוֹר״. וְאֵין לִי אֶלָּא צָרָתָהּ, צָרַת צָרָתָהּ מִנַּיִין? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״לִצְרוֹר״, וְלֹא ״לָצוֹר״.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: **And I have** derived **only** that **she,** his wife’s sister, is exempt from levirate marriage; **from where** do I derive that **her rival wife** is also exempt? **The verse states: “To be a rival** to her” (Leviticus 18:18), which indicates that not only is she prohibited, but so too is her rival wife. **And I have** derived **only her rival wife; from where** is it derived that **the rival wife of her rival wife** is also exempt? **The verse states: “To be a rival [*litzror*],”** using the full spelling with a double *reish*, **and not *latzor*;** this indicates that there are several rival wives, one after another.

The *baraita* continues: *Ve'ein li ela hi* — I know only that there is no *mitzvah* of *yibbum* in the case of the wife's sister herself. *Tzaratah minayin?* — from where do I know that the *tzara* — the co-wife of the wife's sister — is also exempt from *yibbum*? *Talmud lomar litzror* — the verse teaches through the word *litzror*. The word *litzror*, meaning to create friction or agitation, is read as encoding the word *tzara* — co-wife — just as we find by Hannah and Penina: *ve'ki'asata tzaratah gam ka'as*. This is a *drasha* on the word, not a verse from the Torah itself. Thus *litzror* signals that the exemption extends to the co-wife as well.

The *baraita* then continues: *Ve'ein li ela tzaratah* — I know only the direct co-wife. *Tzarat tzaratah minayin?* — what about the co-wife's co-wife? If this *tzara* subsequently marries another brother who then dies, does her co-wife also become exempt? *Talmud lomar litzror velo litzor* — the verse says *litzror* with a doubled *resh* rather than *litzor* with a single one. The doubled form indicates: even the *tzara* of a *tzara*.

This generates an interesting conceptual question that the *Gemara* will return to: is the *tzarat tzarah* really a distinct category, or does the mechanism work differently? Once there is no *mitzvah* of *yibbum*, the woman becomes an *eshet ach* — a forbidden relation in her own right. So when the *tzara* subsequently enters a *yibbum* situation with another brother, perhaps her co-wife is exempt not because of the category of *tzarat tzarah* per se, but simply because the *tzara* has herself become an *ervah* through her status as *eshet ach*. Whether these are truly distinct conceptual categories or simply generate overlapping cases of *eshet ach* remains an open question, though the *Gemara*'s textual derivation from *litzror* stands.

### Mah Matzinu

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵין לִי אֶלָּא אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה, שְׁאָר עֲרָיוֹת מִנַּיִין? אָמַרְתָּ: מָה אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה מְיוּחֶדֶת — שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה, וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת, וַאֲסוּרָה לַיָּבָם; אַף כֹּל שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה, וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת — אֲסוּרָה לַיָּבָם.
> **Translation:** **And I have** derived from this verbal analogy **only** that the mitzva of levirate marriage does not apply to **a wife’s sister. From where** is it derived that the same applies to **the other** women **with whom relations are forbidden? You** can **say** as follows: **Just as** the case of **a wife’s sister is specific** in **that she is a forbidden relative, and** this is a prohibition **for whose intentional** violation, i.e., for intentional sexual relations with her, **one is liable to** receive ***karet*, and for whose unwitting** violation one is liable to bring **a sin-offering, and** she is **prohibited to the *yavam*** in levirate marriage; **so too,** with regard to **all** women **with whom relations are forbidden** by a prohibition **for whose intentional** violation **one is liable to** receive ***karet* and for whose unwitting** violation one is liable to bring **a sin-offering,** they are **prohibited to the *yavam*** in levirate marriage.

*Ve'ein li ela achot ishah* — I know only that the exemption and the exemption of the *tzara* apply in the case of the wife's sister. *She'ar arayot minayin?* — from where do I know the same applies to all other *arayot*? The *baraita* employs a *mah matzinu* — a paradigm inference: *mah achot ishah miyuchedet shehi ervah vechayavin al zedonah kareit ve'al shigegatah chatat ve'asurah leyavam* — just as the wife's sister is an *ervah* for which intentional transgression carries *kareit* and unintentional transgression requires a *chatat* offering, and she is forbidden to the *yavam* — *af kol shehi ervah vechayavin al zedonah kareit ve'al shigegatah chatat ve'asurah leyavam* — so too any other woman who is an *ervah* of comparable severity is likewise forbidden to the *yavam*. There is nothing distinctive about the wife's sister that should make her unique. The relevant criterion is simply: *ervah* with a *kareit* penalty. Any such case falls under the same rule.

### Tzarot Exemption

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵין לִי אֶלָּא הֵן, צָרוֹתֵיהֶן מִנַּיִין? אָמַרְתָּ: מָה אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה מְיוּחֶדֶת — שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת, וַאֲסוּרָה לַיָּבָם, וְצָרָתָהּ אֲסוּרָה; אַף כֹּל שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה, וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת, וַאֲסוּרָה לַיָּבָם — צָרָתָהּ אֲסוּרָה. מִכָּאן אָמְרוּ חֲכָמִים: חֲמֵשׁ עֶשְׂרֵה נָשִׁים פּוֹטְרוֹת צָרוֹתֵיהֶן וְצָרוֹת צָרוֹתֵיהֶן מִן הַחֲלִיצָה וּמִן הַיִּיבּוּם עַד סוֹף הָעוֹלָם.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: **And I have** derived **only** the cases of **those** women themselves with whom relations are forbidden; **from where** is it derived that **their rival wives** are also exempt from levirate marriage? **You** can **say** as follows: **Just as a wife’s sister is specific** in **that she is a forbidden relative and** this is a prohibition **for whose intentional** violation **one is liable to** receive ***karet*, and for whose unwitting** violation one is liable to bring **a sin-offering,** and she is **prohibited to the *yavam*** in levirate marriage, **and her rival wife is** likewise **prohibited; so too, any** woman **with whom relations are forbidden and** this is a prohibition **for whose intentional** violation **one is liable to** receive ***karet*, and for whose unwitting** violation one is liable to bring **a sin-offering, and** this woman is one **who is forbidden to the *yavam*, her rival wife is** likewise **forbidden.** The *baraita* concludes: **From here the Sages stated** that **fifteen women exempt their rival wives and the rival wives of their rival wives from *ḥalitza* and from levirate marriage forever.**

Once the exemption is extended to all *arayot*, the *baraita* asks: *Tzaroteihen minayin?* — from where do I know that the co-wives of all such *arayot* are also exempt? The same paradigmatic reasoning applies: *mah achot ishah miyuchedet shehi ervah vechayavin al zedonah kareit ve'al shigegatah chatat ve'asurah leyavam vetzaratah asurah* — just as the wife's sister, being an *ervah* of that severity, is forbidden to the *yavam* and her *tzara* is also forbidden — *af kol* — so too any similarly situated *ervah* exempts her co-wife as well. This establishes the general principle: *ha'arayot pturot vepoterot tzaroteihen* — *arayot* are themselves exempt and they exempt their co-wives. The entire principle is derived from the wife's sister as paradigm, and then generalized to all fifteen *arayot* enumerated in the *mishna*.

### Chamesh Esrei

The *baraita* then articulates this conclusion formally: *Mikan amru chachamim* — from here the Sages said: *chamesh esrei nashim poterot tzaroteihen vetzarat tzaroteihen min hachalitzah umin hayibbum ad sof kol ha'olam* — fifteen women exempt their co-wives and co-co-wives from *chalitzah* and *yibbum* indefinitely. The *baraita* then raises a further question: *yachol she'ani marbeh af sheish arayot hachamurot me'elu sheyihiyu tzaroteihen asurot* — perhaps I should include even the six more weighty *arayot*, those more severe than the fifteen, and say that their co-wives are also forbidden.

### Sheish Chamurot

A brief look at *Daf* 13 clarifies what the "six more weighty" cases are. The *mishna* there states: *sheish arayot chamurot me'elu mipnei shenesuot l'acherim* — six *arayot* are more weighty than those fifteen because they are married to other people — specifically because they could never be married to one's brother. Examples include one's mother and one's sister. In these cases, since the brother could never have been married to such a woman, there is never a context of *yibbum* to begin with. Since there is no *yibbum* context, the co-wife does not become an *eshet ach*, and therefore she remains permitted.

### Tzara Context

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יָכוֹל שֶׁאֲנִי מְרַבֶּה אַף שֵׁשׁ עֲרָיוֹת חֲמוּרוֹת מֵאֵלּוּ, שֶׁיְּהוּ צָרוֹתֵיהֶם אֲסוּרוֹת?
> **Translation:** § One **might** have thought **that I** should **include** in this principle **even the six** women **with whom relations are forbidden that are more severe than these,** i.e., one’s mother, his father’s sister, and so on, as stated in a later mishna (13b), since they too are forbidden by a prohibition entailing *karet*. This would mean **that their rival wives should** likewise **be prohibited** to enter into levirate marriage with this *yavam* as forbidden rival wives.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַרְתָּ: מָה אֲחוֹת אִשְׁתּוֹ מְיוּחֶדֶת שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה, וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת, וְאֶפְשָׁר לִינָּשֵׂא לָאַחִים, וַאֲסוּרָה לַיָּבָם, וְצָרָתָהּ אֲסוּרָה; אַף כֹּל שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה, וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת, וְאֶפְשָׁר לִינָּשֵׂא לָאַחִים, וַאֲסוּרָה לַיָּבָם — צָרָתָהּ אֲסוּרָה.
> **Translation:** In response, **you** can **say: Just as a wife’s sister is specific** in **that she is a forbidden relative and** this is a prohibition **for whose intentional** violation **one is liable to** receive ***karet* and for whose unwitting** violation one is liable to bring **a sin-offering, and** yet she is **permitted to marry** one of the **brothers but** she is **prohibited to the *yavam*** in levirate marriage, **and her rival wife is** likewise **prohibited** in levirate marriage; **so too,** with regard to **any** woman **with whom relations are forbidden and** this is a prohibition **for whose intentional** violation **one is liable to** receive ***karet* and for whose unwitting** violation one is liable to bring **a sin-offering, and** who is **permitted to marry** one of the **brothers but** she is **prohibited to the *yavam*** in levirate marriage, **her rival wife is** also **prohibited** in levirate marriage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יָצְאוּ שֵׁשׁ עֲרָיוֹת חֲמוּרוֹת מֵאֵלּוּ, הוֹאִיל דְּאִי אֶפְשָׁר לִינָּשֵׂא לָאַחִים — צָרוֹתֵיהֶן מוּתָּרוֹת, שֶׁאֵין צָרָה אֶלָּא מֵאָח.
> **Translation:** **This excludes** the **six** women **with whom relations are forbidden** by **a more severe** prohibition **than those, since they may not marry the brothers,** i.e., they are forbidden to all of the brothers. One’s mother may never marry his brother, either because she is also that brother’s mother, or because she is his father’s wife. Consequently, **their rival wives are permitted, as** the *halakha* with regard to **a rival wife applies only** due **to the brother.** In other words, the prohibition against marriage to the rival wife of a forbidden relative is applicable only in instances of levirate bonds. When the levirate bond does not take effect at all, the rival wife is not forbidden.

The *baraita* now answers the question it raised: *amrat* — you respond. *Mah achot ishah miyuchedet shehi ervah vechayavin al zedonah kareit ve'al shigegatah chatat ve'efshar l'hinasay l'ach* — what is distinctive about the wife's sister is that she is an *ervah*, carries a *kareit* penalty, and — crucially — she *could* have been married to a brother. *Im ken asurah leyavam vetzaratah asurah* — in such a case she is forbidden to the *yavam* and her co-wife is forbidden. *Af kol shehi ervah vechayavin al zedonah kareit ve'al shigegatah chatat ve'efshar l'hinasay l'ach* — similarly, any *ervah* that could have been married to a brother: *asurah leyavam vetzaratah asurah* — she is forbidden and her *tzara* is forbidden. *Yatz'u sheish arayot hachamurot me'elu sheharei ee efshar l'hinasay l'ach* — this excludes the six more severe cases, since they cannot be married to a brother. *Tzaroteihen mutarot* — their co-wives are therefore permitted. *She'ein tzara ela mei'ach* — the entire concept of a *tzara* creating a *yibbum* exemption exists only in the context of a co-wife of one's brother.

It is worth pausing to note what this derivation is not saying. One might have thought that an *ervah* forbids her co-wife in some freestanding way — that mere proximity to a forbidden relationship contaminates the co-wife regardless of any *yibbum* context. The *baraita* rejects that entirely. The *hava amina* — the initial assumption — that the six more severe *arayot* might extend their prohibition to their co-wives through some intrinsic quality of severity is precisely what the *achot ishah* analogy dismantles. The only mechanism by which a *tzara* becomes forbidden is the *yibbum* framework: when there is a deceased brother's marriage, when there would otherwise be a *mitzvah* of *yibbum*, and when the woman in question is an *ervah* who could in principle have been married to that brother. Remove any one of those elements — as is structurally the case with the six more severe *arayot*, who could never have been married to the brother at all — and the co-wife prohibition simply does not arise. The entire institution of *tzara* is, at its root, a function of *ach*, of brotherhood, and nothing more.

## Onesh Punishment



### Onesh Minayin

The *baraita* now transitions from prohibition to punishment. *Azharah shamanu* — we have heard that there is a prohibition: from the verse *isha el achota lo tikach litzror* read in light of the word *aleha*, we learn that a wife's sister remains forbidden even in a *yibbum* context. But how do we know what punishment applies? *Onesh minayin?* — from where do we derive that if one nonetheless goes ahead and performs *yibbum* with an *ervah*, the full punishment is in force? One might have thought that since a *mitzvah* is involved, the punishment is somehow attenuated.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַזְהָרָה שָׁמַעְנוּ, עוֹנֶשׁ מִנַּיִין? אָמַר קְרָא: ״כִּי כׇּל (אִישׁ) אֲשֶׁר יַעֲשֶׂה מִכֹּל הַתּוֹעֵבוֹת וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* adds: **We have learned the warning** concerning this prohibition that the *yavam* may not marry his forbidden relative from the verse: “And you shall not take a woman to her sister, to be a rival to her, to uncover her nakedness, with her in her lifetime” (Leviticus 18:18). **From where** is **the punishment** that he incurs if he transgresses and marries her derived? **The verse states: “For whoever shall do any** of these **abominations,** the souls that do them shall be cut off from among their people” (Leviticus 18:29).

### Kareit Yibbum


The verse provides the answer: *amar kra ki kol asher ya'aseh mikol hato'evot ha'eleh venichretu* — the verse says, whoever does any of these abominations shall be cut off. This *kareit* passage comes at the conclusion of the entire enumeration of *arayot*, and that enumeration includes *isha el achota lo tikach litzror*. Since the prohibition of the wife's sister includes the *yibbum* context — as we established through the word *aleha* — and the punishment of *kareit* is attached to the entire list of *arayot*, the *kareit* applies equally in the *yibbum* context. The word *litzror* further teaches that the prohibition and its punishment extend to the *tzara* as well. And once we know that having relations with the *tzara* in a *yibbum* context is forbidden and carries *kareit*, it follows that the *tzara* herself acquires the status of *eshet ach* — a forbidden relation — once there is no *mitzvah* of *yibbum* attaching to her.

It is worth pausing here to note the distinction between the terms *ervah* and *arayot*, since both have been in play throughout this discussion. *Arayot* is the plural, and can refer abstractly to the entire category of forbidden sexual relations. *Ervah*, by contrast, functions as a kind of status-label attached to a particular person relative to a particular man: the sister is not an *ervah* to the world at large, but she is an *ervah* to her brother. The *Gemara* and the *pesukim* it follows consistently treat the matter from this angle — not that the relationship is incestuous in the abstract, but that this specific woman is forbidden to this specific man. That framing, it should be acknowledged, reflects the thoroughly male-oriented perspective embedded in the entire halakhic discussion.

## Hava Amina



### Meichdi Reasoning

With the *baraita* concluded, the *Gemara* steps back to analyze the logical foundations of the entire exercise. *Meichdi* — let us think about this: *ta'ama dechtav rachmana aleha* — it is because the Torah wrote *aleha* that we know *yibbum* does not apply in the case of an *ervah*. *Halav hacha hava amina achot isha meyabemet* — were it not for that verse, I would have thought that you could indeed perform *yibbum* with a wife's sister. But why would I have thought such a thing?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** טַעְמָא דִּכְתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״עָלֶיהָ״, הָא לָאו הָכִי, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה מִיַּיבְּמָה, מַאי טַעְמָא? דְּאָמְרִינַן: אָתֵי עֲשֵׂה וְדָחֵי לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה?!
> **Translation:** § Up until this point, the Gemara has cited the *baraita* that interprets the biblical basis for the *halakha* of the mishna. The Gemara further analyzes the foundational principles of this topic. **The reason** that these women are exempt from levirate marriage is **that the Merciful One writes “with her,”** from which it may be inferred that **if that was not so, I would say** that **a wife’s sister enters into levirate marriage** with her sister’s husband. **What is the reason** that one would have assumed that this is the case? It is **as we say,** in accordance with a principle, that **a positive** mitzva **comes and overrides a prohibition.** In this case, the positive mitzva to enter into levirate marriage overrides the prohibition against marrying one’s wife’s sister.

### Asei Dochei


*Mai ta'ama?* — what was the reasoning behind that *hava amina*? *Amrinan ati asei vedachi lo ta'asei* — I might have said: a positive *mitzvah* comes and overrides a negative prohibition. The *mitzvah* of *yibbum* is a positive commandment; the prohibition of a wife's sister is a negative prohibition. Hence the *hava amina* that the *mitzvah* might override the prohibition.

### Kareit Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵימַר דְּאָמְרִינַן אָתֵי עֲשֵׂה וְדָחֵי לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה — לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה גְּרֵידָא, לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה שֶׁיֵּשׁ בּוֹ כָּרֵת מִי דָּחֵי? וְתוּ: לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה גְּרֵידָא מְנָלַן דְּדָחֵי —
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks if that principle is applicable in this case. One can **say that we said** that **a positive** mitzva **comes and overrides a prohibition** only when there is **a prohibition** for which one is punished by lashes **alone.** However, with regard to **a prohibition that includes** the punishment of ***karet*, does** a positive mitzva **override** it? This prohibition is more severe than a regular one, and therefore perhaps a positive mitzva does not override it. **And furthermore,** with regard to **a prohibition** for which one is punished by lashes **alone, from where do we** derive **that** a positive mitzva **overrides** it?


But the *Gemara* immediately pushes back on this *hava amina*: *eima de'amrinan asei vidachei lo ta'asei* — when do we say a positive *mitzvah* overrides a negative? *Lo ta'asei greida* — only when it is a straightforward negative prohibition, not a particularly weighty one. *Lo ta'asei she'yesh bo kareit* — a negative prohibition that carries *kareit* — *mi dachi?* — does a positive *mitzvah* ever override that? The principle of *asei dochei lo ta'asei* does not apply to prohibitions of such severity.

One might respond: but the *mitzvah* of *yibbum* already overrides one prohibition that carries *kareit* — namely *eshet ach*. If it overrides that, perhaps it could override other *arayot* as well? That is indeed the most natural formulation of the *hava amina*. However, the *Gemara* has not yet reached that point. First it wants to establish the more fundamental question: where do we know the principle of *asei dochei lo ta'asei* at all? Understanding that principle is essential to understanding why anyone might have thought *yibbum* could override *arayot* — and why ultimately it cannot when *kareit* is involved.

## Shatnez-Tzitzit



### Velo Litzor

The *Gemara* now opens a broader inquiry: *lo ta'asei greida minalan de'dachi?* — from where do we even know that a positive *mitzvah* overrides a straightforward negative prohibition? This takes the discussion temporarily away from *yibbum*, but the purpose is clear: to establish the principle of *asei dochei lo ta'asei* so that we can understand the *hava amina* that *yibbum* might have overridden the prohibition of *arayot*.

[DAF:4a]
### Lo Tilbash

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּכְתִיב: ״לֹא תִלְבַּשׁ שַׁעַטְנֵז״, ״גְּדִלִים תַּעֲשֶׂה לָּךְ״.
> **Translation:** **As it is written: “You shall not wear diverse kinds** of wool and linen together. **You shall make for yourself twisted fringes** on the four corners of your covering with which you cover yourself” (Deuteronomy 22:11–12). These verses teach that despite the prohibition against wearing diverse kinds of wool and linen, it is permitted to prepare ritual fringes of diverse kinds, e.g., sky-blue dyed threads of wool on linen garments. This shows that the positive mitzva of ritual fringes overrides the prohibition of diverse kinds.

The *Gemara* answers: *de'ksiv lo tilbash shatnez, gedilim ta'aseh lach* — because the verse says do not wear *shatnez*, and the very next verse says make yourself fringes. *Shatnez* is the prohibition of mixing wool and linen; *tzitzit* may require a wool string on a linen garment, which would constitute *shatnez*. The juxtaposition teaches that the positive *mitzvah* of *tzitzit* overrides the negative prohibition of *shatnez*.

### Smuchim Drasha

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: סְמוּכִים מִן הַתּוֹרָה מִנַּיִן, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״סְמוּכִים לָעַד לְעוֹלָם עֲשׂוּיִם בֶּאֱמֶת וְיָשָׁר״.
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Elazar said: From where** in the Torah is it derived that one may draw homiletical interpretations from the **juxtaposition** of verses? In other words, from where is it derived that the fact that certain verses are adjacent one to the other is a reason to apply the *halakhot* from one verse to the other? **As it is stated:** “The works of His hands in truth and justice, all His commandments are sure. **Juxtaposed forever and ever, made in truth and uprightness”** (Psalms 111:7–8). This verse indicates that it is appropriate to draw inferences from the juxtaposition of God’s commandments.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת אָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר בֶּן עֲזַרְיָה: מִנַּיִן לִיבָמָה שֶׁנָּפְלָה לִפְנֵי מוּכֵּה שְׁחִין שֶׁאֵין חוֹסְמִין אוֹתָהּ — שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״לֹא תַחְסוֹם שׁוֹר בְּדִישׁוֹ״, וּסְמִיךְ לֵיהּ: ״כִּי יֵשְׁבוּ אַחִים יַחְדָּיו״.
> **Translation:** **And** similarly, **Rav Sheshet said** that **Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya: From where** is it derived with regard **to a *yevama* who came before** a *yavam* **afflicted with boils that one may not muzzle her,** i.e., she cannot be forced to enter into levirate marriage, and he is compelled to release her by *ḥalitza*? **As it is stated: “You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the corn”** (Deuteronomy 25:4), **and, juxtaposed to it,** is the verse: **“If brothers dwell together”** (Deuteronomy 25:5), which begins the passage that deals with the *halakhot* of levirate marriage. This teaches that just as it is prohibited to muzzle the ox, so too, one may not muzzle and ignore the complaints of a *yevama* who does not wish to marry a *yavam* afflicted with boils.

But how do we know that juxtaposition in the Torah is a legitimate hermeneutic tool? *Ve'amar Rabbi Elazar: smuchin min hatorah minayin?* — from where is the principle of *smuchin* — learning from textual juxtapositions — itself derived? *Shene'emar smuchim la'ad le'olam asuyim be'emes veyashar* — as the verse in *Tehillim* says, they are joined forever, made in truth and uprightness — which the *Gemara* reads as endorsing the method of *smuchin*. Thus the juxtaposition of *shatnez* and *gedilim ta'aseh lach* is a valid source for the principle that *asei dochei lo ta'aseh*.

At this point the *Gemara* introduces a related teaching. *Ve'amar Rav Sheshes amar Rabbi Elazar mishum Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah: minayin leyevama she'nafla lifnei Mukeh Shechin she'ein chosmin oto* — from where do we know that if a *yevama* falls before a *yavam* who is a repugnant person, such as one afflicted with severe boils, you do not silence her? *Shene'emar lo tachsom shor be'disho* — do not muzzle an ox while it threshes — *vesamich lei ki yeshvu achim yachdav* — and immediately juxtaposed to it is the passage establishing the *mitzvah* of *yibbum*. This *smuchin* teaches that we attend to the woman's objections and ensure that *chalitzah* is performed rather than *yibbum*. This is a significant point: although so much of this entire *tractate* is framed from the man's perspective — who is permitted to whom, where a *mitzvah* applies — this *drasha* establishes at the very outset that the woman has a voice and that her protest is legally cognizable.

## Smuchim General



### Reb Yehuda Position

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: אֲפִילּוּ לְמַאן דְּלָא דָּרֵישׁ סְמוּכִים בְּעָלְמָא, בְּמִשְׁנֵה תוֹרָה דָּרֵישׁ. דְּהָא רַבִּי יְהוּדָה בְּעָלְמָא לָא דָּרֵישׁ, וּבְמִשְׁנֵה תוֹרָה דָּרֵישׁ.
> **Translation:** **And Rav Yosef said: Even according to the one who does not generally derive homiletic interpretations from juxtaposed** verses, nevertheless, he does **derive** them **from Deuteronomy, as Rabbi Yehuda does not generally derive homiletic interpretations** from juxtaposed verses, **and** yet **he does derive** them **from Deuteronomy.**

The *Gemara* then notes: *afilu lema'an delo darash smuchin be'alma, be'Mishneh Torah darash* — even one who does not generally make *drashot* from *smuchin* will do so in *Mishneh Torah* — that is, in *Devarim*, where *Moshe Rabbeinu* recaps the earlier laws. This is directly relevant because the juxtaposition of *shatnez* and *tzitzit* appears in *Devarim*, which means that even those who reject *smuchin* as a general hermeneutic accept this particular *drasha* and therefore accept the principle of *asei dochei lo ta'aseh*.

Who is it that generally rejects *smuchin*? *Detanya: Rabbi Yehuda be'alma lo darash* — we know from a *baraita* that *Rabbi Yehuda* in general does not derive *halacha* from juxtaposition.

### Ben Azzai vs Yehuda

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבְעָלְמָא מְנָלַן דְּלָא דָּרֵישׁ? דְּתַנְיָא: בֶּן עַזַּאי אוֹמֵר, נֶאֱמַר: ״מְכַשֵּׁפָה לֹא תְחַיֶּה״, וְנֶאֱמַר: ״כׇּל שׁוֹכֵב עִם בְּהֵמָה מוֹת יוּמָת״, סְמָכוֹ עִנְיָן לוֹ: מָה שׁוֹכֵב עִם בְּהֵמָה בִּסְקִילָה — אַף מְכַשֵּׁפָה בִּסְקִילָה.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks: **And from where do we** derive that Rabbi Yehuda **generally does not derive homiletic interpretations** from juxtaposed verses? **As it is taught** in a *baraita* with regard to the punishment of a sorceress that **ben Azzai says** that **it is stated: “You shall not allow a sorceress to live”** (Exodus 22:17), although the manner of her execution is not specified, **and it is stated: “Whoever lies with a beast shall surely be put to death”** (Exodus 22:18). The Torah **juxtaposed this matter to that** so as to say: **Just as one who lies with a beast** is executed **by stoning** (see Leviticus 20:16), **so too, a sorceress** is executed **by stoning.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לוֹ רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: וְכִי מִפְּנֵי שֶׁסְּמָכוֹ עִנְיָן לוֹ, נוֹצִיא זֶה לִסְקִילָה?
> **Translation:** With regard to this proof, **Rabbi Yehuda said to** ben Azzai: **And** simply **due to** the fact **that** the Torah **juxtaposed this matter to that** one, **shall we take this** person **out to be stoned?** Should he be sentenced to the most severe of the death penalties on the basis of a juxtaposition of passages?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא: אוֹב וְיִדְּעוֹנִי בִּכְלַל מְכַשְּׁפִים הָיוּ, וְלָמָּה יָצְאוּ? לְהַקִּישׁ לָהֶם וְלוֹמַר לָךְ: מָה אוֹב וְיִדְּעוֹנִי בִּסְקִילָה — אַף מְכַשֵּׁפָה בִּסְקִילָה.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** Rabbi Yehuda claims that the source is the following statement: **Mediums and wizards were included among all sorcerers. And why were they singled out** from the rest in the verse: “And a man or a woman who is a medium or a wizard shall surely be put to death; they shall stone them with stones, their blood is upon them” (Leviticus 20:27)? It is to **draw an analogy to them and say to you: Just as a medium and a wizard** are executed **by stoning, so too, a sorceress** is executed **by stoning.** This shows that Rabbi Yehuda does not derive homiletic interpretations from juxtaposed verses.

The *baraita* spells this out: *Ben Azzai omer: ne'emar machasheifa lo techayeh* — it says, do not suffer a sorceress to live — *vene'emar kol shochev im behemah mot yumat* — and it is juxtaposed to: whoever lies with an animal shall be put to death. *Samchu inyan lo* — they juxtaposed the two. *Mah shochev im behemah be'skilah* — just as lying with an animal is punishable by stoning — *af machasheifa be'skilah* — so too a sorceress is executed by stoning. This *smuchin* derivation appears in *Shemot* in *Parshat Mishpatim*, not in *Devarim*.

*Amar lei Rabbi Yehuda: ve'chi mipnei she'samchu inyan lo notzi zeh le'skilah?* — *Rabbi Yehuda* objects: just because they are juxtaposed we are going to stone someone? Juxtaposition alone is not a sufficient basis for a ruling of such gravity. Many things are juxtaposed in the Torah — in *Parshat Kedoshim* alone, the most disparate subjects appear side by side — and that cannot automatically serve as the basis for a *drasha*.

*Ela* — rather, says *Rabbi Yehuda*, I agree the sorceress is stoned, but not because of *smuchin*. *Ov veyidoni be'chlal machasheifim hayu* — the *ov* and *yidoni*, two forms of necromancy, were in the general category of sorcery. *Velama yatz'u* — why were they singled out? *Ba'even yirgemu otam dmeihem bam* — because the Torah states explicitly that they are stoned. *Lehakish lahem* — they were extracted from the general category in order to serve as a comparison: *mah ov veyidoni be'skilah af machasheifa be'skilah* — just as *ov* and *yidoni* are stoned, so too the sorceress. This is not *smuchin* but rather the standard hermeneutic of a *davar shehaya be'chlal veyatza min hakhlal lelamed* — a matter included in a general rule that was singled out to teach about the whole category — which is one of the standard thirteen hermeneutic principles. *Smuchin*, by contrast, is not among those thirteen principles, which is why *Rabbi Yehuda* refuses to employ it.

### Mishneh Torah Rule

*U've'Mishneh Torah minalan de'darash?* — but how do we know that *Rabbi Yehuda* is willing to use *smuchin* in *Devarim*? *De'tanan* — because we learned in the *mishna* what follows.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבְמִשְׁנֵה תוֹרָה מְנָלַן דְּדָרֵישׁ? דִּתְנַן: נוֹשֵׂא אָדָם אֲנוּסַת אָבִיו וּמְפוּתַּת אָבִיו, אֲנוּסַת בְּנוֹ וּמְפוּתַּת בְּנוֹ. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹסֵר בַּאֲנוּסַת אָבִיו וּמְפוּתַּת אָבִיו.
> **Translation:** § **And from where do we** derive **that** Rabbi Yehuda **does derive** homiletic interpretations **in Deuteronomy? As we learned** in a mishna: **A person may wed** a woman **raped by his father and** one **seduced by his father,** despite the fact that his father’s wife is forbidden to him. Similarly, he may marry a woman **raped by his** son **and** one **seduced by his son.** Although one is prohibited by Torah law from marrying the wife of his father or the wife of his son, these prohibitions do not apply to a woman raped or seduced by them. **And Rabbi Yehuda prohibits** him from marrying **a woman raped by his father and a woman seduced by his father.**

## Anusas Aviw



### Mefutat Aviv


The *mishna* states: *noseh adam anusat aviv umefutat aviv, anusat beno umefutat beno* — a man may marry a woman his father raped or seduced, and a woman his son raped or seduced. The prohibition of *eshet av* — father's wife — and *eshet beno* — daughter-in-law — does not extend to women with whom the father or son had non-marital sexual relations. These are simply not included in those prohibitions.

### Reb Yehuda Assur

*Rabbi Yehuda oser be'anusat aviv umefutat aviv* — *Rabbi Yehuda* disagrees in the case of a woman the father had sexual relations with, whether by rape or seduction, and holds she is forbidden. He agrees, however, that a woman the son had relations with does not become forbidden to the father.

### Knaf Interpretation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַב גִּידֵּל אָמַר רַב: מַאי טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, דִּכְתִיב: ״לֹא יִקַּח אִישׁ אֶת אֵשֶׁת אָבִיו וְלֹא יְגַלֶּה כְּנַף אָבִיו״, כָּנָף שֶׁרָאָה אָבִיו — לֹא יְגַלֶּה.
> **Translation:** **And Rav Giddel said** that **Rav said: What is the reason for Rabbi Yehuda’s** opinion? **As it is written: “A man shall not take his father’s wife, and shall not uncover his father’s skirt”** (Deuteronomy 23:1). The latter expression: “And shall not uncover his father’s skirt,” is referring to **a skirt that has been seen by his father,** i.e., any woman who has had relations with his father **may not be uncovered** by his son, meaning that his son may not marry her.


*Ve'amar Rav Gidal amar Rav: mai ta'ama de'Rebbe Yehuda?* — what is the basis for *Rabbi Yehuda's* position? The verse says: *lo yikach ish et eshet aviv velo yigaleh knaf aviv* — a man shall not marry his father's wife and shall not uncover the hem of his father. The phrase *velo yigaleh knaf aviv* — shall not uncover his father's hem — is initially puzzling. On one reading, it describes the wrongness of the act: this is the woman under whose garment-hem the father was; she is, as it were, *knaf aviv* — the covering of his father. On *Rabbi Yehuda's* reading, the phrase *velo yigaleh knaf aviv* — he shall not uncover any nakedness that was his father's — extends the prohibition beyond the formal category of *eshet av* to any woman the father had sexual relations with. The logic follows from the very meaning of *knaf*: what matters is not the formal marital bond but the fact of the father's intimacy with this woman.

### Smuchim Anusa



> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִמַּאי דְּבַאֲנוּסָה כְּתִיב — מֵעִילָּוֵיהּ דִּקְרָא, דִּכְתִיב: ״וְנָתַן הָאִישׁ הַשּׁוֹכֵב עִמָּהּ לַאֲבִי הַנַּעֲרָה חֲמִשִּׁים כָּסֶף״, וּסְמִיךְ לֵיהּ: ״לֹא יִקַּח אִישׁ וְגוֹ׳״.
> **Translation:** **And from where** is it known **that** the verse **is written with regard to a woman raped by his father?** It is **from the previous** verse, which deals with the *halakhot* of rape, **as it is written: “And the man who lay with her must give the maiden’s father fifty shekels of silver”** (Deuteronomy 22:29), **and juxtaposed to it** is the verse: **“A man shall not take his father’s wife** and shall not uncover his father’s skirt.” This shows that Rabbi Yehuda does derive homiletic interpretations from juxtaposed verses in Deuteronomy.

Now the *smuchin* dimension enters. *U'mimai de'be'anusa ktiv?* — how do we know that the verse is referring specifically to a woman the father had relations with outside of marriage? *Me'iluya de'kraya* — because of what is written immediately before the verse in question. The *parasha* preceding *lo yikach ish et eshet aviv* is the passage about a man who rapes an unmarried woman: *venatan ha'ish hashochev imah la'avi hana'arah chamishim kasef* — and the man who lay with her shall pay fifty pieces of silver to the girl's father. It is the case of *anusa* — a raped woman — that is discussed just before *lo yikach ish et eshet aviv*. *Vesamich lei* — and then this prohibition is juxtaposed to it. Thus, *Rabbi Yehuda*, reading *velo yigaleh knaf aviv* in light of the immediately preceding context of *anusa*, understands the phrase as extending the prohibition to women the father had extra-marital relations with.

It is worth pausing here, though, because there is something subtle going on. One might have argued that *Rabbi Yehuda* does not need *smuchin* at all for this reading — that *velo yigaleh knaf aviv* is simply opaque as a standalone phrase, and the only way to make sense of what it means is through the surrounding context. On that view, reading the preceding *anusa* passage to clarify the meaning of *knaf aviv* is just ordinary *pshat*, not *smuchin*. But the *Gemara*'s point is precisely that the phrase is opaque, and it is the juxtaposition — the proximity of the *anusa* passage — that is doing the interpretive work. It is because the two *parashot* are placed next to each other that we derive the meaning of *velo yigaleh knaf aviv*. That is *smuchin*. The opacity of the phrase, rather than undermining the *smuchin* argument, may actually be what makes it clearest here: there is no other way to arrive at the meaning except through the juxtaposition.

This is, in fact, a use of *smuchin*. But it is *smuchin* in *Devarim* — this passage appears in *Parshat Ki Tetzei* in *Devarim* — which is exactly the point the *Gemara* is making: *Rabbi Yehuda*, who rejects *smuchin* in general, accepts it in *Mishneh Torah*.

### Rabbis Counter

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן: אִי הֲוָה סְמִיךְ לֵיהּ, כִּדְקָאָמְרַתְּ. הַשְׁתָּא דְּלָא סְמִיךְ לֵיהּ (דִּכְתִיב ״לֹא יִקַּח אִישׁ אֶת אֵשֶׁת אָבִיו״ בֵּנְתַיִם) —
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks: **And** how do **the Rabbis,** who disagree with the opinion of Rabbi Yehuda, respond to this argument? They say: **If** the two verses were fully **juxtaposed,** it would be interpreted **as you said.** However, **now** that **it is not** properly **juxtaposed, as it is written: “A man shall not take his father’s wife,” in between** the *halakhot* of rape and the pronouncement with regard to uncovering one’s father’s garment, this serves to break the juxtaposition.

*Ve'rabbanan* — and what would the Rabbis respond? They would say: *ee hava samich lei ke'de'ka'amrat* — if it were truly juxtaposed immediately, as you say, you would be right. *Hashata de'lo samich lei* — but now that it is not directly juxtaposed, as the gloss makes clear — *de'chtiv lo yikach ish et eshet aviv beintayim* — since the verse *lo yikach ish et eshet aviv* intervenes between the *anusa* passage and *velo yigaleh knaf aviv* — it is not sufficiently proximate to derive the teaching through *smuchin*.

### Shomeret Yavam


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּשׁוֹמֶרֶת יָבָם הַכָּתוּב מְדַבֵּר, וְלַעֲבוֹר עָלָיו בִּשְׁנֵי לָאוִין.
> **Translation:** Consequently, this particular **verse** concerning the uncovering of one’s father’s garment **is speaking of a woman waiting for her *yavam*,** in this case one’s father. In other words, the *yevama* of a father who is waiting for levirate marriage to the father is already considered “his father’s skirt,” and she is therefore forbidden to the son. Although this woman who is awaiting levirate marriage is in fact his uncle’s wife and explicitly prohibited to him in any case, this passage comes to teach that **he violates two prohibitions.** In other words, were he to engage in relations with her he would be penalized both for relations with his uncle’s wife and relations with “his father’s skirt.”

If so, what does the Rabbis' reading of *velo yigaleh knaf aviv* refer to? *Beshomeret yavam hakatuv medaber* — the verse is speaking of a *shomeret yavam*, a woman who is quasi-married to the father by virtue of the bond of *zikah*. That is: if the father's brother died childless, the father now has a *mitzvah* of *yibbum* toward his brother's widow. That widow is in a quasi-marital relationship with the father, since *zikah* — the *yibbum* bond — is like a form of inchoate marriage. *Velo yigaleh knaf aviv* then teaches that the son may not have relations with that woman either — she is effectively in the domain of the father.

The *Gemara* then notes: but you do not really need a verse to prohibit this, because that woman is the son's aunt. The son is already forbidden to his aunt by a separate *issur*. The verse must therefore be coming to add a second prohibition — *lav aleiha bishnei lavin* — so that there are two distinct negative prohibitions applying to the act, not merely to spell out a novel *halacha*.

## Muchach Mufneh



### Muchach Argument


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבְמִשְׁנֵה תוֹרָה מַאי טַעְמָא דְּדָרֵישׁ? אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא — מִשּׁוּם דְּמוֹכַח, וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא — מִשּׁוּם דְּמוּפְנֵי.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks: **But** as Rabbi Yehuda does not generally derive homiletic interpretations from juxtaposed verses, **what is the reason that he derives** these interpretations **in Deuteronomy?** The Gemara responds: **If you wish, say** that it is **because** it **is evident** from the context; **and if you wish, say** instead that it is **because** this verse is extraneous and is therefore **free** for this inference.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא מִשּׁוּם דְּמוֹכַח — דְּאִם כֵּן לִכְתְּבֵיהּ רַחֲמָנָא גַּבֵּי עֲרָיוֹת. וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא מִשּׁוּם דְּמוּפְנֵי — דְּאִם כֵּן לִכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא: ״לֹא יִקַּח אִישׁ אֶת אֵשֶׁת אָבִיו״, ״לֹא יְגַלֶּה כְּנַף אָבִיו״ לְמָה לִי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates: **If you wish, say** it is **because** it **is evident; as, if** it is **so** that the verse did not intend to teach by juxtaposition, **let the Merciful One write** this *halakha* prohibiting marriage to a father’s wife **alongside** the other women **with whom relations are forbidden,** in Leviticus. Since this verse is out of place, it is certainly coming to teach by way of juxtaposition. **And if you wish, say** instead that it is **because** this verse is **free, as, if** it is **so** that the verse is not coming to teach an additional *halakha*, **let the Merciful One write** only: **“A man shall not take his father’s wife.” Why do I** need the phrase: **“And shall not uncover his father’s skirt”?** This phrase is superfluous, and therefore it teaches by juxtaposition.

The *Gemara* now returns to the question of why *smuchin* is valid in *Mishneh Torah* in the first place. *U've'Mishneh Torah mai ta'ama de'darash?* — why does everybody agree to *smuchin* in *Devarim*? *Ee ba'it eima mishum de'muchach* — one answer: because it is evident from context. What does that mean?

*De'im ken lichtov rachmana gabei arayot* — if the Torah wished to introduce the prohibition of *velo yigaleh knaf aviv* as a free-standing *ervah*, it should have written it back where it lists the *arayot* in *Acharei Mot* and *Kedoshim*. Why is it appearing here for the first time, in a different context, in *Devarim*? The very fact that this prohibition is introduced here — and not in its more natural location among the *arayot* — signals that it is here to communicate something specific to this context. The juxtaposition itself is communicating. When *Moshe Rabbeinu* recaps the laws of the Torah in *Devarim*, he sometimes frames things differently, places them in new sequences, and introduces them in new contexts. The *Gemara* treats this as hermeneutically significant: if something appears in *Devarim* that was not present in its natural earlier context, it is appearing here because this context is intended to teach us something that the earlier context could not. The new placement is itself the message.

### Mufneh Argument

*Ve'ee ba'it eima mishum de'mufni* — alternatively, because it is superfluous. *De'im ken lichtov rachmana lo yikach ish et eshet aviv velo yigaleh knaf* — the Torah could have simply said do not uncover the hem, without the word *aviv* — of his father. That is already obvious from the surrounding context. So the word *aviv* is extra, and something extra invites *drasha*. By being extra, it licenses us to read it in light of the adjacent *parasha* about *anusa* and derive a law from the juxtaposition.

The *Gemara* acknowledges, however, that this second argument — *mufneh* — is less distinctive to *Mishneh Torah*. Extra words appear throughout the Torah, and learning from *smuchin* on the basis of a superfluous word is not something unique to *Devarim*. The argument merely says: here is a spare word, and we will use that as license to *darshen*. That kind of move is available anywhere in the Torah where a redundancy appears. By contrast, the argument from *muchach* — the idea that something newly appearing in *Devarim* without having been in its original context must be communicating something specific to its new surroundings — is genuinely distinctive to *Mishneh Torah*. That is what makes *Devarim* a uniquely rich context for *smuchin*-based *drashot*, and it is the stronger of the two arguments for why *smuchin* works there. *Shema mina le'afnuyei* — we conclude that the extra word was made superfluous precisely in order to enable the *drasha*.

### Tzemer Upishtim

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לְאַפְנוֹיֵי. וְגַבֵּי צִיצִית נָמֵי, אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא — מִשּׁוּם דְּמוֹכַח, וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא — מִשּׁוּם דְּמוּפְנֶה.
> **Translation:** **Learn from this that** this phrase is **free** to teach an additional *halakha*. **And with regard to ritual fringes as well,** there is a particular reason to derive a homiletic interpretation from the juxtaposition of verses. **If you wish, say** that it is **because** it **is evident** from the context, **and if you wish, say** instead that it is **because** this verse is **free** for this inference.

The *Gemara* now applies the same two arguments — *muchach* and *mufneh* — to the case of *shatnez* and *tzitzit* in *Devarim*. *Ee ba'it eima mishum de'muchach: de'im ken lichtov rachmana gabei parashat tzitzit* — if there was nothing to derive from context, why is the prohibition of *shatnez* appearing here in *Devarim* alongside *tzitzit*? *Gedilim ta'aseh lach* was already written back in *Parshat Shelach* in *Bamidbar*. Why repeat it here? The new context itself communicates: the juxtaposition is intentional, and it teaches that the *mitzvah* of *tzitzit* overrides the prohibition of *shatnez*.

*Ve'ee ba'it eima mishum de'mufni* — or alternatively, something in the verse is superfluous. The Torah already wrote in *Vayikra: uveged kilayim shatnez lo ya'aleh alecha*. The prohibition of *shatnez* is already there. *Lo tilbash shatnez lama li?* — why does it need to repeat the prohibition here in *Devarim*? The repetition is superfluous, and that superfluity licenses the *drasha* from *smuchin*. *Shema mina le'afnuyei* — conclude from this that it was made extra for the purpose of enabling the *drasha*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא מִשּׁוּם דְּמוֹכַח — דְּאִם כֵּן לִכְתְּבֵיהּ רַחֲמָנָא גַּבֵּי פָּרָשַׁת צִיצִית, לְמַאי הִלְכְתָא כַּתְבֵיהּ הָכָא? וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא מִשּׁוּם דְּמוּפְנֶה — מִכְּדִי כְּתַב: ״וּבֶגֶד כִּלְאַיִם שַׁעַטְנֵז לֹא יַעֲלֶה עָלֶיךָ״, ״לֹא תִלְבַּשׁ שַׁעַטְנֵז״ לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לְאַפְנוֹיֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates: **If you wish, say** it is **because** it **is evident; as, if** it is **so** that no inference should be drawn from the adjacent verses, **let the Merciful One write** this verse **by the portion of ritual fringes** (Numbers, chapter 15). With regard **to what *halakha*** did the Torah **write it here?** Clearly, the Torah is teaching a *halakha* from the adjacent verses. **And if you wish, say** it is **because** this verse is **free, since** the Torah has already **written: “Neither shall there come upon you a garment of diverse kinds mingled together”** (Leviticus 19:19). **Why,** then, **do I** need the verse: **“You shall not wear diverse kinds,** wool and linen together” (Deuteronomy 22:11)? **Learn from this that** the verse is **free** for the derivation of a homiletic interpretation from juxtaposed verses.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנֵי מִצְרָךְ צְרִיכִי, דְּאִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״לֹא יַעֲלֶה עָלֶיךָ״, הָוֵה אָמֵינָא: כׇּל דֶּרֶךְ הַעֲלָאָה אֲסַר רַחֲמָנָא, וַאֲפִילּוּ מוֹכְרֵי כְסוּת, כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״לֹא תִלְבַּשׁ שַׁעַטְנֵז״, דּוּמְיָא דִלְבִישָׁה, דְּאִית בֵּיהּ הֲנָאָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this explanation: Both of **these** verses **are necessary, as, had the Merciful One written** only: **“Neither shall there come upon you,” I would say** that **the Merciful One prohibits every manner by** which a garment of diverse kinds **comes upon** you, **and** this applies **even** to **sellers of coverings,** who do not wear the garments but merely rest them on their shoulders. Therefore, **the Merciful One writes: “You shall not wear diverse kinds,”** to teach that the prohibition applies only in cases **similar to wearing, which provides benefit** and does not simply involve placing the covering upon oneself.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״לֹא תִלְבַּשׁ״, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: דַּוְקָא לְבִישָׁה, דִּנְפִישׁ הֲנָיָיתַהּ, אֲבָל הַעֲלָאָה — לָא, כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״לֹא יַעֲלֶה עָלֶיךָ״.
> **Translation:** **And had the Merciful One written** only: **“You shall not wear,” I would say** that this means **specifically wearing, which** provides **significant benefit,** both warmth and adornment, **but** merely **placing** a garment of diverse kinds upon oneself is **not** prohibited, even if one is warmed by the clothing. Therefore, **the Merciful One writes: “Neither shall there come upon you.”**

### Tilbash Elevation


The *Gemara* challenges this: *hani mitzterechei* — you actually need both verses, and neither is superfluous. Here is why. *Dichasav rachmana lo ya'aleh alecha* — the verse in *Vayikra* says do not put [*shatnez*] upon you. *Hava amina kol derech ha'ala'ah asrah rachmana* — I would have thought: any form of placing *shatnez* on oneself is forbidden, even if it is not in a mode of wearing. *Va'afilu mochrei chsut* — even the fabric merchants who carry many garments stacked on their bodies in order to transport them. *Kasav rachmana lo tilbash shatnez* — therefore the Torah says in *Devarim*: do not *wear* *shatnez*. The verb *tilbash* — to wear — tells me it must be something comparable to genuine wearing: *dumya de'levisha de'it bah hana'ah* — similar to wearing in that there is tangible benefit and pleasure, not merely the act of placing it on oneself for transport purposes. It is worth being precise about what this means in practice. The requirement is not strictly limited to standard single-layer wearing, nor does *lo ya'aleh alecha* forbid every conceivable form of placing *shatnez* on the body. What emerges from the combination of the two verses is a functional standard: the act must be one that performs the same role as wearing — providing warmth or bodily coverage — regardless of whether it technically constitutes classic *levisha*. So someone who puts on ten garments simultaneously for the purpose of transporting them does not transgress, because despite the physical contact the purpose is not benefit of the wearing kind; but someone who covers himself with a blanket of *shatnez* would be captured by *lo ya'aleh alecha*, since that act does provide genuine body-benefit even though it is not *levisha* in the strict sense.

### Hana'ah Levels

Conversely: *de'ai khasav rachmana lo tilbash* — had the Torah only said do not wear, *hava amina davka levisha de'nafish hana'ah* — I would have said only actual wearing, where the benefit is substantial, is forbidden. *Aval ha'ala'ah* — but a blanket placed over oneself, *de'lo nafish hana'ah* — where the benefit is lesser — *la* — perhaps that would not be forbidden under *shatnez*. So *kasav rachmana lo ya'aleh alecha* — the verse in *Vayikra* comes to extend the prohibition to cases of placing on oneself that are not technically *levisha* but still involve a recognizable benefit. Each verse therefore carries independent work: *lo ya'aleh alecha* extends the prohibition beyond wearing; *lo tilbash* restricts it to acts that involve genuine personal benefit, excluding mere transport.

### Gezeirah Shavah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם כֵּן, לִכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא ״לֹא תִלְבַּשׁ שַׁעַטְנֵז״, ״צֶמֶר וּפִשְׁתִּים״ לְמָה לִי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: In any case part of the verse is superfluous, as, **if so, let the Merciful One write** only: **“You shall not wear diverse kinds.” Why do I** need the addition of **“wool and linen”?** The definition of diverse kinds in clothing is already known from another source.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִכְּדִי כְּתַב ״וּבֶגֶד כִּלְאַיִם שַׁעַטְנֵז לֹא יַעֲלֶה עָלֶיךָ״, וְתָנָא דְּבֵי רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל: הוֹאִיל וְנֶאֶמְרוּ בַּתּוֹרָה סְתָם ״בְּגָדִים״, וּפָרַט לְךָ הַכָּתוּב בְּאֶחָד מֵהֶן צֶמֶר וּפִשְׁתִּים — אַף כֹּל צֶמֶר וּפִשְׁתִּים, ״צֶמֶר וּפִשְׁתִּים״ דִּכְתַב רַחֲמָנָא לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לְאַפְנוֹיֵי.
> **Translation:** How so? **Since it is written: “Neither shall there come upon you a garment of diverse kinds mingled together”** (Leviticus 19:19), **and the school of Rabbi Yishmael taught: Since** the word **garments is stated in the Torah unmodified,** without stating from what materials those garments were made, **and the verse specified in one of** its references to garments, in the context of the *halakhot* of ritual impurity of leprosy: **“A woolen garment or a linen garment”** (Leviticus 13:47), the following conclusion can be drawn: Just as when the Torah mentions a garment in the case of leprosy it is referring to one made of wool or linen, **so too, all** garments mentioned in the Torah are those made from **wool or linen.** Other fabrics are not classified as those used for garments. If so, **why do I** need the phrase: **“Wool and linen” that the Merciful One wrote** with regard to diverse kinds? **Learn from this that** the superfluous passage is **free.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַכַּתִּי אִיצְטְרִיךְ, סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ אָמֵינָא: הַעֲלָאָה הִיא דְּלָא נְפִישׁ הֲנָאָתַהּ, אֲבָל לְבִישָׁה דִּנְפִישׁ הֲנָיָיתַהּ — כֹּל תְּרֵי מִינֵי אֲסַר רַחֲמָנָא, כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״צֶמֶר וּפִשְׁתִּים״!
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **But** the verse is **still necessary,** as it could **enter your mind to say** that it **is** referring only to **placing** a garment on oneself, **which does not** provide **great benefit; however,** with regard to the actual **wearing** of a garment, **which** entails **great benefit, the Merciful One prohibits** wearing **any two types** together. Therefore, **the Merciful One writes “wool and linen”** with regard to wearing garments of diverse kinds as well.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם כֵּן, לִשְׁתּוֹק קְרָא מִינֵּיהּ, וְתֵיתֵי ״שַׁעַטְנֵז״ ״שַׁעַטְנֵז״ מֵהַעֲלָאָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **If so, let the verse be silent** and refrain **from** mentioning wool and linen at all, **and** the *halakha* that only wearing wool and linen together is prohibited can **be derived** by means of a verbal analogy between the terms **“diverse kinds”** (Deuteronomy 22:11), and **“diverse kinds”** from **placing** a covering of diverse kinds upon oneself (Leviticus 19:19). The repetition of wool and linen must be coming to teach that one should derive a homiletic interpretation from these juxtaposed verses.

But the *Gemara* then identifies what truly is superfluous. *Im kein lichtov rachmana lo tilbash shatnez — tzemer ufishtim lama li?* — if that is so, why does the verse in *Devarim* need to say *tzemer ufishtim yachdav* — wool and linen together? Why specify the composition of *shatnez*?

One would think this is the least superfluous element — after all, without *tzemer ufishtim yachdav* we would not know what *shatnez* is. But the *Gemara* argues otherwise: *michdi kasav uveged kilayim shatnez lo ya'aleh alecha* — since the verse in *Vayikra* already says *uveged kilayim shatnez*, and *de'tana de'vei Rabbi Yishmael* — the school of *Rabbi Yishmael* teaches — *kol bi nimru baTorah stam begadim uferat lecha hakatuv be'echad meihem tzemer ufishtim* — wherever the Torah speaks of garments without specification, it means wool or linen garments, as established by the one instance where the Torah explicitly mentions *veged tzemer o veged pishtim* in the context of *tzara'at*. Thus, when *Vayikra* says *uveged kilayim shatnez* — a mixed garment — by the rule of *Rabbi Yishmael*, that means a garment mixing wool and linen. I would already have known *shatnez* means wool and linen. *Af kol tzemer ufishtim* — every garment is either wool or linen, and the mixing can only be wool with linen.

Why then does *Devarim* repeat *tzemer ufishtim yachdav*? The *Gemara* says it is still needed: *salka da'atach amina ha'ala'ah hi de'lo nafish hana'ah* — I might have thought that the act of *ha'ala'ah* — placing on oneself — involves lesser benefit and pleasure, and in that context the derivation from *Rabbi Yishmael* about the composition of garments applies, so *shatnez* there means only wool and linen. *Aval levisha de'nafish hana'ah* — but wearing, where there is greater pleasure, *kol terei minei asar rachmana* — perhaps the Torah prohibited any two different types of fiber mixed together, not just wool and linen. The word *tzemer ufishtim yachdav* is therefore needed even in *Devarim*, to explicitly confirm that the definition of *shatnez* as wool-and-linen specifically applies to the more pleasurable act of *levisha* just as much as to *ha'ala'ah*.

So *tzemer ufishtim* is still not superfluous? The *Gemara* responds: *im kein lishtok kera minei veteisei shatnez shatnez mehala* — if so, let the verse in *Devarim* be silent about *tzemer ufishtim* and I would have derived it from a *gezeirah shavah*: the word *shatnez* appears in *Vayikra* and the word *shatnez* appears in *Devarim*; from the verbal analogy I would have learned that the definition of *shatnez* is the same in both verses — wool and linen — even for the act of *levisha*. Therefore, the explicit mention of *tzemer ufishtim yachdav* in the verse about *levisha* in *Devarim* is in fact superfluous. *Shema mina le'afnuyei* — it must be that it was made extra, in order to enable the *drasha* from the juxtaposition with *tzitzit*. And that *drasha* is what establishes the principle of *asei dochei lo ta'aseh*.

## Summary Asei



### Established Principle

We can now draw together the threads of this entire *sugya*. The *Gemara* set out to explain why a *pasuk* was needed to prohibit *yibbum* in the case of *arayot* — that is, why the *hava amina* existed at all that *yibbum* might override those prohibitions. The answer begins with the principle of *asei dochei lo ta'aseh*: a positive *mitzvah* overrides a negative prohibition. We derive this principle from the juxtaposition in *Devarim* of the prohibition of *shatnez* and the *mitzvah* of *tzitzit*, which teaches that one may attach a wool string to a linen garment even though that constitutes *shatnez*, because the positive *mitzvah* of *tzitzit* overrides the negative prohibition of *shatnez*.

This principle is accepted by everyone, including those who generally reject *smuchin* as a hermeneutic, because the juxtaposition appears in *Mishneh Torah* — in *Devarim* — where *smuchin* is universally valid. It is valid there either because something newly appearing in *Devarim* in a different context from its original setting is evidently communicating something by means of that new context (*muchach*), or because the repetition of material already stated elsewhere renders the repeated passage superfluous (*mufneh*), specifically the phrase *tzemer ufishtim yachdav*, which could have been derived from a *gezeirah shavah* of *shatnez*–*shatnez* and therefore need not have been written out explicitly here.

With the principle of *asei dochei lo ta'aseh* thus firmly established and universally accepted, we have the foundation for the *hava amina*: one could have thought that the positive *mitzvah* of *yibbum* would override the negative prohibition of *arayot* in the *yibbum* context. The reason that *hava amina* does not hold in practice — because the prohibition of *arayot* carries *kareit*, and *asei dochei lo ta'aseh* does not apply to prohibitions of such severity — is precisely the point the opening *baraita* of the *sugya* established through its derivation from the word *aleha*. The full picture is now in place, and the discussion will continue from here.
