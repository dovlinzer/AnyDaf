## Gerama & Fire



### Rabbi Yossi Position

The present daf picks up in the middle of 120b, ten lines before the lines widen, at the words *osin mechitza*. Having concluded the previous *mishnah*, the Gemara turns to another *mishnah* that addresses issues central to *hilkhot Shabbat* — in particular, the question of saving property from a fire and the concern that rescue efforts might lead to outright prohibited labor.

The *mishnah* records a dispute regarding placing pottery vessels filled with water around a fire. The fear is that the heat will cause the vessels to burst, releasing the water and thereby extinguishing the fire. Rabbi Yossi prohibits this; the *chakhamim* permit it. The Gemara seizes on this dispute as a window into the broader question of *gerama* — indirect causation — on Shabbat.

### Gerama Definition

*Lemeima*, says the Gemara: *d'rabbanan savri geram kibuie mutar* — the *chakhamim* hold that causing a fire to extinguish itself indirectly is permitted. This formulation is striking, for it sounds like an outright, unconditional permission — some *rishonim* indeed hold that *gerama* is permitted even without any special justification, though at minimum the Gemara implies it is permitted in the context of a fire. *Ve'Rabbi Yossi savar geram kibuie assur* — Rabbi Yossi holds that indirect causation of extinguishment is forbidden.

### Baraita Resolution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא לְעוֹלָם לָא תֵּיפוֹךְ, וּבָרָיְיתָא כּוּלָּהּ רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הִיא. וְחַסּוֹרֵי מְחַסְּרָא וְהָכִי קָתָנֵי: עוֹשִׂין מְחִיצָה בְּכֵלִים רֵיקָנִין וּבִמְלֵאִים שֶׁאֵין דַּרְכָּן לְהִשְׁתַּבֵּר. וְאֵלּוּ הֵן כֵּלִים שֶׁאֵין דַּרְכָּן לְהִשְׁתַּבֵּר: כְּלֵי מַתָּכוֹת. וּכְלֵי כְּפַר שִׁיחִין וּכְלֵי כְּפַר חֲנַנְיָה נָמֵי אֵין דַּרְכָּן לְהִשְׁתַּבֵּר, שֶׁרַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹמֵר: אַף כְּלִי כְּפַר שִׁיחִין וּכְלֵי כְּפַר חֲנַנְיָה אֵין דַּרְכָּן לְהִשְׁתַּבֵּר.
> **Translation:** **Rather, actually, do not reverse** the attribution of the opinions in the mishna, **and the entire *baraita* is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yosei, and it is incomplete and is teaching the following: One may establish a barrier with empty vessels and full ones that do not typically break. And these are vessels that do not typically break: Metal vessels; and vessels** from **the village of Shiḥin and vessels** from **the village of Ḥananya also do not typically break, as Rabbi Yosei says: Even vessels** from **the village of Shiḥin and vessels** from **the village of Ḥananya do not typically break.**

Yet *ve'ipcha shemanan lehu* — we have apparently heard that they hold the reverse positions. A *baraita* teaches: *osin mechitza b'kelim reikim u'mle'im she'ein darkan l'hishtaber* — one may make a wall of empty vessels or full ones that will not normally break. This implies that the *tanna* of this *baraita* agrees with Rabbi Yossi that full vessels which would break and extinguish the fire are forbidden, and Rabbi Yossi himself then adds that even the pottery of Kfar Shichin and Kfar Chananya does not normally break — a more lenient position than the *chakhamim*. The positions appear inverted relative to the *mishnah*.

One resolution proposed is to reverse the attributions in the *mishnah* — the *chakhamim* would then be the stringent party and Rabbi Yossi the lenient one, and his statement in the *baraita* about the hard pottery would be understood as arguing against the *chakhamim* on their own terms. The Gemara, however, rejects this: *Rabba bar Tachlifa* says in the name of Rav that the *tanna* who holds *geram kibuie assur* is Rabbi Yossi — this is an established tradition that fixes Rabbi Yossi as the stringent position and cannot be reversed. Instead, the Gemara resolves the *baraita* by reading it as entirely Rabbi Yossi's view — *v'chisorei mechasra v'hachi kattani* — the *baraita* is incomplete and must be read as follows: one may make a wall of empty or full vessels that do not normally break; and vessels from Kfar Shichin and Kfar Chananya likewise do not normally break — because Rabbi Yossi says those vessels do not normally break. The entire *baraita* is Rabbi Yossi's, and the apparent role reversal dissolves.

### God's Name Gerama

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרָמֵי דְּרַבָּנַן אַדְּרַבָּנַן, וְרָמֵי דְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי אַדְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי. דְּתַנְיָא: הֲרֵי שֶׁהָיָה שֵׁם כָּתוּב לוֹ עַל בְּשָׂרוֹ — הֲרֵי זֶה לֹא יִרְחוֹץ וְלֹא יָסוּךְ וְלֹא יַעֲמוֹד בִּמְקוֹם הַטִּינּוֹפֶת. נִזְדַּמְּנָה לוֹ טְבִילָה שֶׁל מִצְוָה — כּוֹרֵךְ עָלֶיהָ גֶּמִי, וְיוֹרֵד וְטוֹבֵל. רַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹמֵר: לְעוֹלָם יוֹרֵד וְטוֹבֵל כְּדַרְכּוֹ, וּבִלְבַד שֶׁלֹּא יְשַׁפְשֵׁף!
> **Translation:** The conclusion is that Rabbi Yosei prohibits indirect extinguishing, and the Rabbis permit it. The Gemara **raises a contradiction between** one statement of **the Rabbis** and a second statement of **the Rabbis; and** it **raises a contradiction between** one statement of **Rabbi Yosei** and a second statement of **Rabbi Yosei. As it was taught** in a *baraita*: **If** one **had a** sacred **name** of God **written on his skin he may neither wash** it in water lest it be erased, **nor may he smear** it with oil, **nor may he stand in a place of filth** because it is disrespectful of God’s name. **If an immersion of mitzva happened to** present itself, **he wraps a reed over** God’s name **and** then **descends and immerses. Rabbi Yosei says: Actually, he descends and immerses in his** usual **manner,** even if it is not an immersion of mitzva, **provided that he does not rub** the spot and erase the name. Apparently, Rabbi Yosei’s opinion is more lenient than that of the Sages with regard to indirectly causing a prohibited outcome.

The Gemara now presents a genuine internal contradiction: *verami Rabbi Yossi ad Rabbi Yossi* — Rabbi Yossi contradicts himself. A *baraita* teaches that if one has written God's name in ink on one's arm, one may not bathe, anoint, or stand in a foul-smelling place, lest one erase it — for *mechikat Hashem*, erasing God's name, is a *de'oraita* prohibition. Yet if immersion in a *mikveh* for the sake of a *mitzvah* becomes necessary, the *chakhamim* say to wrap a band of reed around the arm and immerse; Rabbi Yossi says simply immerse in the normal manner, provided one does not scrub the arm. Immersing with God's name on one's arm will, naturally, cause the water to wash it away — an act of *gerama* in erasing God's name. Here Rabbi Yossi is not troubled by *gerama*, whereas in the fire case he is.

The Gemara proposes that perhaps the prohibition of *mechikat Hashem* is derived from the verse *lo taasun ken l'Hashem Elokeichem* — "do not do so to Hashem your God" — which forbids only direct action: *assiya hu d'assur, grama shari*. The Gemara then asks: but the same logic applies to Shabbat, where the verse reads *lo ta'aseh kol melacha* — "do not do any labor" — equally excluding indirect causation. This framing makes clear that *gerama* was never a *de'oraita* concern on Shabbat; the entire question is at the *de'rabbanan* level.

### Biblical vs Rabbanic

The Gemara's resolution now becomes a *de'rabbanan* distinction. By the prohibition of erasing God's name, *gerama* is simply permitted — the verse excludes it from the prohibition entirely. By Shabbat, there is likewise no biblical concern, but one might still have expected a *rabbinic* prohibition. The Gemara proceeds to explain why Rabbi Yossi differs on Shabbat specifically.

### Anxiety & Fire

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁאנֵי הָתָם דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״וְאִבַּדְתֶּם אֶת שְׁמָם מִן הַמָּקוֹם הַהוּא לֹא תַעֲשׂוּן כֵּן לַה׳ אֱלֹהֵיכֶם״ — עֲשִׂיָּיה הוּא דְּאָסוּר, גְּרָמָא שְׁרֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **It is different there** because **the verse says:** “And you shall break down their altars, and you shall smash their pillars, and their sacred trees, and burn with fire, and the graven images of their gods you shall hew down **and you shall destroy their name from that place. You shall not do so to the Lord your God”** (Deuteronomy 12:3–4). From the prohibition: “You shall not do so,” Rabbi Yosei derives that actually **doing so is that which is prohibited; indirectly causing** that result **is permitted.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי, הָכָא נָמֵי, כְּתִיב: ״לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה [כׇל] מְלָאכָה״ — עֲשִׂיָּיה הוּא דְּאָסוּר, גְּרָמָא שְׁרֵי! מִתּוֹךְ שֶׁאָדָם בָּהוּל עַל מָמוֹנוֹ, אִי שָׁרֵית לֵיהּ — אָתֵי לְכַבּוֹיֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If so, here too,** with regard to Shabbat, **it is written:** “And the seventh day is Shabbat for the Lord your God, **you shall not perform any labor”** (Exodus 20:9). And here, too, one could derive: **Performance is that which is prohibited; indirectly causing** a prohibited action **is permitted.** The Gemara answers: Actually, Rabbi Yosei maintains there is no prohibition in indirectly causing a fire to be extinguished; however, **since a person is agitated about his property, if you permit him** to indirectly extinguish the fire, **he will come to extinguish** it directly.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי הָכִי קַשְׁיָא דְּרַבָּנַן אַדְּרַבָּנַן: וּמָה הָתָם דְּאָדָם בָּהוּל עַל מָמוֹנוֹ — שְׁרֵי, הָכָא לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If so, there is a contradiction** between one statement **of the Rabbis** and another statement **of the Rabbis: If** in the case of a fire, **where a person is agitated about his property,** nevertheless, the Rabbis hold that indirect extinguishing **is permitted; here,** in the case of erasing God’s name, **all the more so** it should be permitted to indirectly cause erasure of God’s name. Why did the Rabbis in the *baraita* issue a stringent ruling in this matter?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתִסְבְּרָא הַאי גֶּמִי הֵיכִי דָּמֵי? אִי דְּמִיהַדַּק — קָא הָוֵי חֲצִיצָה, אִי לָא מִיהַדַּק — עָיְילִי בֵּיהּ מַיָּא! חֲצִיצָה, תִּיפּוֹק לֵיהּ מִשּׁוּם דְּיוֹ! בְּלַחָה. דְּתַנְיָא: הַדָּם וְהַדְּיוֹ הַדְּבַשׁ וְהֶחָלָב, יְבֵשִׁין — חוֹצְצִין, לַחִים — אֵין חוֹצְצִין. מִכׇּל מָקוֹם קַשְׁיָא?
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this: **And** how can **you understand** it that way? **What are the circumstances** of **this reed** with which God’s name is covered while immersing according to the Rabbis? **If** it is **firmly attached, it is an interposition; if it is not firmly attached, the water enters** and erases the name. The Gemara questions this: If the concern is with regard to an **interposition, derive** that **it** is an interposition in any case **due to** the **ink** on his skin. The Gemara answers: This is referring to **moist** ink that does not interpose, **as it was taught** in a *baraita*: With regard to **blood, and ink,** and **honey, and milk** on a person’s skin, when they are **dry,** they **interpose** during immersion; when they are **moist,** they **do not interpose.** The Gemara continues: **Nevertheless,** it is **difficult:** Why do the Rabbis require wrapping a reed around God’s name?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא בַּר רַב שֵׁילָא, הַיְינוּ טַעְמַיְיהוּ דְּרַבָּנַן, דְּקָסָבְרִי: אָסוּר לַעֲמוֹד בִּפְנֵי הַשֵּׁם עָרוֹם. מִכְּלָל דְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי סָבַר מוּתָּר לַעֲמוֹד בִּפְנֵי הַשֵּׁם עָרוֹם? דְּמַנַּח יְדֵיהּ עִילָּוֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rava bar Rav Sheila said** that **this is the reasoning of the Rabbis: They hold** that **it is prohibited** for a person **to stand before the name** of God while **naked,** and therefore there must be a barrier between one’s naked body and the name. The Gemara asks: Is that to say **by inference** that **Rabbi Yosei holds** that **it is permitted to stand before the name** while **naked?** The Gemara answers: No, he does not allow doing so; however, he does not require that the name be covered with a reed. It is sufficient for **one to place his hand over** the name.

The Gemara's answer is: *mitoch she'adam bahul al mamono* — because a person is agitated over his property. Rabbi Yossi in principle has no conceptual objection to *gerama*; it is permitted even on Shabbat. But in the specific context of a fire, if one is allowed to engage in the elaborate arrangement of water-filled vessels, one's anxiety over the loss of possessions will lead one to go further and directly extinguish the fire. This is the same delicate balance that runs through the entire chapter: if too much is prohibited, people will simply violate Shabbat outright; if too much is permitted, anxious homeowners will overstep in their rescue efforts. *Grama kibuie*, therefore, is permitted in principle, but Rabbi Yossi forbids it by fire due to this special *de'rabbanan* concern — a concern that does not apply in the case of God's name on one's arm.

This prompts a further question: the *chakhamim* who argue on Rabbi Yossi permit the water-filled vessels by fire. But then why do they require covering God's name before *tevilah*? If a person is agitated enough during a fire that we fear *gerama* will lead to direct extinguishment, should we not *a fortiori* be lenient in the case of the *mikveh*, where no such anxiety is present? And if *gerama* is permissible, why does anyone require the covering at all?

At this point the Gemara reconsiders the entire framework. *Ve'dilma*, it asks — does the reed band (*gemi*) even accomplish anything? If it is tight on the arm, it constitutes a *chatzitzah* and invalidates the *tevilah*; if it is loose, the water penetrates anyway and provides no protection for the ink. And one might further think the ink itself is a *chatzitzah* — but a *baraita* resolves this: blood, ink, honey, and milk are *chatzitzah* only when dry; when moist, they are water-permeable and are not a *chatzitzah*. The *gemi*, however, remains problematic.

The Gemara therefore abandons the *gerama* framework for this case entirely, and Rava bar Rav Shila provides the true explanation: *d'kasvari assur la'amod bifnei Hashem arom* — the *chakhamim* hold that it is forbidden to stand naked before God's name. The covering is not about preventing erasure of God's name; it is about not exposing God's name while unclothed. This explains why a loose cover suffices — it need not keep water out, only conceal the name.

The *chakhamim* require one to seek out such a covering, even at some inconvenience, while Rabbi Yossi holds one may simply place one's hand over the arm. The Gemara asks: might one's hand slip? And the response is that the same concern applies symmetrically. The Gemara concludes that the ideal solution — when a loose cover is readily available — is accepted by all. The dispute reduces to whether one must delay the *tevilah* to go searching for a cover when none is at hand.

### Tevilah Ba-Zmanah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְרַבָּנַן נָמֵי, דְּמַנַּח יְדֵיהּ עִילָּוֵיהּ! זִימְנִין דְּמִשְׁתְּלֵי וְשָׁקֵיל לֵיהּ. לְרַבִּי יוֹסֵי נָמֵי, זִימְנִין דְּמִשְׁתְּלֵי וְשָׁקֵיל לֵיהּ? אֶלָּא: אִי דְּאִיכָּא גֶּמִי — הָכִי נָמֵי, הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן — לְהַדּוֹרֵי אַגֶּמִי, רַבָּנַן סָבְרִי
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: If so, according **to the Rabbis** it should **also** be sufficient for **one to place his hand over it.** The Gemara explains: The Rabbis are concerned because **sometimes one** will **forget and remove** his hand. The Gemara asks: Shouldn’t **Rabbi Yosei also** be concerned because **sometimes one** will **forget and remove** his hand? **Rather, if there is a reed** available, Rabbi Yosei **indeed** concedes that one covers God’s name with it. However, **with what** situation **are we dealing here?** We are dealing with a case in which one would have **to seek a reed.** In that case, **the Rabbis hold**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** טְבִילָה בִּזְמַנָּהּ לָאו מִצְוָה וּמַהְדְּרִינַן, וְרַבִּי יוֹסֵי סָבַר טְבִילָה בִּזְמַנָּהּ מִצְוָה וְלָא מַהְדְּרִינַן.
> **Translation:** that performing **immersion at its** designated **time is not a mitzva, and we seek** a reed to wrap around God’s name even if it means postponing immersion to the next day, **and Rabbi Yosei holds** that **immersion at its** designated **time is a mitzva, and** therefore **we do not seek** a reed, since immersion cannot be postponed.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְסָבַר רַבִּי יוֹסֵי טְבִילָה בִּזְמַנָּהּ מִצְוָה? וְהָתַנְיָא: הַזָּב וְהַזָּבָה, הַמְצוֹרָע וְהַמְצוֹרֶעֶת, בּוֹעֵל נִדָּה וּטְמֵא מֵת, טְבִילָתָן בַּיּוֹם. נִדָּה וְיוֹלֶדֶת — טְבִילָתָן בַּלַּיְלָה. בַּעַל קֶרִי — טוֹבֵל וְהוֹלֵךְ כׇּל הַיּוֹם כּוּלּוֹ. רַבִּי יוֹסֵי אוֹמֵר: מִן הַמִּנְחָה וּלְמַעְלָה אֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ לִטְבּוֹל! הַהִיא, רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה הִיא, דְּאָמַר: דַּיָּיהּ טְבִילָה בָּאַחֲרוֹנָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And does Rabbi Yosei hold** that **immersion at its** designated **time is a mitzva? Wasn’t it taught** in a *baraita*: With regard to **a *zav* and a *zava*, a male and female leper, one who has relations with a menstruating woman, and a person impure** with impurity imparted by **a corpse, their immersion is during the day.** They immerse at the designated time even on Yom Kippur, when bathing is prohibited. **A menstruating woman and a woman after childbirth immerse at night. A man who has had a seminal emission immerses** at any point **during the entire day** after the emission. **Rabbi Yosei says: From** the time that he recited **the afternoon prayer and on he does not immerse.** Since he already recited the afternoon prayer, he waits until after Yom Kippur to immerse, and then recites the evening prayer in a state of purity. Apparently, Rabbi Yosei holds that immersion at the designated time is not a mitzva. The Gemara rejects this: In **that** *baraita* the reference is not to the Rabbi Yosei most commonly cited in tannaitic literature without a patronymic, Rabbi Yosei ben Ḥalafta, but it **is** to **Rabbi Yosei, son of Rabbi Yehuda, who said: Her latest immersion is sufficient.** A woman who is uncertain with regard to the correct time for her immersion need not immerse multiple times. She may postpone her immersion until a time when she will be certain to fulfill her obligation, even though it might not be immersion at the designated time.

This brings us to the question of *tevila bizmana*: is there a *mitzvah* to perform one's immersion precisely at its appointed time, or may one delay? The *chakhamim* hold *tevila bizmana lav mitzvah* — there is no obligation to immerse at the exact time. Therefore, if one must search for something to cover God's name, the *tevilah* may simply wait. Rabbi Yossi holds *tevila bizmana mitzvah* — immersion at its designated time is a *mitzvah* — and therefore one should not be compelled to postpone it in order to search for a cover. Placing one's hand over the arm is sufficient.

A *baraita* is then cited concerning *Yom Kippur*, which categorizes various categories of those who require *tevilah* — *zav*, *zavah*, *metzora*, *bo'el niddah*, *tamei meit* — and specifies whether their immersion falls by day or by night. It concludes that a *ba'al keri* may immerse at any time during the day. Rabbi Yossi says that from *Mincha* onward he need not immerse. This might suggest Rabbi Yossi holds *tevila bizmana lav mitzvah* — why immerse on *Yom Kippur* if one can simply wait until nightfall? The Gemara, however, identifies this Rabbi Yossi as Rabbi Yossi b'Rabbi Yehuda, not the *stam* Rabbi Yossi of our *mishnah*. Rabbi Yossi b'Rabbi Yehuda explicitly holds *tevila bizmana lav mitzvah* — as evidenced by his ruling that a woman in complex cases of uncertain status need only immerse once at the end of the entire uncertain period rather than at every possible time. The *stam* Rabbi Yossi of our *mishnah*, by contrast, holds *tevila bizmana mitzvah*. The practical difference is pointed: according to the *chakhamim*, since there is no obligation to immerse at the precise time, one can simply defer the *tevilah* and go find a cover — even if *shkia* is imminent, there is no urgency. According to Rabbi Yossi, requiring the person to delay and search would itself constitute an imposition, since performing the *tevilah* at its designated moment is itself the *mitzvah*.

The upshot of the entire sugya is that *gerama* is permitted — certainly at the *de'oraita* level, and the language of the Gemara suggests it may be entirely permitted even *de'rabbanan*. The *chakhamim* permit it by fire outright; Rabbi Yossi agrees that *gerama* is in principle permitted but introduces a special *de'rabbanan* concern specific to fires, where agitation over property loss may lead to direct violation. By the *mikveh* scenario, the covering requirement has nothing to do with *gerama* at all, but rests on the separate concern of standing unclothed before God's name. What is worth flagging here is how radical the Gemara's framing actually is: the Gemara never frames *gerama* itself as *assur de'rabbanan* — the only concern raised is that in specific circumstances it may lead to further violations. The practical ruling is that *gerama* is at least *assur de'rabbanan* on Shabbat — and this principle underlies many of the leniencies developed by responsa authorities for indirect activation of electrical devices on Shabbat, much of which rests on the *gerama* foundation.

## Amirah L'Goy & Katan



### Non-Jew Fire Help

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ גּוֹי שֶׁבָּא לְכַבּוֹת — אֵין אוֹמְרִים לוֹ ״כַּבֵּה״ וְ״אַל תְּכַבֶּה״, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁאֵין שְׁבִיתָתוֹ עֲלֵיהֶן. אֲבָל קָטָן שֶׁבָּא לְכַבּוֹת — אֵין שׁוֹמְעִין לוֹ, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁשְּׁבִיתָתוֹ עֲלֵיהֶן.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: If **a gentile comes to extinguish** a Jew’s fire on Shabbat, **one may not say to him: Extinguish, and: Do not extinguish, because** responsibility for **his rest is not** incumbent **upon** the Jew. **However,** if **a** Jewish **child comes to extinguish** a fire on Shabbat, **they do not listen to him** and allow him to extinguish it, even though he is not yet obligated in mitzva observance, **because** responsibility for **his rest is** incumbent **upon** the Jew.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ אָמַר רַבִּי אַמֵּי: בִּדְלֵיקָה הִתִּירוּ לוֹמַר ״כׇּל הַמְכַבֶּה אֵינוֹ מַפְסִיד״. נֵימָא מְסַיַּיע לֵיהּ: גּוֹי שֶׁבָּא לְכַבּוֹת — אֵין אוֹמְרִים לוֹ ״כַּבֵּה״ וְ״אַל תְּכַבֶּה״ מִפְּנֵי שֶׁאֵין שְׁבִיתָתוֹ עֲלֵיהֶן. ״כַּבֵּה״ הוּא דְּלָא אָמְרִינַן לֵיהּ, הָא ״כׇּל הַמְכַבֶּה אֵינוֹ מַפְסִיד״ — אָמְרִינַן לֵיהּ. אֵימָא סֵיפָא, ״אַל תְּכַבֶּה״ לָא אָמְרִינַן לֵיהּ, וְ״כׇל הַמְכַבֶּה אֵינוֹ מַפְסִיד״ נָמֵי לָא אָמְרִינַן לֵיהּ. אֶלָּא מֵהָא לֵיכָּא לְמִשְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: **Rabbi Ami said:** During **a fire, the** Sages **permitted to say** in the presence of gentiles: **Anyone who extinguishes** the fire **will not lose,** so that the gentiles will come and extinguish the fire; it is only prohibited to tell gentiles to do so explicitly. The Gemara suggests: **Let us say** that the mishna **supports his** statement: If **a gentile comes to extinguish** a Jew’s fire on Shabbat, **one may not say to him: Extinguish, and: Do not extinguish, because** responsibility for **his rest is not** incumbent **upon** the Jew. It can be inferred from the language of the mishna: **It is** a direct command, e.g., **extinguish, that we may not say to him; however, anyone who extinguishes will not lose, we** may **tell him,** which supports Rabbi Ami’s statement. The Gemara rejects this. **Say** the **latter** clause of the mishna: **Do not extinguish, we do not tell him.** It can be inferred that **neither do we say to him: Anyone who extinguishes will not lose. Rather, nothing can be inferred from this** mishna.

The *mishnah* teaches: *nochri sheba lekhabot* — if a non-Jew comes of his own accord to extinguish a fire, one may not say to him "extinguish it," for that constitutes *amirah l'akum* — enlisting a non-Jew to perform *melacha* on one's behalf. Yet one need not say to him "do not extinguish it" either. *Mipnei she'ein shvitato aleihen* — because his resting is not incumbent upon you. You have no obligation to ensure that a non-Jew refrains from *melacha* on Shabbat. One may not actively engage him, but one need not stop him either.

The *mishnah* then draws a sharp contrast with the case of a minor: *aval katan sheba lekhabot ein shomin lo* — if a minor comes to extinguish, one may not passively allow it. *Mipnei sheshvitato aleihen* — because his resting is your obligation. The Torah's command that *binkha uvitekha* — your son and your daughter — shall not perform *melacha* on Shabbat establishes an affirmative parental and supervisory responsibility. Unlike the non-Jew, whose conduct on Shabbat is entirely his own affair, the minor falls within the Jew's sphere of halakhic responsibility, and this distinction is what drives the asymmetry between the two cases.

One might ask: since extinguishing a fire without the purpose of producing charcoal is only a *de'rabbanan* prohibition — it is *melacha she'einah tzrikhah le-gufah* in the view of Rabbi Shimon — why is *amirah l'akum* prohibited even here? *Tosafot* addresses this directly: *ein omrim lo kabbe afilu bidvar de'rabbanan amirah l'nochri shevut* — one may not instruct a non-Jew even for a *de'rabbanan* prohibition. *Amirah l'akum* itself is a *de'rabbanan* prohibition, but it applies regardless of whether the underlying act is biblical or rabbinic.

The Gemara then cites Rabbi Ami: in the case of a fire, the rabbis permitted one to make a general public announcement — to no one in particular — that whoever chooses to come and extinguish the fire will not lose out, and after Shabbat one may pay them for their services. This is not a direct instruction to a specific non-Jew to extinguish the fire; it is a general declaration addressed to whoever happens to hear it, and the non-Jew acts entirely of his own volition. The question of whether one may go further — instructing a non-Jew to engage in *grama kibuie* — is foreclosed by *Tosafot*'s ruling that even *de'rabbanan* acts are off-limits for *amirah l'akum*.

The Gemara attempts to derive support for the permissibility of the general announcement from the *mishnah* itself: if the *mishnah* says "do not tell him to extinguish," this implies that telling him in the general, indirect fashion — "whoever extinguishes will not lose out" — would be permitted. But the Gemara rejects this inference, noting that the *mishnah*'s parallel clause — "do not tell him not to extinguish" — could equally be read to mean that any statement more directive than this silence is forbidden, including the general announcement. *Ella mihacha leka l'mishmana mineh* — one cannot prove either way from the *mishnah*. Nevertheless, *Tosafot* rules that the general announcement is permitted, and this is the practical ruling.

### Yosef ben Simai

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מַעֲשֶׂה וְנָפְלָה דְּלֵיקָה בַּחֲצֵירוֹ שֶׁל יוֹסֵף בֶּן סִימַאי בְּשִׁיחִין, וּבָאוּ אַנְשֵׁי גִּיסְטְרָא שֶׁל צִיפּוֹרִי לְכַבּוֹת, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁאַפִּטְרוֹפּוֹס שֶׁל מֶלֶךְ הָיָה, וְלֹא הִנִּיחָן מִפְּנֵי כְּבוֹד הַשַּׁבָּת, וְנַעֲשָׂה לוֹ נֵס וְיָרְדוּ גְּשָׁמִים וְכִיבּוּ. לָעֶרֶב שִׁיגֵּר לְכׇל אֶחָד מֵהֶן שְׁתֵּי סְלָעִין, וְלָאִפַּרְכוֹס שֶׁבָּהֶן — חֲמִשִּׁים. וּכְשֶׁשָּׁמְעוּ חֲכָמִים בְּדָבָר, אָמְרוּ: לֹא הָיָה צָרִיךְ לְכָךְ, שֶׁהֲרֵי שָׁנִינוּ: גּוֹי שֶׁבָּא לְכַבּוֹת — אֵין אוֹמְרִים לוֹ ״כַּבֵּה״ וְ״אַל תְּכַבֶּה״.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: **There was an incident that a fire ignited** on Shabbat **in the courtyard of Yosef ben Simai in** a place called **Shiḥin. And men came from the fortress [*gistera*] of Tzippori to extinguish** the fire, **because he was a steward [*apotropos*] of the king** and they wanted to help him. **However,** Yosef ben Simai **would not allow them** to extinguish the fire **in deference to Shabbat; and a miracle transpired for him and rain fell and extinguished** the fire. **That evening** after Shabbat **he sent two *sela* to each one of** the soldiers who came to his aid, **and fifty to their commander [*iparkhos*]. And when the Sages heard about this, they said: He need not** have prevented them from extinguishing the fire, **as we learned** in the mishna: If **a gentile comes to extinguish** a Jew’s fire on Shabbat, **one may not say to him: Extinguish, and: Do not extinguish, because** responsibility for **his rest is not** incumbent **upon** the Jew; rather, the gentile may do as he pleases.

A *baraita* recounts: a fire broke out in the courtyard of Yosef ben Simai in Shichin, and the soldiers of the garrison of Tzippori came to extinguish it — apparently from a neighboring village, drawn by the news, perhaps because Yosef ben Simai held a position of authority as an officer of the king. Yosef ben Simai refused to allow them to extinguish the fire, *mipnei kvod Shabbat* — out of concern for the honor of Shabbat. A miracle occurred: rain fell and extinguished the fire. That evening he sent each of the soldiers two *sela'im* and fifty *sela'im* to their commander, rewarding their goodwill even though he had turned them away.

When the rabbis heard of this, they said: he need not have done that. The *mishnah* already teaches that one need not stop a non-Jew from extinguishing a fire on his own initiative. The narrative carries a dual message: it celebrates the extraordinary piety of Yosef ben Simai, while making unmistakably clear that ordinary people should not attempt to replicate it. Miracles are not to be assumed, and the halakha provides an adequate path that does not require turning away help. The concern Yosef ben Simai expressed was perhaps not about a specific halakhic violation — he was not technically violating anything by allowing them to proceed — but about the experiential dimension of Shabbat: having an active firefighting effort underway undermines the spirit of the day, particularly for a public figure whose conduct represented *kevod Shabbat* before the surrounding population.

### Minor Agency

The *mishnah* continues: *aval katan sheba lekhabot ein shomin lo* — but if a minor comes to extinguish the fire, one may not allow him to proceed, *mipnei sheshvitato aleihem* — because his resting is your obligation. Unlike the non-Jew, whose Shabbat rest is not the Jew's responsibility, the minor's Shabbat observance is. The Gemara then asks whether this *mishnah* implies a general principle — that one is obligated to prevent a minor from performing any biblical prohibition (*katan ochel nevelot beit din metzuvin alav lehafrisho*). Rabbi Yochanan answers: no. The *mishnah* is speaking specifically of a case where the minor acts *al daat aviv* — for his father's sake, to benefit his father.

### Acting for Parent

When a minor acts on his own — for example, eating *treifa* food for himself — it is not one's obligation to interfere, at least where the child is below the age of *chinuch* or is not one's own child. *Tosafot* notes that if the minor has reached the age of *chinuch* or is one's own child, the parent does bear an obligation to stop him. But the case of the *mishnah* is different: the minor is extinguishing the fire to benefit his father. In that case he is not acting for himself; he is an extension of the father. Allowing him to proceed is therefore analogous to *amirah l'goy* — it is as if the father had effectively enlisted him, even without explicit instruction.

This framing also helps clarify why the Gemara must invoke the *al daat aviv* qualifier at all. If the minor were at the age of *chinuch* for Shabbat, one would be obligated to stop him regardless of whose benefit he was acting for. The *al daat aviv* distinction therefore presupposes a minor young enough to be below the age of *chinuch* — and yet, as the lecturer notes, it is a somewhat puzzling scenario to imagine a child simultaneously too young to have reached *chinuch* for Shabbat and yet old enough to participate meaningfully in extinguishing a fire. The Gemara does not itself raise the concern of *chinuch*; it is *Tosafot* who introduces it, and in so doing forces the conclusion that the case must involve a child below that threshold.

### Non-Jew vs Minor

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲבָל קָטָן שֶׁבָּא לְכַבּוֹת אֵין שׁוֹמְעִין לוֹ מִפְּנֵי שֶׁשְּׁבִיתָתוֹ עֲלֵיהֶן. שָׁמְעַתְּ מִינַּהּ קָטָן אוֹכֵל נְבֵלוֹת, בֵּית דִּין מְצֻוִּוין עָלָיו לְהַפְרִישׁוֹ. אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: בְּקָטָן הָעוֹשֶׂה לְדַעַת אָבִיו. דִּכְווֹתַהּ גַּבֵּי גּוֹי, דְּקָא עָבֵיד לְדַעְתֵּיהּ דְּיִשְׂרָאֵל — מִי שְׁרֵי?! גּוֹי לְדַעְתֵּיהּ דְּנַפְשֵׁיהּ עָבֵיד.
> **Translation:** We learned in the mishna: **However,** if **a** Jewish **child comes to extinguish** a fire on Shabbat, **they do not listen to him** and allow him to extinguish it, even though he is not yet obligated in mitzva observance, **because** responsibility for **his rest is** incumbent **upon** the Jew. The Gemara seeks to conclude: **Learn** from **this** that **a child who eats** meat from **unslaughtered animals** or violates other prohibitions, **the court is commanded to prevent him** from eating it. This mishna would resolve a dilemma that arose regarding that issue. The Gemara rejects this suggestion: **Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** This mishna is referring to **a child who is acting with** the intention of fulfilling **his father’s will,** and therefore one is obligated to prevent him from doing so. However, if a child sins of his own volition, one is not obligated to prevent him from doing so. The Gemara asks: If so, the case **with regard to a gentile** in the mishna must be interpreted in a **similar** manner as referring to a case where **he is acting with** the intention to fulfill the **will** of **a Jew. Is** that **permitted?** It is prohibited to derive benefit from an action performed by a gentile for a Jew on Shabbat. The Gemara responds: This is not the case; **the gentile** is **acting of his own volition.** Because he is paid for extinguishing the fire he is not doing so in order to help the Jew.

This leads the Gemara to ask: if the minor acts *al daat aviv* and thereby becomes an extension of the father, should the same logic not apply to the non-Jew who comes to extinguish the fire for the sake of the Jew? Is he not also acting for the Jew's benefit? The Gemara answers: *nochri l'daatei d'nafshei avid* — the non-Jew acts for his own purposes. Even if his motivation is friendship, anticipated reward, or goodwill toward the Jew, he makes his own autonomous calculation. He is not subservient; he acts because it serves his own interests as he perceives them. The minor, by contrast, acts entirely out of felt obligation to the parent and without the independent judgment of an adult. He therefore functions as a complete extension of the father rather than as an independent agent.

It is worth being precise about what makes a non-Jew's action his own rather than the Jew's. The non-Jew is always considered to act *l'nafshei* — for himself — unless the Jew directly instructs or requests the action. Even when the non-Jew is doing something that benefits the Jew, so long as he arrives at that action through his own autonomous decision, it remains his act. This is why the *amirah l'akum* prohibition is primarily triggered by a direct demand or request: absent that, the non-Jew retains his independent agency, and the various permitted workarounds involving a non-Jew function precisely because his choice remains his own. The minor's situation is structurally different: his felt subservience and lack of adult judgment mean his choice does not carry that same independence, which is why the Gemara treats him as a more complete extension of the parent than a non-Jew acting on the parent's behalf ever could be.

This analysis yields a striking halakhic observation. The *mishnah* appeared to ground the prohibition of allowing one's minor to extinguish the fire in the biblical verse — *atah u'vincha u'vitecha* — which explicitly includes one's children among those who must rest on Shabbat. But the Gemara reframes the entire discussion: the question becomes one of general obligations to prevent minors from performing *issurim*, compounded by the specific concern that when a minor acts for a parent he becomes an extension of that parent, akin to *amirah l'akum*. The affirmative biblical obligation to ensure that one's children rest — implied by the *mishnah*'s language — is effectively subsumed into these generic categories. Some *rishonim* and *poskim* do revisit this independent biblical obligation of *shevisat katanim*, but it is a striking feature of the Gemara's analysis that this dimension nearly disappears from view.

## Vessels & Protection



### Covering Candles

The next *mishnah* states: *kofin k'ara al gabei ner beshvil shelo te'echoz bekora* — one may overturn a vessel over a burning candle so that the beam above does not catch fire. This is not an act of extinguishing; it is a protective measure. Similarly, one may cover the excrement of a minor and a scorpion so that one does not step on it and is not bitten.

### Minor Excrement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ כּוֹפִין קְעָרָה עַל גַּבֵּי הַנֵּר בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁלֹּא תֶּאֱחוֹז בַּקּוֹרָה, וְעַל צוֹאָה שֶׁל קָטָן, וְעַל עַקְרָב שֶׁלֹּא תִּישָּׁךְ. אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: מַעֲשֶׂה בָּא לִפְנֵי רַבָּן יוֹחָנָן בֶּן זַכַּאי בַּעֲרָב, וְאָמַר: חוֹשְׁשַׁנִי לוֹ מֵחַטָּאת.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **One may overturn a bowl on top of a lamp so that** fire **will not take hold in the** ceiling **beam** on Shabbat. **And** similarly, one may overturn a bowl **on top of a child’s feces** inside the house so he will not touch it and dirty himself, **and on top of a scorpion so that it will not bite. Rabbi Yehuda said: An incident came before Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai in** his village of **Arav,** where a person covered a scorpion on Shabbat, **and** Rabban Yoḥanan **said: I am concerned** that **he** is liable to bring **a sin-offering** because he might have violated a Torah prohibition.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ רַב יְהוּדָה וְרַב יִרְמְיָה בַּר אַבָּא וְרַב חָנָן בַּר רָבָא אִיקְּלַעוּ לְבֵי אָבִין דְּמִן נְשִׁיקְיָא. לְרַב יְהוּדָה וְרַב יִרְמְיָה בַּר אַבָּא
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara relates: **Rav Yehuda and Rav Yirmeya bar Abba and Rav Ḥanan bar Rava happened to come to the house of Avin from** a place called **Nashikiya. For Rav Yehuda and Rav Yirmeya bar Abba,**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַיְיתוֹ לְהוּ פּוּרְיָיתָא, לְרַב חָנָן בַּר רָבָא לָא אַיְיתוֹ לֵיהּ. אַשְׁכְּחֵיהּ מַתְנֵי לֵיהּ לִבְרֵיהּ: וְעַל צוֹאָה שֶׁל קָטָן מִפְּנֵי קָטָן. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אָבִין שָׁטְיָא מַתְנֵי שְׁטוּתָא לִבְנֵיהּ, וַהֲלֹא הִיא עַצְמָהּ מוּכֶנֶת לִכְלָבִים! וְכִי תֵּימָא דְּלָא חַזְיָא לֵיהּ מֵאֶתְמוֹל — וְהָתַנְיָא: נְהָרוֹת הַמּוֹשְׁכִין וּמַעֲיָינוֹת הַנּוֹבְעִין — הֲרֵי הֵן כְּרַגְלֵי כׇּל אָדָם.
> **Translation:** **they brought beds for them** to sit on, whereas **for Rav Ḥanan bar Rava, they did not bring** one **for him.** Rav Ḥanan was insulted and got angry at his host. **He found** Rabbi Avin **teaching his son** the mishna and saying: **And** one may place a bowl **on top of the feces of a child due to the child** so that he will not touch it and dirty himself. Rav Ḥanan **said to him: Avin the fool is teaching his son folly; aren’t** the feces **themselves prepared** as food **for dogs?** An object that is fit for consumption by a dog may be carried, so why need one cover the feces if he can remove them? **And if you say** these feces **were not prepared for** that use **from yesterday,** that would not prohibit moving them. **Wasn’t it taught** in a *baraita*: With regard to **rivers that flow and springs that gush,** they do not have specific Shabbat boundaries, but **they** may be used anywhere and their boundaries **are like the feet of all people** who draw from them. Even though the water was not within the boundaries when Shabbat began, since the nature of flowing water is to move from place to place, it is as if they were prepared for this purpose. So too, here, since a child will relieve himself in any place, it is as if it was prepared from before Shabbat.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֶלָּא הֵיכִי אַתְנְיֵיהּ? אֵימָא: עַל צוֹאָה שֶׁל תַּרְנְגוֹלִים מִפְּנֵי קָטָן.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Avin asked: **And how, then, should I teach it to him?** Rav Ḥanan answered: **Say,** one may place a bowl **on top of chicken feces due to a child.**

The *mishnah*'s mention of covering the excrement of a minor prompts discussion. The *mishnah*'s apparent *chiddush* is that one may move a vessel to cover something that might otherwise be *muktzah*. But the Gemara challenges the *chiddush*: *hi atzmah mukhenet le-klavim* — the excrement of a minor is not *muktzah* to begin with, since it has some use. Perhaps the *chiddush* is that it constitutes *nolad* — something new that came into being on Shabbat? A *baraita* teaches that flowing rivers and springs are not treated as *nolad* because their flow is continuous and expected. Similarly, the excrement of a minor is expected and therefore not *nolad*.

The broader context of this discussion arose during a visit of several rabbis — Rav Yehuda, Rav Yirmiya bar Abba, and Rav Chanan bar Rava — to the home of Avin of Neshikaya. The host provided benches for Rav Yehuda and Rav Yirmiya bar Abba but not for Rav Chanan bar Rava, who sat on the floor. Feeling slighted, Rav Chanan bar Rava, overhearing the host teaching his son the version of this teaching about covering the excrement of a minor, declared that the man was teaching his son nonsense — pointing out that there was no *chiddush* since the excrement was not *muktzah*. The host asked how he should teach the passage. The resolution: the teaching should read *al tzo'ah she-l tarnegolim mipnei katan* — covering the droppings of chickens so that the minor does not dirty himself. Bird droppings, unlike the excrement of a minor, are not *ra'uy le-klavim* and would be *muktzah*, and the *chiddush* is therefore that one may cover them to protect the child.

### Bird Droppings

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתִיפּוֹק לֵיהּ דְּהָוֵי גְּרָף שֶׁל רְעִי! וְכִי תֵּימָא: גְּרָף שֶׁל רְעִי אַגַּב מָנָא — אִין, אִיהוּ גּוּפֵיהּ — לָא, וְהָא הָהוּא עַכְבָּר דְּאִישְׁתְּכַח בְּאִיסְפַּרְמָקֵי דְּרַב אָשֵׁי, וַאֲמַר לְהוּ: נִקְטוּהּ בְּצוּצִיתֵיהּ וְאַפְּקוּהּ! בְּאַשְׁפָּה. וְקָטָן בְּאַשְׁפָּה מַאי בָּעֵי לֵיהּ? בְּחָצֵר. חָצֵר נָמֵי, גְּרָף שֶׁל רְעִי הוּא! בְּאַשְׁפָּה שֶׁבְּחָצֵר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And derive** that **it** is permitted to remove the feces **because it is** like **a chamber pot of feces,** which may be moved out of the room because it is disgusting. **And if you say** that **a chamber pot of feces together with the vessel, yes,** it may be moved; but feces **itself, no,** it may not be moved. What about **that mouse that was found among the spices [*isperamaki*]** of **Rav Ashi, and he said to** his servants: **Take it by its tail and remove it?** Apparently, a disgusting object may be moved even without a vessel. Rather, we must say that the feces were **in the garbage** dump, and since the feces were not before the household members it was only permitted to conceal them, not to move them. The Gemara asks: **And what is a child doing in the garbage** dump in the public domain? It is not adjacent to the house; how would the child get there? The Gemara answers that the feces were not in the garbage dump but were **in the courtyard.** The Gemara rejects this: **In the courtyard, it is also** considered **a chamber pot of feces** and may be moved. Rather, it is referring to **a garbage** dump **that is in the courtyard,** to which the child sometimes has access. Feces in a place designated for garbage are no more disgusting than their surroundings, and therefore it is prohibited to move the feces.

Even so, the Gemara challenges: the bird droppings in the yard could simply be removed as *graf shel re'i* — the rule that permits removing repulsive waste from one's domain. The Gemara responds that *graf shel re'i* allows one to move the waste contained *within* a vessel, not to pick up the waste directly. But another source shows that even repulsive waste may be moved directly — the case of a rat found in Rav Ashi's perfumes, where Rav Ashi instructed that it be taken by its tail and removed. The Gemara concludes that the case of the *mishnah* concerns waste in the garbage area of one's yard — a location where the rules permitting removal of *graf shel re'i* do not apply — and therefore covering it is the only available option.

### Scorpion Trapping

The *mishnah*'s permission to cover a scorpion relates to the laws of trapping on Shabbat. Before reaching that analysis, however, the Gemara raises a preliminary question: given everything established about the bird droppings case, what exactly is the *chiddush* — the novel ruling — of permitting one to cover a scorpion? The answer is that while covering the scorpion may look similar to covering the excrement, the scorpion case introduces its own distinct set of concerns, chief among them being the laws of trapping, which do not arise in the excrement discussion at all. Trapping the scorpion constitutes *melacha she'einah tzrikhah le-gufa* — labor performed not for the sake of the usual objective of that labor (one does not want the scorpion for any productive purpose) — and is therefore only a *de'rabbanan* prohibition in the view of Rabbi Shimon. Rabbi Yehuda records that a case involving trapping a scorpion in the town of Arav came before Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai, who expressed concern that the person might be liable to bring a *chatat* — indicating that Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai may have held like Rabbi Yehuda that *melacha she'einah tzrikhah le-gufa* is a full biblical violation.

## Killing Dangerous Animals



### Dangerous Animals Kill

Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi teaches: *kol ha-mazikin neheragin be-Shabbat* — all dangerous animals that cause injury to people may be killed on Shabbat. *Rashi* specifies that this refers to those that are potentially fatal. Crucially, this permission extends even to proactive killing — if one encounters a poisonous snake or scorpion that is not actively attacking, one may still kill it, since this protects others from danger. This constitutes *melacha she'einah tzrikhah le-gufa*; one is not killing for the usual productive purpose of the labor but purely as a protective measure.

### Five Animals

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעַל עַקְרָב שֶׁלֹּא תִּישָּׁךְ. אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ בֶּן לֵוִי: כׇּל הַמַּזִּיקִין נֶהֱרָגִין בְּשַׁבָּת. מֵתִיב רַב יוֹסֵף: חֲמִשָּׁה נֶהֱרָגִין בְּשַׁבָּת, וְאֵלּוּ הֵן: זְבוּב שֶׁבְּאֶרֶץ מִצְרַיִם, וְצִירְעָה שֶׁבְּנִינְוֵה, וְעַקְרָב שֶׁבְּחַדְיָיב, וְנָחָשׁ שֶׁבְּאֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל, וְכֶלֶב שׁוֹטֶה בְּכׇל מָקוֹם. מַנִּי? אִילֵימָא רַבִּי יְהוּדָה — הָא אָמַר: מְלָאכָה שֶׁאֵינָהּ צְרִיכָה לְגוּפָהּ חַיָּיב עָלֶיהָ. אֶלָּא לָאו רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן, וְהָנֵי הוּא דְּשָׁרֵי, אַחֲרִינֵי — לָא!
> **Translation:** **And** we also learned in the mishna that one may cover **a scorpion** with a bowl on Shabbat so **that it will not bite. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi said: All harmful creatures are killed on Shabbat. Rav Yosef raised an objection to this** from the following *baraita*: **Five** creatures **may be killed** even **on Shabbat, and they are: The** poisonous **fly that is in the land of Egypt, and the hornet that is in Ninveh, and the scorpion that is in Ḥadyab, and the snake that is in Eretz Yisrael, and a mad dog in any place.** The Gemara clarifies this: In accordance with **whose** opinion is this *baraita*? **If you say** it is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda, didn’t he say** that **one is liable for a** prohibited **labor that is not needed for its own sake,** and it is therefore prohibited to kill even these creatures? **Rather, is it not** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon, and these are those** creatures **that it is permitted** to kill; **others, no,** it is **not** permitted to kill?

Rav Yosef challenges this from a *baraita*: *chamisha neheragin be-Shabbat* — there are specifically five animals that may be killed on Shabbat: the fly of Egypt, the wasp of Ninveh, the scorpion of Chadyav, the snake of Eretz Yisrael, and a rabid dog anywhere. These five are either particularly lethal or particularly prone to attacking people. But the *baraita* limits the permission to these five — not all dangerous animals — which contradicts Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's general ruling.

The Gemara asks: whose position is the *baraita*? If it represents Rabbi Yehuda, who holds *melacha she'einah tzrikhah le-gufa chayav* — that such labor is a full biblical violation — then even these five could only be killed if they pose an immediate threat of *pikuach nefesh*. If it represents Rabbi Shimon, who permits *melacha she'einah tzrikhah le-gufa*, then the list of five represents an implicit limitation: only these, not all dangerous animals. Either way, it poses a difficulty for Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's sweeping ruling.

Rabbi Yirmiya dismisses the challenge: *dilma meshabeshta hi* — perhaps the *baraita* is a corrupt text. The Gemara does acknowledge that only *baraitot* edited by Rabbi Chiya and Rabbi Oshaya — essentially the *Tosefta* — carry full textual authority; other *baraitot* may contain errors.

### Piety vs Pragmatism

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה: וּמַאן נֵימָא לַן דְּהָא מְתָרַצְתָּא הִיא? דִּילְמָא מְשַׁבַּשְׁתָּא הִיא? אָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: אֲנָא מַתְנֵינָא לַהּ, וְאוֹתֵיבְנָא לַהּ, וַאֲנָא מְתָרֵיצְנָא לַהּ: בְּרָצִין אַחֲרָיו, וְדִבְרֵי הַכֹּל.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yirmeya said: And who will say to us that this** *baraita* **is accurate? Perhaps it is corrupted,** and an objection cannot be raised from it. **Rav Yosef said: I taught** the *baraita* **and raised an objection from it, and I will answer it** as follows: Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi permitted killing all harmful creatures on Shabbat **when they are running after him;** as in that case the danger is real, **and** therefore it is permitted to kill them according to **all opinions.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנֵי תַּנָּא קַמֵּיהּ דְּרָבָא בַּר רַב הוּנָא: הַהוֹרֵג נְחָשִׁים וְעַקְרַבִּים בְּשַׁבָּת — אֵין רוּחַ חֲסִידִים נוֹחָה הֵימֶנּוּ. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: וְאוֹתָן חֲסִידִים אֵין רוּחַ חֲכָמִים נוֹחָה מֵהֶם. וּפְלִיגָא דְּרַב הוּנָא. דְּרַב הוּנָא חַזְיֵיהּ לְהָהוּא גַּבְרָא דְּקָא קָטֵיל זִיבּוּרָא, אֲמַר לֵיהּ: שַׁלֵּימְתִּינְהוּ לְכוּלְּהוּ?
> **Translation:** The ***tanna*** who recited tannaitic literature **before Rava bar Rav Huna taught** a *baraita*: **One who kills snakes and scorpions on Shabbat, the spirit of** the **pious is not pleased with him.** Rava bar Rav Huna **said to him: And** with regard to **those pious, the spirit of the Sages is not pleased with them,** as snakes and scorpions harm people. The Gemara comments: **And** this statement **disagrees** with the opinion of **Rav Huna,** for **Rav Huna saw a person killing a hornet** on Shabbat and **said to him: Have you finished killing all** the hornets? This indicates that he was not pleased with him.

Rav Yosef, however, objects to this dismissal: *ana matniti lah* — that is my *baraita*, a tradition I myself preserve, and it should not be discarded simply because it is inconvenient. Yet he also resolves the difficulty himself: *be'rutzo batreihu de'kulei alma la peligi* — the *baraita* is discussing the case where these animals are actually pursuing and threatening a person. In that case, all authorities agree they may be killed, for it is *pikuach nefesh*. The *baraita* is therefore not about proactive killing at all; it is about reactive self-defense. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's ruling, by contrast, permits proactive killing according to Rabbi Shimon for all genuinely dangerous animals, not only these five.

A *tanna* teaches before Rava bar Rav Huna: *ha'horeg nechashim ve'akravim be'Shabbat ein ruach chasidim nocha heimenu* — one who goes out of his way to kill snakes and scorpions on Shabbat, the spirit of the pious does not rest easily with him. Even if such killing is only a *de'rabbanan* prohibition under Rabbi Shimon, it is unseemly to go about actively hunting these animals. Rava bar Rav Huna responds sharply: *ve'otan chasidim ein ruach chachamim nocha mehem* — those so-called pious people do not find favor with the sages. The sages take a broader view of public welfare, and one should not be excessively stringent at the expense of communal safety.

### Divine Providence

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: נִזְדַּמְּנוּ לוֹ נְחָשִׁים וְעַקְרַבִּים, הֲרָגָן — בְּיָדוּעַ שֶׁנִּזְדַּמְּנוּ לוֹ לְהוֹרְגָן. לֹא הֲרָגָן — בְּיָדוּעַ שֶׁנִּזְדַּמְּנוּ לְהוֹרְגוֹ וְנַעֲשָׂה לוֹ נֵס מִן הַשָּׁמַיִם. אָמַר עוּלָּא וְאִיתֵּימָא רַבָּה בַּר בַּר חָנָה אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: בְּנִישּׁוֹפִין בּוֹ.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: One who **snakes and scorpions happened** before him, if he **killed them, it is clear that they happened** before **him** in order for him **to kill them.** If **he did not kill them it is clear that they happened** before **him** in order for them **to kill him, but a miracle from heaven transpired for him** and he was saved. **Ulla said, and some say** it was **Rabba bar bar Ḥana** who said that **Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** This is referring to **when they were touching** and brushing up against him (Rabbeinu Ḥananel), as in that case if they cause him no harm, it is surely a miracle that saved him.

A *baraita* offers a striking theological framing: *nizdamnu lo nechashim ve'akravim* — if snakes and scorpions happen to cross one's path on Shabbat and one kills them, *be'yaduah shenizdamnu lo lehorgan* — it is evident that heaven arranged for them to cross one's path so that one would have the merit of eliminating them. If one did not kill them, *be'yaduah shenizdamnu lehorgo* — they were sent to kill, and a miracle from heaven intervened to spare the person. This framing functions as a theological endorsement of proactive killing: encountering such creatures is itself providential, and acting to eliminate them is meritorious. Ulla (or in some versions, Rabbah bar bar Chana) in the name of Rabbi Yochanan qualifies this: the providential inference — that they were sent to kill — applies only when the animals were actually hissing aggressively at the person, not when they were simply encountered incidentally.

### Incidental vs Proactive

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי אַבָּא בַּר כָּהֲנָא: פַּעַם אַחַת נָפַל אֶחָד בְּבֵית הַמִּדְרָשׁ, וְעָמַד נִיוְתִּי אֶחָד וַהֲרָגוֹ. אָמַר רַבִּי: פָּגַע בּוֹ כַּיּוֹצֵא בּוֹ!
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Abba bar Kahana said: One time, a** snake **fell into the study hall** on Shabbat, **and a Nabatean [*Nivati*] stood and killed it. Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **said: One of its type killed it.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיבַּעְיָא לְהוּ: ״פָּגַע בּוֹ כַּיּוֹצֵא בּוֹ״ דְּשַׁפִּיר עֲבַד, אוֹ לָא? תָּא שְׁמַע, דְּרַבִּי אַבָּא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי חִיָּיא בַּר אַבָּא וְרַבִּי זֵירָא הֲווֹ יָתְבִי אַקִּילְעָא דְּבֵי רַבִּי יַנַּאי. נְפַק מִילְּתָא מִבֵּינַיְיהוּ, בְּעוֹ מִינֵּיהּ מֵרַבִּי יַנַּאי: מַהוּ לַהֲרוֹג נְחָשִׁים וְעַקְרַבִּים בְּשַׁבָּת? אֲמַר לְהוּ: צִירְעָה אֲנִי הוֹרֵג, נָחָשׁ וְעַקְרָב — לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן?! דִילְמָא לְפִי תּוּמּוֹ. דַּאֲמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: רוֹק דּוֹרְסוֹ לְפִי תּוּמּוֹ. וְאָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: נָחָשׁ דּוֹרְסוֹ לְפִי תּוּמּוֹ. וְאָמַר רַב קַטִּינָא: עַקְרָב דּוֹרְסוֹ לְפִי תּוּמּוֹ.
> **Translation:** **A dilemma was raised before** the Sages: When Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi said: **One of its type killed it,** did he mean that **he acted properly or not? Come** and **hear** a resolution to this dilemma from that which was related about **Rabbi Abba, son of Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba, and Rabbi Zeira,** who **were sitting in the courtyard of Rabbi Yannai’s house. A matter emerged from among them,** and **they raised a dilemma before Rabbi Yannai: What is** the ruling with regard **to killing snakes and scorpions on Shabbat? He said to them: I would kill a hornet, all the more so** would I kill **a snake or a scorpion,** as they are more dangerous and it is permitted to kill them. The Gemara rejects this: There is no conclusive proof from this, as **perhaps** it is only permitted when one steps on it **innocently** as he is walking, so that it does not appear to others that he intended to step on it. **As Rav Yehuda said:** With regard to **spittle** on Shabbat, one may **trample it innocently** and need not be concerned about the prohibitions of smoothing or leveling holes. **And Rav Sheshet said:** With regard to **a snake, one may trample it innocently. And Rav Ketina said:** With regard to **a scorpion, one may trample it innocently.**

A final story is cited: Rabbah bar bar Chana reports that once a scorpion or snake appeared in the study hall and a certain person stood up and killed it. Rabbi said of him: *paga bo ke-yotzei bo* — "something just like it encountered him." The *baraita* leaves ambiguous whether this was a compliment — the creature was attacking, and he courageously dispatched it — or an insult — he himself behaved like a dangerous creature in going out of his way to kill it.

The Gemara concludes with a practical clarification from the incident involving Rabbi Abba son of Rabbi Chiya bar Abba and Rav Zevid, who were sitting on the porch of Rabbi Yanai when a snake appeared from between them. They asked Rabbi Yanai whether one may kill snakes and scorpions on Shabbat. He replied: I even kill wasps — snakes and scorpions, *a fortiori*. The Gemara qualifies this ruling: *dilma le-fi tumo* — perhaps this applies only incidentally. That is, the permission may extend to stepping on or passing over a creature in the course of one's normal movement, but not to going out of one's way specifically to chase it down and kill it. Within the framework of Rabbi Shimon — who treats proactive killing as only a *de'rabbanan* violation of *melacha she'einah tzrikhah le-gufa* — there remains a spectrum of permissibility: some hold it is entirely encouraged, others discourage it, and still others permit it only when done incidentally rather than through deliberate pursuit.
