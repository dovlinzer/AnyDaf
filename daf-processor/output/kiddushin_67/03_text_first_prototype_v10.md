## Two Witnesses Debate



### One vs. Two Witnesses

The sugya opens with the dispute between Abaye and Rava concerning the principle of *ein davar she-be'erva pahot mi-shenayim* — that matters of personal status require no fewer than two witnesses. The question is not merely about the formal act of *kiddushin* or *get*, where two witnesses are needed to constitute the act itself, but about the evidentiary standard required to establish that such an act occurred, or to determine personal status more broadly. Rava holds that two witnesses are required both to effectuate and to prove matters of personal status. Abaye disagrees, maintaining that one uncontested witness suffices for proof even in such matters.

The stakes of this dispute extend well beyond the narrow domain of marriage and divorce. The same standard of proof applies, for instance, to questions of whether a woman was taken captive — which would render her forbidden to her *kohen* husband — or whether a woman committed adultery, which would render her forbidden to her husband entirely.

### Mikvah Case Source

Rava introduces his proof from a *mishnah* (cited also in the *Tosefta*): *Amar Rabbi Shimon* — there was an incident involving a *megurah*, a cistern or body of water, in a place called Diskim (or, as Rashi notes, this may be a personal name rather than a place), situated in Yavneh. This *mikvah* had long stood with a *chezkat shelemah* — a presumption of having sufficient water. When it was eventually measured, it was found to be lacking the requisite forty *se'ah*.

The question that arises is: what is the status of all the purifications performed using this *mikvah* since the last time it was confirmed to be complete? The *mikvah* did not become deficient the very day after the prior measurement. So when did it lose its valid status, and what are the consequences for everything done in the interim?

### Mikvah Dispute

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״נִתְגַּיְּירוּ הַלֵּךְ אַחַר הַפָּגוּם שֶׁבִּשְׁנֵיהֶם״ – בְּמַאי? אִילֵימָא בְּמִצְרִי שֶׁנָּשָׂא עַמּוֹנִית, מַאי פָּגוּם אִיכָּא, ״עַמּוֹנִי״ – וְלֹא עַמּוֹנִית!
> **Translation:** The Gemara discusses the second clause of Rabbi Yoḥanan’s statement: If **they converted, follow the flawed** lineage **of the two. To which** case does this refer? **If we say** that it is referring **to an Egyptian man who married an Ammonite woman, what flawed** lineage **is there** here? The lineage of an Ammonite woman who converts is not flawed at all, as the Sages expounded that the verse: “An Ammonite…shall not enter into the assembly of the Lord” (Deuteronomy 23:4) is referring to **an Ammonite man but not an Ammonite woman,** which means that she can marry a Jew of unflawed lineage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא בְּעַמּוֹנִי שֶׁנָּשָׂא מִצְרִית, וְאִי זָכָר הָוֵי – הַאי שִׁדְיֵהּ אַבָּתְרֵיהּ דִּידֵיהּ, וְאִם נְקֵבָה הָוְיָא – שִׁדְיַהּ אַבָּתְרַהּ דִּידַהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** it must be referring **to an Ammonite man who married an Egyptian woman, and** in that case **if** the child **is a male, cast him after** his father, and render him permanently prohibited from entering the congregation as an Ammonite male. **And if** the child born to them **is a female, cast her after** the mother, so that she is considered a second-generation Egyptian, who may not marry a Jew of unflawed lineage.

Two *tanna'im* debate the question. Rabbi Tarfon rules leniently: *mikvah zeh be-hezkat shalem hu omed* — the *mikvah* stands with a presumption of being complete. He invokes the principle of *chazakah*: since the *mikvah* had a recognized valid status, one cannot retroactively undermine that status based on mere doubt about when it became deficient. The doubt does not override the *status quo* of the *mikvah* being *shalem*. This is the foundational logic of *chazakah*: we assume the prior status persists until we have definitive evidence that it changed, which means we push the change to the last possible moment — when the measurement was actually taken.

Rabbi Akiva, however, rules stringently. He too invokes *chazakah*, but from the opposite direction: *adam zeh be-hezkat tame hu omed* — the person who immersed had a *status quo* of being *tameh* before entering the *mikvah*. Just as one cannot arbitrarily strip away the *mikvah's* status based on doubt, one equally cannot strip away the person's prior *tumah* status based on doubt. The fundamental question thus becomes a collision of two competing *chazakot*: the *chazakah* of the *mikvah* as valid versus the *chazakah* of the person as *tameh*.

Rabbi Tarfon then introduces a *mashal*: consider a *kohen* who was standing and offering *korbanot*, and it subsequently came to light that his mother had been divorced before marrying his father, making him a *ben grushah* and hence a *challal*. In that case, his *avodah* performed prior to the discovery is declared *ksherah* — valid. Now, admittedly, once we have this information, we know retroactively that he was a *challal* all along. Logically, one might expect his past *avodah* to be invalidated. Yet the *halacha* is that it is not, and the *gemara* will derive this from a *pasuk*. It is worth pausing here to notice what kind of disqualification the *ben grushah* case involves: the *kohen's* own body and lineage are not inherently defective — the problem is entirely external to him, arising from his parents' conduct. His disqualification, in other words, *psulo me-acherim* — derives from others. This distinction, between a defect that is internal to a person or object and one that originates externally, will prove central to the reasoning that follows. Rabbi Tarfon is invoking this case analogically, perhaps also gesturing toward the practical and societal consequences of the alternative — if a *kohen* at age seventy discovered he was a *ben grushah*, are we to retroactively recall and invalidate everything he performed over the preceding decades? There is reason, policy and otherwise, to affirm the prior status.

### Comparative Arguments

Rabbi Akiva responds with a counter-analogy: consider a *kohen* who was offering *korbanot* and was subsequently discovered to have a *mum* — a bodily blemish. In that case, his *avodah* is *pesulah* — retroactively invalid. This is striking, because intuitively one might have thought the opposite: being a *ben grushah* or *ben chalutzah* is a more fundamental deficiency of status — it goes to the heart of one's *kehuna* — whereas a *ba'al mum* retains his *kedushat kehuna* in every other respect. Yet the *halacha* distinguishes sharply: the *ba'al mum* whose *avodah* is retroactively disqualified, and the *ben grushah* whose *avodah* is retroactively valid.

Rabbi Akiva's point is structural: these two cases generate different legal consequences, so one must ask which case the *mikvah* scenario more closely resembles. And he argues that the *mikvah* case resembles the *ba'al mum* case: just as by a *ba'al mum* a single witness suffices to establish the disqualification, similarly, the deficiency of the *mikvah* can be established by one person — the deficiency is, so to speak, *pesulah bi-gufo*, present in the body of the thing itself. By contrast, the disqualification of a *ben grushah* or *ben chalutzah* — *psulo me-acherim* — derives from others, namely the conduct of the parents, and requires two witnesses.

Rabbi Akiva then formalizes the comparison: *mikvah pasul bi-yachid u-va'al mum pasul bi-yachid* — the deficiency of the *mikvah* is established by one person and the *ba'al mum* is established by one person. But the *ben grushah* and *ben chalutzah* — *psulo bi-shnayim* — their disqualification requires two witnesses. This is precisely the reason the *gemara* introduced this entire passage: to prove that matters of *erva*, of personal status, require two witnesses. He additionally offers a second line of comparison: *mikvah pesulah be-gufah, ba'al mum pasul be-gufo* — the problem is inherent in the body of the *mikvah* or the person, whereas *ben grushah u-ven chalutzah* — *psulo me-acherim* — the problem originates externally, from what others did.

Rabbi Tarfon, unable to counter this reasoning, concedes with characteristic rhetorical flourish: *kol ha-poreish mimcha ke-poreish min ha-chayim* — anyone who separates from you separates from life itself.

### Contested Testimony

The *gemara* then turns to interrogate the precise meaning of the *ba'al mum* case. It asks: *hai ba'al mum she-pesulah bi-yachid heichi dami?* — What exactly is the case where one witness suffices for a *ba'al mum*? If the *kohen* himself is contesting the claim — denying he has a blemish — would we really believe a single witness against him? Surely not. So presumably, says Rava, the case is where the *kohen* is not contesting the testimony — *de-shatik* — and one uncontested witness is believed. And yet, even in that scenario, by a *ben grushah* and *ben chalutzah* two witnesses are still required. This is Rava's proof: even uncontested testimony of a single witness is insufficient for personal status matters; two are always required.

Abaye responds directly — and this is precisely the counterargument that presents itself naturally. Abaye says: *le'olam de-ka machish lei* — actually, we are dealing with a contested case. The *kohen* is denying it. But why, then, do we believe the single witness? Because the witness can say to the *kohen*: *shlach achvei* — strip and show us. If you deny having a blemish, demonstrate it. The single witness is believed even over the *kohen's* objection because the claim is objectively verifiable — the *ba'al mum* is *pesulah be-gufo*, the deficiency is on the body itself, and the *kohen's* refusal to demonstrate substantiates the witness's account.

This is precisely why the comparison to *ben grushah* and *ben chalutzah* breaks down for Abaye. In that case, if the *kohen* contests a single witness's claim that he is a *ben grushah*, there is nothing to show, no demonstration possible. The status derives entirely from a historical fact about the parents — *psulo me-acherim* — and so a contested single witness cannot be believed, because there is no objective verification to demand. Hence, for contested testimony about personal status where there is nothing to demonstrate, two witnesses are required. But for Abaye, this is the reason for the two-witness requirement in the *ben grushah* case, not a general rule that personal status always requires two witnesses. It is worth pausing to note what Abaye has done here: he has reframed the entire evidentiary question. The issue is not whether the subject matter is *davar she-be'erva* — personal status — as such, but whether the underlying fact is physically demonstrable. Where it is demonstrable, a single witness suffices even in a contested case; where it is not demonstrable, the absence of any verification mechanism is precisely what demands two witnesses. The two-witness requirement thus tracks epistemic access to the contested fact, not the category of law in which it arises. Whether this reframing succeeds as a full account of Rava's position is a question the *gemara* will probe in the section that follows.

### Personal Status Rule

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֹּל שֶׁאֵין לָהּ עָלָיו קִידּוּשִׁין. מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי? דְּאָמַר רַבִּי חִיָּיא בַּר אַבָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן וּמָטוּ בָּהּ מִשּׁוּם דְּרַבִּי יַנַּאי, וְרַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרָבָא וּמָטוּ בָּהּ מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי: אָמַר קְרָא: ״וְיָצְאָה מִבֵּיתוֹ וְהָלְכָה וְהָיְתָה לְאִישׁ אַחֵר״ – לַאֲחֵרִים וְלֹא לִקְרוֹבִים.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that in **any** case **where** a woman **cannot** join in **betrothal with him,** but she can join in betrothal with others, the offspring is a *mamzer*. The Gemara explains: **From where are these matters** derived? **As Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba says** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says, and** some **determined** that **it** was said **in the name of Rabbi Yannai; and Rav Aḥa, son of Rava, determined** that **it** was said **in the name of Rabbi Yosei HaGelili: The verse states** with regard to a divorced woman: **“And she departs out of his house, and goes and becomes another man’s wife”** (Deuteronomy 24:2). This teaches that she can become the wife **of others, but not of relatives,** i.e., betrothal to forbidden relatives does not take effect.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְקֵיף לַהּ רַבִּי אַבָּא: וְאֵימָא ״לְאַחֵר״ וְלֹא לְבֵן! בֵּן בְּהֶדְיָא כְּתִיב בֵּיהּ: ״לֹא יִקַּח אִישׁ אֶת אֵשֶׁת אָבִיו״, ״אַחֵר״ לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ לַאֲחֵרִים וְלֹא לִקְרוֹבִים.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Abba objects to this: But** one can **say** and explain that the term **“another”** in the verse indicates: **But not to** her husband’s **son,** i.e., betrothal is ineffective only in the case of a prohibition that warrants court-imposed capital punishment, not one that warrants *karet*. The Gemara rejects this suggestion: **With regard to a son it is explicitly written: “A man shall not take his father’s wife”** (Deuteronomy 23:1), which means that betrothal is ineffective in this situation. If so, **why do I** need the emphasis of the term **“another”? Learn from** this term **that** she may marry **others but not relatives.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵימָא: אִידֵּי וְאִידֵּי לְבֵן, הָא – לְכַתְּחִילָּה, וְהָא – דִּיעֲבַד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges this explanation: **But** one can **say** that both **this** verse **and that** verse are referring **to a son** and yet both are necessary, as **this** verse: “Shall not take,” is referring to the *halakha **ab initio*****.** It does not mean that betrothal is ineffective, but merely that one may not marry his father’s wife. **And that** verse: “And becomes another man’s wife,” teaches that even **after the fact,** if the son attempted to betroth his father’s wife, his act is of no consequence.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְכַתְּחִילָּה מֵאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה נָפְקָא: וּמָה אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה, בְּכָרֵת לָא תָּפְסִי בַּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין, חַיָּיבֵי מִיתוֹת בֵּית דִּין לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן?
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: One learns that betrothal is ineffective in this case ***ab initio*** from a different source, as it is **derived from** the prohibition proscribing **a wife’s sister** by the following *a fortiori* inference: **If,** with regard to the prohibition proscribing **a wife’s sister,** whose transgression is punished **with *karet*, she cannot be betrothed** by her sister’s husband, in accordance with the verse: “And you shall not take a woman with her sister” (Leviticus 18:18), then with regard to cases that entail **liability to** receive **court-imposed capital punishment,** e.g., intercourse with one’s father’s wife, is it **not all the more so** the case that betrothal does not take effect?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵימָא: אִידֵּי וְאִידֵּי בַּאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה, הָא – לְכַתְּחִילָּה, הָא – דִיעֲבַד! אִין הָכִי נָמֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests another interpretation of the verses. **But** as the verse has already prohibited betrothal to a wife’s sister, one can **say** as follows: Both **this** verse: “You shall not take” (Leviticus 18:18), **and that** one: “Becomes another man’s wife” (Deuteronomy 24:2), are referring **to a wife’s sister,** as **this** one: “You shall not take,” prohibits this relationship ***ab initio*,** whereas **that** verse: “Another man’s wife,” teaches that this betrothal is of no effect even **after the fact.** The Gemara responds: **Yes, it is indeed so.** The verse should be interpreted in this manner.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַשְׁכְּחַן אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה, שְׁאָר עֲרָיוֹת מְנָלַן? יָלְפִינַן מֵאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה: מָה אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה מְיוּחֶדֶת, שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת – וְלָא תָּפְסִי בָּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין, אַף כֹּל שֶׁהִיא עֶרְוָה וְחַיָּיבִין עַל זְדוֹנָהּ כָּרֵת וְעַל שִׁגְגָתָהּ חַטָּאת – לָא תָּפְסִי בָּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **We found** a source for the prohibition proscribing **a wife’s sister; from where do we** derive that betrothal is also ineffective for the **other forbidden relations?** The Gemara answers: **We derive** it **from** the case of **a wife’s sister,** by means of the following analogy: **Just as** the prohibition with regard to **a wife’s sister,** which is **specified** by the Torah, **is** a prohibition of **a forbidden relative** that is punished with ***karet* for its intentional** transgression **and** requires **a sin-offering for its unwitting** transgression, **and betrothal is ineffective in** this case, **so too,** with regard to **any** prohibition **that** involves **a forbidden relative whose intentional** transgression is punished with ***karet* and whose unwitting** transgression renders one liable to bring **a sin-offering, betrothal is** likewise **ineffective in** those cases.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא כּוּלְּהוּ אָתְיָין, אֶלָּא אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ וְאֵשֶׁת אָח – אִיכָּא לְמִיפְרַךְ: מָה לַאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה, שֶׁכֵּן אֵין לָהּ הֶיתֵּר בִּמְקוֹם מִצְוָה – תֹּאמַר בְּאֵשֶׁת אָח שֶׁיֵּשׁ לָהּ הֶיתֵּר בִּמְקוֹם מִצְוָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Granted,** virtually **all** other forbidden relatives can be **derived** in this manner. **But** the cases of **a married woman and a brother’s wife** are exceptions, and the analogy in these cases **can be refuted** as follows. **What** is unique **about a wife’s sister is that she is not permitted in the case of a mitzva,** as even where the mitzva of levirate marriage would apply, one still may not marry one’s wife’s sister. **Will you say** the same **with regard to a brother’s wife, who is permitted in the case of** the **mitzva** of levirate marriage?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ נָמֵי אִיכָּא לְמִיפְרַךְ: מָה לְהָנָךְ, שֶׁכֵּן אֵין לָהּ הֶיתֵּר בְּחַיֵּי אוֹסְרָן – תֹּאמַר בְּאֵשֶׁת אִישׁ שֶׁיֵּשׁ לָהּ הֶיתֵּר בְּחַיֵּי אוֹסְרָן!
> **Translation:** With regard to **a married woman** the interpretation **can likewise be refuted: What** is unique **about these** cases of a wife’s sister and a brother’s wife is **that they cannot be permitted during the lives of those whose** existence **renders them prohibited,** as neither a wife’s sister nor a brother’s wife can be permitted while the person who causes the prohibition is alive. This caveat is added because one’s wife’s sister does become permitted once his wife has died. **Will you say** the same **with regard to a married woman, who** can become **permitted during the life of the one who renders her forbidden,** by means of a divorce?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹנָה וְאִיתֵּימָא רַב הוּנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יְהוֹשֻׁעַ: אָמַר קְרָא: ״כׇּל אֲשֶׁר יַעֲשֶׂה מִכֹּל הַתּוֹעֵבֹת הָאֵלֶּה וְנִכְרְתוּ״, הוּקְשׁוּ כׇּל הָעֲרָיוֹת כּוּלָּם לַאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה, מָה אֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה לֹא תָּפְסִי בָּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין – אַף כׇּל עֲרָיוֹת כּוּלָּם לֹא תָּפְסִי בָּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** this *halakha*, that betrothal is ineffective for other forbidden relatives, is derived from a different source, as **Rabbi Yona says, and some say** this was taught by **Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua: The verse states** explicitly in the chapter dealing with forbidden relatives: **“Whoever shall do any of these abominations shall be cut off”** (Leviticus 18:29). In this verse **all those with whom relations are forbidden are juxtaposed** to one another, and therefore also **to a wife’s sister: Just as betrothal is not effective** in the case of **a wife’s sister, so too, betrothal is not effective with regard to all those with whom relations are forbidden.**

> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **If so,** that this *halakha* is derived from here,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲפִילּוּ נִדָּה נָמֵי! אַלְּמָה אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: הַכֹּל מוֹדִים בְּבָא עַל הַנִּדָּה וְעַל הַסּוֹטָה, שֶׁאֵין הַוָּלָד מַמְזֵר? אָמַר חִזְקִיָּה: אָמַר קְרָא ״וּתְהִי נִדָּתָהּ עָלָיו״, אֲפִילּוּ בִּשְׁעַת נִדָּתָהּ – תְּהֵא בָּהּ הֲוָיָה.
> **Translation:** then **even** if he betrothed **a menstruating woman as well,** his betrothal should not be effective and the offspring should be a *mamzer*, as a menstruating woman is included in the list in that chapter of those with whom sexual intercourse is forbidden. If so, **why** did **Abaye say: All concede with regard to one who engages in intercourse with a menstruating woman or with a *sota*,** a woman forbidden to her husband on suspicion of being unfaithful to him, **that the offspring is not a *mamzer*? Ḥizkiyya said:** In the case of a menstruating woman, **the verse states: “And her impurity be [*ut’hi*] upon him”** (Leviticus 15:24), from which it is derived that **even at the time of her impurity,** the type of **becoming [*havaya*]** stated with regard to betrothal (see Deuteronomy 24:2) **should** apply **to her.** The Gemara is interpreting the connection between the words *ut’hi* and *havaya*, as both share the same Hebrew root.

The *gemara* does not definitively resolve the dispute at this juncture. Whether the *ba'al mum* case was contested or uncontested remains open — for if the *mum* is internal, there is nothing to demonstrate in any event, making it structurally more similar to an uncontested case, which would restore Rava's proof. The *gemara* then briefly addresses the underlying *halachic* sources for the distinction between *ben grushah* and *ba'al mum*. How do we know a *kohen* discovered to be a *ben grushah* does not have his past *avodah* invalidated? Rav Yehuda citing Shmuel derives it from the *pasuk* concerning Pinchas: *ve-haytah lo u-le-zaro acharav* — the covenant of *kehuna* extends to his descendants, *bein zera kasher bein zera pasul*, whether the offspring is fit or disqualified. The father of Shmuel derives it from the verse *bareich Hashem cheilo* — bless his strength — reading *cheilo* as referring even to *chullin she-bo*, those whose sanctity may be impaired, and *u-po'al yadav tirtzeh* as indicating acceptance of his service through the moment of discovery.

Rabbi Yochanan offers yet another source: *u-vata el ha-kohen asher yihyeh ba-yamim ha-hem* — you shall come to the *kohen* who will be in those days. This teaches that even if it turns out the *kohen* before whom you stood was later discovered to be unfit, the interaction was valid — *zeh kasher ve-nit-chalal*, he was fit and became disqualified thereafter.

Regarding the *ba'al mum*, Rav Yehuda citing Shmuel derives from the continuation of the Pinchas passage: *lachen emor hinni noten lo et briti shalom* — the covenant of *shalom* applies only *ke-shehu shalem*, when he is whole and unblemished, *ve-lo ke-shehu chaser*, not when he is lacking. The word *shalom* in the *pasuk* is written with a broken *vav* — a *vav keti'a* — as Rav Nachman notes, a scribal tradition attested in *Sifrei Torah*, with various traditions as to the precise form. This textual irregularity is the basis for reading *shalom* as *shalem*, and thereby limiting the grant of validity to an unblemished *kohen*.

On the conceptual level, this distinction remains genuinely puzzling. A *challal* has no *kedushat kehuna* at all — he is exempt from all its prohibitions and obligations — while a *ba'al mum* retains his sanctity fully, merely being unable to perform the *avodah*. Yet it is the *challal* whose prior *avodah* is retroactively valid and the *ba'al mum* whose prior *avodah* is retroactively disqualified. One possible line of reasoning, parallel to a point the *gemara* makes in another context, is that the *ba'al mum's* disqualification was present and objectively discoverable — it was there to be seen — whereas the *challal's* disqualification was a matter of hidden historical fact. That which was discoverable and observable carries a greater legal charge. But this remains difficult.

The practical ruling follows Rava — we generally rule *ein davar she-be'erva pahot mi-shenayim* even as a standard of proof. This has significant implications beyond invalidating personal status: a woman seeking to prove she is divorced, or a convert seeking to prove the validity of his conversion, faces the same evidentiary threshold. These remain live questions wherever status must be affirmatively established.

## Genealogical Status



### Child Follows Father

The *mishnah* opens with a tripartite framework governing *yichus* — genealogical status — based on the nature of the union between the parents.

The first principle: *kol makom she-yesh kiddushin ve-ein aveira, ha-velad holech achar ha-zachar* — wherever *kiddushin* is binding and there is no transgression, the child's status follows the father. The *mishnah* illustrates this with the most basic case: whether the mother is a *kohenet*, a *leviyah*, or a *yisraelit*, and the father is a *kohen*, *levi*, or *yisrael*, the child inherits the father's tribal and priestly status. A *kohen* father produces a *kohen* child regardless of the mother's background. This is the standard, unexceptional case of a valid and permitted marriage.

The *gemara* also records a significant exception to the general categories of unions that produce *mamzerim*: *hakol modin b'va al ha-niddah v'al ha-sotah she-ein ha-velad mamzer* — everyone agrees that one who has relations with a *niddah* or a *sotah*, the child is not a *mamzer*. This is notable because it cuts across what might otherwise be expected from the general rules. According to Rabbi Shimon HaTimni, who holds that any *chayav kareit* union produces a *mamzer*, *niddah* is nonetheless an exception — the child is not a *mamzer* despite *niddah* being a *chayav kareit* prohibition. And according to Rabbi Akiva, who holds that even *chayevei lavin* produce a *mamzer*, *sotah* is nonetheless excluded: the *issur lav* that forbids a husband from resuming relations with his wife after she has committed adultery does not cause a child subsequently born of that resumed relationship to be a *mamzer*. Both *niddah* and *sotah*, then, stand outside the framework that would otherwise assign *mamzer* status, a point the *mishnah's* broader discussion will continue to develop.

### Child Follows Disqualifi

The second principle: *kol makom she-yesh kiddushin ve-yesh aveira, ha-velad holech achar ha-pagum* — wherever *kiddushin* is binding but the union involves transgression, the child follows the disqualified party. The examples given are the forbidden unions of *kohanim*: an *almanah* with a *kohen gadol*, or a *grushah* or *chalutzah* with a *kohen hedyot*. The child born of such a union does not himself become a *kohen gadol* or a divorcée — rather, the child becomes a *challal*, stripped of the full sanctity of *kehuna*, which is the operative consequence of a forbidden *kohen* union. The conceptual principle is that the stigma of the forbidden relationship attaches to the offspring, regardless of whether the disqualified party is the father or the mother.

### Mamzer Status

The third principle extends this logic to *mamzer* status: if a woman who is a *mamzeret* marries a *yisrael*, or a *yisrael* woman marries a *mamzer*, the child is a *mamzer*. The *kiddushin* in such unions is binding — since the prohibition is an *isur lav* and not an *isur karet* or punishable by death — but the child inherits the disqualified status of whichever parent bears it. This applies symmetrically regardless of whether the *mamzer* is the father or the mother.

### Matrilineal Descent

The *mishnah* then turns to two remaining categories defined by the woman's status. First: *kol mi she-ein lah alav kiddushin aval yesh lah al acherim kiddushin* — a woman with whom *kiddushin* is not binding for this particular man, but who can nonetheless contract valid marriage with others — *ha-velad mamzer*. This is the category of *arayot*, the forbidden incestuous and adulterous relations enumerated in the Torah. For instance, a married woman's union with someone other than her husband produces a *mamzer*, because *kiddushin* cannot take effect with the adulterer, but she remains capable of valid marriage with her husband.

Finally: *kol mi she-ein lah lo alav ve-lo al acherim kiddushin* — a woman who cannot contract valid marriage with anyone — *ha-velad kemotah*, the child follows the mother's status and is not a *mamzer*. The *mishnah* identifies this category as the *vlad shifcha ve-nochrit*, the child of a non-Jewish woman or a slave woman. Since no Jew can contract *kiddushin* with a non-Jewish woman or a slave, and since these women cannot marry any Jew, the child follows the mother entirely — and is either a non-Jew or a slave, not a *mamzer*. This is the *mishnah's* expression of matrilineal descent: where no Jewish *kiddushin* can attach to the mother in any circumstances, the child's status is defined entirely by her.

## Rule vs. Exception



### Challenge to Completeness

The *gemara* opens with Rabbi Shimon challenging Rabbi Yochanan: *klala hu?* — Is this general principle truly comprehensive? Does the rule *kol makom she-yesh kiddushin ve-ein aveira ha-velad holech achar ha-zachar* genuinely account for all cases?

### Convert & Mamzeret

Rabbi Shimon immediately identifies a problem. *Harei ger she-nasa mamzeret* — consider a convert who married a *mamzeret*. According to one opinion, a *mamzeret* is permitted to marry a *ger*, making the *kiddushin* binding with no transgression. Yet even in that case, the child is a *mamzer* — *ha-velad holech achar ha-pagum*. Here we have a case of valid *kiddushin* with no *aveira*, yet the child does not follow the father; the child follows the stigmatized party. The *mishnah's* general rule is therefore violated.

A *baraita* confirms: *ger she-nasa mamzeret — ha-velad mamzer*. This is the position of Rabbi Yosi. Rabbi Yochanan's response is that our *mishnah* need not be Rabbi Yosi's view. *Matnitin Rabbi Yehuda hi* — perhaps the *mishnah* reflects Rabbi Yehuda's position. Rabbi Yehuda holds that a *ger* is in fact *forbidden* to marry a *mamzeret* — *ger lo yisa mamzeret*. Under Rabbi Yehuda's view, the union involves transgression — *yesh kiddushin ve-yesh aveira* — and so the child following the *pagum* fits squarely within the *mishnah's* second rule. The apparent exception dissolves once we attribute the *mishnah* to Rabbi Yehuda.

### Klal Inclusion

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִכְּדֵי אִיכָּא לְאַקּוֹשַׁהּ לְנִדָּה, וְאִיכָּא לְאַקּוֹשַׁהּ לַאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה, מַאי חָזֵית דְּמַקְּשַׁתְּ לְהוּ לַאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה? אַקְּשַׁהּ לְנִדָּה! קוּלָּא וְחוּמְרָא – לְחוּמְרָא מַקְּשִׁינַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **After all, there is** the possibility **of juxtaposing** all other forbidden relatives **to a menstruating woman, and there is** also the possibility **of juxtaposing** them **to a wife’s sister. What did you see that you juxtaposed them to a wife’s sister?** Why not **juxtapose them** instead **to a menstruating woman?** The Gemara answers: When there is an option of juxtaposing a case in a manner that leads to **a leniency, or** juxtaposing it to a *halakha* that entails **a stringency, we juxtapose** it in a fashion that leads **to a stringency.**

But the *mishnah* does not list the *ger* and *mamzeret* case explicitly under the second rule, even according to Rabbi Yehuda. Why not? The *gemara* answers: *tanna kol makom le-atuyei* — the phrase *kol makom* functions as a *klal*, a general statement broad enough to include unlisted cases within the scope of the rule. The *mishnah* says *kol makom she-yesh kiddushin ve-yesh aveira* — wherever there is *kiddushin* and transgression — and that formulation is deliberately expansive, encompassing the *ger u-mamzeret* case without explicit mention.

Alternatively, even if the *mishnah* reflects Rabbi Yosi's view, the case of *ger u-mamzeret* would be an exception to the first rule, handled by the word *ve-eizo zu* in the *mishnah's* phrasing, as we shall see.

The *gemara* then raises a further unlisted case: *vaharei challal she-nasa bat Yisrael* — a *challal* who married a *bat Yisrael*. This union involves no transgression — a *challal* is not forbidden to a non-*kohen* woman — and *kiddushin* is fully binding. The child follows the father and is a *challal*. This is a straightforward application of the first rule, yet it is absent from the *mishnah's* list. Similarly, a *Yisrael* who married a *challala* — again, no transgression, binding *kiddushin*, and the child follows the father and is *kasher*. Why were these not listed?

The *gemara* answers: *de-lo pasika lei* — the case cannot be stated cleanly given the *mishnah's* framing. If one tried to extend the list to include *challala*, one would write something like: *kohenet, leviyah, Yisraelit, ve-challala she-niset le-kohen, levi, ve-Yisrael* — but then one is implicitly including the possibility of a *challala* marrying a *kohen*, which is in fact *assur*. The case does not lend itself to the tidy phrasing the *mishnah* prefers, and so it was left out despite being a genuine application of the rule. The *klal* of *kol makom* is understood to encompass it nonetheless.

### Eizehu Exceptions

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אַחָא בַּר יַעֲקֹב אָמַר: אָתְיָא בְּקַל וָחוֹמֶר מִיבָמָה: וּמָה יְבָמָה, שֶׁהִיא בְּלָאו – לָא תָּפְסִי בָּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין, חַיָּיבֵי מִיתוֹת וְחַיָּיבֵי כָרֵיתוֹת לֹא כׇּל שֶׁכֵּן? אִי הָכִי, שְׁאָר חַיָּיבֵי לָאוִין נָמֵי!
> **Translation:** **Rav Aḥa bar Ya’akov said** that there is a different source for the *halakha* that betrothal is ineffective with forbidden relatives: This principle **is derived by** means of **an *a fortiori*** inference **from** the case of **a *yevama*: Just as a *yevama*,** before she is released from the *yavam* through *ḥalitza*, **is** forbidden **by** a mere **prohibition,** which entails lashes, and yet **betrothal is not effective with her,** with regard to **those** people with whom sexual intercourse renders one **liable to** receive the **death** penalty **or liable to** be punished with ***karet*,** is it **not all the more so** the case that betrothal should not be effective in these cases? The Gemara asks: **If so,** meaning that this is the source, one should **also** derive that betrothal is ineffective with any **other** people with whom one **is** only **liable** for violating **a prohibition** of engaging in intercourse, by means of the same analogy.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: חַיָּיבֵי לָאוִין בְּהֶדְיָא כְּתִיב בְּהוּ – ״כִּי תִהְיֶיןָ לְאִישׁ שְׁתֵּי נָשִׁים הָאַחַת אֲהוּבָה וְהָאַחַת שְׂנוּאָה״, וְכִי יֵשׁ שְׂנוּאָה לִפְנֵי הַמָּקוֹם וַאֲהוּבָה לִפְנֵי הַמָּקוֹם? אֶלָּא: ״אֲהוּבָה״ – אֲהוּבָה בְּנִישּׂוּאֶיהָ, ״שְׂנוּאָה״ – שְׂנוּאָה בְּנִישּׂוּאֶיהָ. וְקָאָמַר רַחֲמָנָא: ״כִּי תִהְיֶיןָ״.
> **Translation:** Rav Pappa says: It **is written explicitly** in the Torah that a man can betroth women **with whom he is liable** for violating ordinary **prohibitions** of intercourse. The Torah states in a different context: **“If a man has two wives, the one beloved and the one hated”** (Deuteronomy 21:15). Rav Pappa asks rhetorically: **But is there one who is hated before the Omnipresent and one who is beloved before the Omnipresent? Rather, “beloved”** means **beloved in her marriage,** i.e., her marriage is permitted; **“hated”** means **hated in her marriage,** i.e., her marriage involves the violation of a prohibition. **And** despite the fact that the latter marriage is between a man and a woman who are forbidden to one another, their union still has the status of a marriage, as **the Merciful One states: “If a man has** two wives,” i.e., he is married to both of them.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא דְּאָמַר: אֵין קִידּוּשִׁין תּוֹפְסִין בְּחַיָּיבֵי לָאוִין, ״כִּי תִהְיֶיןָ״ בְּמַאי מוֹקֵים? בְּאַלְמָנָה לְכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל, וּכְרַבִּי סִימַאי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And according to** the opinion of **Rabbi Akiva, who says: Betrothal does not take effect** even **with** those women with whom one **is** only **liable** for violating **a prohibition** of engaging in intercourse, **with regard to what** case **does he establish** the verse: **“If a man has** two wives”? The Gemara answers: He explains that this verse is referring **to a widow** married **to a High Priest, and** this is **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Simai.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי סִימַאי אוֹמֵר: מִן הַכֹּל הָיָה רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא עוֹשֶׂה מַמְזֵר חוּץ מֵאַלְמָנָה לְכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל, שֶׁהֲרֵי אָמְרָה תּוֹרָה: ״לֹא יְחַלֵּל״ – חִילּוּלִים עוֹשֶׂה, וְאֵין עוֹשֶׂה מַמְזֵרוּת.
> **Translation:** **As it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Simai says: From all** relationships that involve prohibitions, **Rabbi Akiva would render** the offspring **a *mamzer*, except for** the marriage of **a widow to a High Priest, as the Torah said: “And he shall not profane [*yeḥallel*]”** (Leviticus 21:15), which teaches that **he renders them profane [*ḥillulim*],** i.e., his children from this marriage are *ḥalalim*, **but he does not render** them labeled with ***mamzer* status.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְרַבִּי יְשֵׁבָב, דְּאָמַר: בּוֹאוּ וְנִצְוַוח עַל עֲקִיבָא בֶּן יוֹסֵף שֶׁהָיָה אוֹמֵר: כֹּל שֶׁאֵין לוֹ בִּיאָה בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל – הַוָּלָד מַמְזֵר? הָנִיחָא לְרַבִּי יְשֵׁבָב אִי לְאַפּוֹקֵי מִדְּרַבִּי סִימַאי קָאָתֵי – שַׁפִּיר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** what can be said **according to** the opinion of **Rabbi Yeshevav, who says: Come, let us shout at Akiva ben Yosef, who would say:** In **every** case where **a Jew may not engage in intercourse** with a particular woman, and he does so, **the offspring** that results from this union **is a *mamzer*,** even the child of a widow and a High Priest? **This works out well** even **according to** the opinion of **Rabbi Yeshevav if he comes to exclude the reason of Rabbi Simai,** i.e., if he means to take issue with the ruling of Rabbi Akiva in the specific case mentioned by Rabbi Simai, that of a widow married to a High Priest, then Rabbi Yeshevav too concedes that according to the opinion of Rabbi Akiva, betrothal does take effect in a case where a positive mitzva is violated by the betrothal. Accordingly, one can establish the phrase “and the one hated” (Deuteronomy 21:15) as referring to those whose marriage entailed the violation of a positive mitzva.

Now the *gemara* examines the function of *eizehu* — the word *ve-eizo zu* that introduces the specific lists in the *mishnah*. Initially, there is an assumption that *ve-eizo zu* is exhaustive — that it confines the application of the rule to precisely those cases enumerated. If so, any unlisted application would be a problem for the *mishnah's* completeness. Jenna's observation here is apt: at this stage, the *gemara* is working with the premise that *ve-eizo zu* is limiting, before ultimately arriving at the resolution that *ve-eizo zu* serves a different function.

The *gemara* develops the position that *ve-eizo zu* functions not only to define the scope of the rule but to flag exceptions — cases that fall outside the expected application of the rule. By saying "and which cases are these?" and then providing a list, the *mishnah* implicitly acknowledges that the list does not exhaust all possible applications; there are others not mentioned that nonetheless fall under the *klal*. At the same time, the specificity of the list implies that there are scenarios where one might have expected the rule to apply but where it does not. The word *eizehu* thus does double duty: it confirms that the rule applies to the listed cases, and it signals that certain unlisted cases are exceptions.

One such exception surfaces in the *gemara's* discussion of Rabbi Simai's position on *mamzerus*. *D'tanya Rebbi Simai omer* — the Rabbis taught that Rabbi Simai says — that Rabbi Akiva would derive *mamzerus* from virtually all prohibited unions, with one notable exception: *almana l'Kohen Gadol*, a widow who marries the *kohen gadol*. In that case, Rabbi Simai holds that the Torah's use of the language *lo yechalel* — he shall not desecrate — indicates that such a union produces *chilulim*, the status of *challal*, but does not produce *mamzerus*. The *rishonim* note that the same logic extends beyond the *kohen gadol*: a regular *kohen* who marries a *grushah* likewise produces a *challal* rather than a *mamzer*, because the Torah creates a distinct category for children of *kohanim* who marry those they are forbidden to. This is an important distinction — the *challal* is demoted from *kehunah* but is not in the drastically reduced status of a *mamzer*. A *challal* can marry any Jewish woman he wishes and his granddaughter, through the right alignment of marriages, can even re-enter *kehunah*; whereas the only historical mechanism for exiting *mamzerus* is one that is no longer operative. Rabbi Shaveiv, however, disagrees with Rabbi Simai and maintains that even the child of an *almana l'Kohen Gadol* would be a *mamzer* — a position that carries its own surprising implications, given how few *kohanim gedolim* there are at any one time. The tension between these views then drives the *gemara* to ask whether the relevant *drasha* came to undermine Rabbi Simai's position in favor of Rabbi Shaveiv's, or whether it was articulating something independent.

The overall framework that emerges is this: anything that is an application of the rule not explicitly listed is included under *kol makom*; anything that is an exception to the rule is signaled by *ve-eizo zu*. The two phrases together provide the *mishnah* with the flexibility to be both a general rule and a practically accurate guide.

### Israelite & Challala

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אִי טַעְמָא דְנַפְשֵׁיהּ קָאָמַר, וַאֲפִילּוּ חַיָּיבֵי עֲשֵׂה, בְּמַאי מוֹקֵים לַהּ?
> **Translation:** **But if** he states **a reasoning of his own,** i.e., he states an independent statement critical of Rabbi Akiva’s ruling that the child of any illicit union is a *mamzer*, **and** it is a categorical statement that applies to all illicit unions, **even those liable** for violating **a positive mitzva,** i.e., Rabbi Akiva holds that even the offspring of this relationship is a *mamzer*, **with regard to what** case **does he interpret** the “hated” woman of the above verse?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּבְעוּלָה לְכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל. וּמַאי שְׁנָא? מִשּׁוּם דְּהָוֵי לֵיהּ עֲשֵׂה שֶׁאֵין שָׁוֶה בַּכֹּל.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: Rabbi Yeshevav would say that the verse is referring **to a non-virgin** married **to a High Priest,** as there is a positive mitzva that a High Priest should marry a virgin. The Gemara asks: **And** in **what** way **is** this case **different from** the previous ones? If Rabbi Yeshevav holds that a child born of any act of intercourse prohibited by a positive mitzva is a *mamzer*, the marriage of a non-virgin to a High Priest likewise involves the violation of a positive mitzva. The Gemara answers: **Because it is a positive mitzva that is not equally applicable to all,** and since this command applies only to a High Priest and not to other Jews, its violation is considered less severe than that of other positive mitzvot.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבָּנַן, אַדְּמוֹקֵי לַהּ בְּחַיָּיבֵי לָאוִין, נוֹקְמַהּ בְּחַיָּיבֵי עֲשֵׂה!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** with regard to **the Rabbis,** who disagree with Rabbi Akiva’s opinion, **rather than establishing** the verse: “If a man has two wives, the one beloved and the one hated” (Deuteronomy 21:15), as referring **to** those who are **liable for** violating **prohibitions, let them establish it** as referring **to those liable for** violating **a positive mitzva.** In other words, betrothal should not be effective if it involves the violation of a prohibition. And as for the “hated” woman whose marriage is nevertheless valid, mentioned in that verse, this is referring to one whose engaging in sexual intercourse violates a positive mitzva.

Returning to the unlisted cases of *challal* and *Yisrael* — the *gemara* confirms that *tanna kol makom de-reisha le-atuyei*, the *kol makom* of the opening clause serves to include *Yisrael she-nasa challala* and *challal she-nasa bat Yisrael* as applications of the first rule, even though they were not explicitly enumerated. This is the *gemara's* formal resolution for those cases.

It is worth pausing here to note the position of Rabbi Akiva on a related question that surfaces in this stretch of the *gemara*. The question is which unions produce a *mamzer*, and Rabbi Akiva's view is notably more expansive than that of the *rabbanan* — he holds that *chayvei aseh*, unions prohibited by a positive commandment, can also generate *mamzeirus*. The specific case the *gemara* anchors to is *be'ulah le-kohen gadol*: a *kohen gadol* who marries a woman who was not a virgin violates the commandment *mi-betulah be-amo yikach ishah*, and this is an *issur aseh* rather than a *lav*. According to Rabbi Akiva, this is nevertheless included in the category of unions that produce *mamzeirus*. However, this is a very circumscribed position even for Rabbi Akiva, because on other cases there is *hakol modim* — universal agreement — that *niddah* does not produce a *mamzer*, and similarly *sotah*, a woman who has been adulterous, does not produce a *mamzer* from the prohibited relationship with her husband. The child of the actual adulterer would be a *mamzer*, but in practice the *gemara* notes that one can rely on *rov beilos achar ha-baal* — the presumption that the majority of a married woman's intimacy is with her husband — to avoid that conclusion in most circumstances. So Rabbi Akiva's exceptions to the general rule amount to *niddah*, *sotah*, and *be'ulah le-kohen gadol*, with only the last of these genuinely in play for *mamzeirus* under his view. The *rabbanan*, by contrast, hold that *chayvei lavin* generate *mamzeirus* but do not extend this to *chayvei aseh*, and the *gemara* presses on why they do not simply extend the rule further — a question the *gemara* will continue to develop shortly.

### Framing Difficulty

The *gemara* returns to the question of why the *challal* case was not listed explicitly, even though it is a genuine application of the rule. The answer, as established, is that including *challala* in the list of women would require writing a formulation that also implies a *challala* could marry a *kohen* — *ve-challala le-kohen lo chazya* — which she cannot. The *mishnah* cannot phrase it in a single clean sentence without creating a false implication, and so it leaves the *challal* case to be derived from *kol makom*.

### Egyptian Generations

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנֵי חַיָּיבֵי עֲשֵׂה, בְּמַאי נִינְהוּ? אִי שְׁתֵּיהֶן מִצְרִיּוֹת – שְׁתֵּיהֶן שְׂנוּאוֹת, אִי אַחַת מִצְרִית וְאַחַת יִשְׂרְאֵלִית – שְׁתֵּי נָשִׁים מֵעַם אֶחָד בָּעֵינַן, אִי בְּעוּלָה לְכֹהֵן גָּדוֹל – מִי כְּתִיב ״תִּהְיֶיןָ לְכֹהֵן״?
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **These** cases where they are **liable** for violating **a positive mitzva, what are they? If** you say that **both** wives are **Egyptian** converts, **they are both hated,** as both marriages are prohibited. **If** you claim that **one is an Egyptian woman and the other a Jewish woman** of unflawed lineage, this cannot be the case, as **we require “two wives” from the same nation,** since the Torah equates the two women. **If** the hated one is **a non-virgin** married **to a High Priest,** this too is problematic, as, **is it written: If a priest has** two wives? The verse merely says: “If a man has two wives.” Consequently, the verse cannot be interpreted as referring to those who are liable for violating a positive mitzva.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא? בְּעַל כּוּרְחֵיךְ שִׁבְקֵיהּ לִקְרָא דְּהָוֵי דָּחֵיק, וּמוֹקֵי אַנַּפְשֵׁיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** according to the opinion of **Rabbi Akiva,** that betrothal that involves a prohibition does not take effect, this verse can be referring only to a non-virgin who marries a High Priest, or marriage to a female Egyptian convert, which involve the violation of positive mitzvot. Can the verse really be interpreted as concerning such unlikely cases? The Gemara answers: **You are forced to leave this verse** aside, **as it establishes itself as** dealing with **a difficult** case. In other words, as Rabbi Akiva claims that betrothal is ineffective if any prohibition is involved, he has no choice but to explain the verse that says: “If a man has two wives,” in this forced manner.

The *gemara* identifies another unlisted case: *Mitzri she-nasa Mitzrit* — an Egyptian convert who married a female Egyptian convert. The *halacha* is that an Egyptian convert may enter the community of Israel (*kahal Hashem*) only from the third generation: *dor shelishi yavo lahem bi-kehal Hashem*. The question is how generations are counted when the parents are of different generational standing within the Egyptian convert lineage.

Rabba bar bar Chana citing Rabbi Yochanan states: *Mitzri sheni she-nasa Mitzrit rishona — bena shelishi havei* — if a second-generation Egyptian convert married a first-generation Egyptian female convert, the child is a third-generation, and consequently permitted. Generations are counted by the father. This is another application of the rule that the child follows the father — *yesh kiddushin, ein aveira, ha-velad holech achar ha-zachar* — and it is included in the *klal* of *kol makom*.

Before moving past the Egyptian case, the *gemara* pauses to press on what exactly the governing *pasuk* is describing. The verse, *ki tiheyenah l'ish shtei nashim* — for a man who has two wives — frames the inheritance law for a man with children from two women, one a permitted marriage and one a prohibited one. This raises the question of which combinations of women the verse can actually be addressing. If both wives are Egyptian converts, *ei shteihan mitzriyos shteihan senuos* — then both marriages are equally prohibited, and we no longer have the case of one permissible and one impermissible that the verse presupposes. On the other hand, if one wife is an Egyptian convert and one is an Israelite, then the two women do not come from the same *am*, and the inheritance law requires *shtei nashim me'am echad* — two wives from the same people — before the tensions it adjudicates even arise. This already places real pressure on how the verse is being read. Rabbi Akiva's view, that the verse is speaking exclusively about a *kohen gadol* who married a *be'ulah* alongside a permitted wife, runs into the further difficulty that if that were truly the *Torah*'s intent, the verse should have said *tiheyenah l'kohein* rather than *ki tiheyenah l'ish*. Because Rabbi Akiva's reading requires such a restricted case to be extracted from such a general formulation, the *gemara* concludes *baal karkhakh shavkah likra dehavi dakhik umuki anafshei* — Rabbi Akiva's *drasha* is effectively abandoned because it forces an unnaturally strained reading onto the verse. The Rabbanan's broader reading — that the verse speaks generally of any man with a permitted and a prohibited wife — is preferable precisely because it does not require the *Torah* to have left so specific and limited a case entirely implicit. This tension in Rabbi Akiva's position is at least part of why his view is set aside, and the broader principle that status follows the father is understood to apply across a wide range of cases, including the Egyptian generation count treated here.

### Non-Jewish Nations

Ravin, citing Rabbi Yochanan, introduces a principle regarding non-Jews: *be-umot halech achar ha-zachar* — among the nations, the tribal identity of a child follows the father. If a man from one non-Jewish nation has a child with a woman from another nation, the child's nationality is determined by the father. This too is an unlisted application of the rule that status follows the male, and the *gemara* includes it under *kol makom de-reisha le-atuyei*.

### Converted Nations

The same teaching of Ravin continues: *nitgayeru — halech achar ha-pagum she-be-shneihem* — if they converted, the child follows whichever parent bears the more disqualifying status. For instance, if an *Edomi* convert married a *Mitzri* convert — where the *Edomi* is restricted for two generations and the *Mitzri* for three — the child follows the more stringent status. This is a case of *yesh kiddushin, ein aveira* — two converts, permitted to marry one another — yet the child does not follow the father; it follows the *pagum*. This is an exception to the first rule, and the *gemara* includes it under the category of *eizehu* as an unlisted exception. One might note a further wrinkle here: depending on how one rules regarding the generation count for the *Mitzri*, the precise application of *pagum* in this case may shift — a question the *gemara* will flag explicitly in the consolidation that follows.

### Klal & Eizehu Purpose

The *gemara* now consolidates its analysis. According to Rabbi Yehuda — the likely author of the *mishnah* — the *ger u-mamzeret* case is an *aveira*, so it fits the second rule via the *kol makom* of the *seifa*. The *challal* and *Yisrael* cases are applications of the first rule also included in *kol makom*. The exceptions — the converted nations following the *pagum* despite no *aveira*, and possibly the *Mitzri* generation count depending on the ruling — are flagged by *eizehu*.

According to Rabbi Yosi, the *ger u-mamzeret* case is *muttar* and thus an exception to the first rule, included in *eizehu*. The converted nations' cases are likewise exceptions included there. But then the *kol makom* of the *seifa* — the general rule for when there is *kiddushin* and *aveira* — seems to have nothing left to include that is not already listed. The *gemara* resolves this by noting: *ayidi de-tana reisha eizo zu, tani seifa eizo zu* — since the *mishnah* uses *eizehu* in the first clause, it maintains the same structural parallel in the second clause, even if the second *eizehu* has no independent work to do in limiting or flagging exceptions there. Similarly, the *kol makom* of the *seifa* mirrors the *kol makom* of the *reisha* for structural reasons, even if — according to Rabbi Yosi — no additional cases need to be drawn in by the *seifa's kol makom*. The *mishnah's* symmetrical structure accounts for the repeated usage of both phrases.

### Non-Jewish Tribal Status

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכׇל מִי שֶׁאֵין לָהּ עָלָיו וְכוּ׳. שִׁפְחָה כְּנַעֲנִית מְנָלַן? אָמַר רַב הוּנָא: אָמַר קְרָא: ״שְׁבוּ לָכֶם פֹּה עִם הַחֲמוֹר״ – עַם הַדּוֹמֶה לַחֲמוֹר. אַשְׁכְּחַן דְּלָא תָּפְסִי בַּהּ קִדּוּשֵׁי,
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And** in **any** case **where** a woman **cannot** join in betrothal **with him** or with others, the offspring is like her. This ruling refers specifically to a Canaanite maidservant or a gentile woman. The Gemara asks: **From where do we** derive that betrothal with **a Canaanite maidservant** is ineffective? **Rav Huna says: The verse states** that Abraham commanded his slaves: **“You abide here with [*im*] the donkey”** (Genesis 22:5), which alludes to the fact that his slaves belong to **a nation [*am*] similar to a donkey;** just as betrothal is ineffective with animals, it is likewise ineffective with Canaanite maidservants. The Gemara comments: **We have found that betrothal is ineffective with** a Canaanite maidservant;

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלָדָהּ כְּמוֹתָהּ מְנָלַן? דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״הָאִשָּׁה וִילָדֶיהָ תִּהְיֶה לַאדֹנֶיהָ״.
> **Translation:** **from where do we** derive that **her offspring is like her?** The Gemara answers: **As the verse states** with regard to a Hebrew slave who marries a Canaanite maidservant: **“The wife and her children shall be her master’s”** (Exodus 21:4). This indicates that the offspring of a Canaanite maidservant and a Hebrew slave are slaves, as she is.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נׇכְרִית מְנָלַן? אָמַר קְרָא: ״לֹא תִתְחַתֵּן בָּם״. אַשְׁכַּחְנָא דְּלָא תָּפְסִי בַּהּ קִידּוּשֵׁי, וְלָדָהּ כְּמוֹתָהּ מְנָלַן?
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks: **From where do we** derive that betrothal with **a gentile woman** is ineffective? **The verse states: “Neither shall you make marriages with them”** (Deuteronomy 7:3), which teaches that marrying gentile women is halakhically meaningless. The Gemara asks: **We have found that betrothal is ineffective with her; from where do we** derive that **her offspring is like her?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן יוֹחַי: דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״כִּי יָסִיר אֶת בִּנְךָ מֵאַחֲרַי״ – בִּנְךָ הַבָּא מִיִּשְׂרְאֵלִית קָרוּי בִּנְךָ, וְאֵין בִּנְךָ הַבָּא מִן הַנׇּכְרִית קָרוּי בִּנְךָ, אֶלָּא בְּנָהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan says in the name of Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai: As the verse states** with regard to the same issue: “Your daughter you shall not give to his son…**for he will turn away your son from following Me”** (Deuteronomy 7:3–4). Since the verse is concerned that after one’s daughter marries a gentile, the father will lead his children away from the service of God, this indicates that **your son,** i.e., your grandson, **from a Jewish woman is called “your son”** by the Torah, **but your son from a gentile woman is not called your son, but her son.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבִינָא: שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ: בֶּן בִּתְּךָ הַבָּא מִן הַנׇּכְרִי – קָרוּי בִּנְךָ. נֵימָא קָסָבַר רָבִינָא נׇכְרִי וְעֶבֶד הַבָּא עַל בַּת יִשְׂרָאֵל – הַוָּלָד מַמְזֵר?
> **Translation:** **Ravina said: Learn from** it that **the son of your daughter, born to a gentile, is called your son** in all regards. The Gemara asks: **Shall we say** that **Ravina holds** that with regard to **a gentile or** a Canaanite **slave who engaged in sexual intercourse with a Jewish woman, the offspring is a *mamzer*?** One can infer from the fact that the offspring of this union is called “your son” that he is a Jew, and therefore the principle stated in the mishna should apply: If a woman cannot join in betrothal with someone, their child is a *mamzer*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נְהִי דְּכָשֵׁר לָא הָוֵי, מַמְזֵר לָא הָוֵי, פָּסוּל מִיקְּרֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this suggestion: **Although he is not a fit** offspring, **he is** also **not a *mamzer*.** Rather, **he is** merely **called disqualified.** Since betrothal is inapplicable to a gentile, a gentile is not included in the category of someone with whom a Jewish woman cannot personally join in betrothal, as no Jewish women can be betrothed to him. Nevertheless, as their child’s birth is the result of a transgression, he is considered disqualified.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָהוּא בְּשִׁבְעָה גּוֹיִם כְּתִיב, שְׁאָר אוּמּוֹת מְנָלַן? אָמַר קְרָא: ״כִּי יָסִיר אֶת בִּנְךָ״ – לְרַבּוֹת כׇּל הַמְּסִירִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks a question with regard to Rabbi Yoḥanan’s statement. **That** verse: “Neither shall you make marriages with them” (Deuteronomy 7:3), **is written with regard to the seven nations** of Canaan. **From where do we** derive that betrothal does not take effect with the **other nations?** The Gemara answers: **The verse states** as a reason for prohibiting intermarriages: **“For he will turn away your son** from following Me,” which serves **to include all those who might turn** a child **away,** no matter from which nation.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנִיחָא לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן, דְּדָרֵישׁ טַעְמָא דִּקְרָא. אֶלָּא לְרַבָּנַן, מַאי טַעְמָא?
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **This works out well according to** the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon, who expounds the reason** for the mitzvot of **the verse** and rules accordingly. Since the reason is that the gentile might turn away the son’s heart, there should be no distinction between the Canaanite nations and other gentiles. **But according to** the opinion of **the Rabbis,** who do not expound the reason for the mitzvot of the verse and rule accordingly, since the verse mentions only the Canaanite nations, **what is the reason,** the source for the prohibition, with regard to the other nations?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר קְרָא: ״וְאַחַר כֵּן תָּבוֹא אֵלֶיהָ וּבְעַלְתָּהּ וְגוֹ׳״ – מִכְּלָל דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא לָא תָּפְסִי בַּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **The verse states** with regard to a beautiful captive woman: **“And after that you may go in to her and be her husband,** and she shall be your wife” (Deuteronomy 21:13). One can derive from here **by inference that at the outset,** before she became a Jew, **betrothal would not take effect with her,** despite the fact that he had already brought her into his house, and according to some opinions, had even engaged in sexual intercourse with her.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַשְׁכְּחַן דְּלָא תָּפְסִי בַּהּ קִידּוּשִׁין, וְלָדָהּ כְּמוֹתָהּ מְנָלַן? אָמַר קְרָא: ״כִּי תִהְיֶיןָ לְאִישׁ... וְיָלְדוּ לוֹ״ – כֹּל הֵיכָא דְּקָרֵינַן בֵּיהּ ״כִּי תִּהְיֶינָה״, קָרֵינַן בֵּיהּ ״וְיָלְדוּ לוֹ״. וְכֹל הֵיכָא דְּלָא קָרֵינַן בֵּיהּ ״כִּי תִּהְיֶינָה״ – לָא קָרֵינַן בֵּיהּ ״וְיָלְדוּ לוֹ״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks another question: **We found** a source for the *halakha* that **betrothal is ineffective with her; from where do we** derive that **her child is like her?** The Gemara answers that **the verse states: “If a man has** two wives, the one beloved and the one hated, **and they have borne him** children” (Deuteronomy 21:15), from which it is derived: **Anywhere that we read: “If he has,”** i.e., that a woman can be betrothed, **we** also **read: “And they have borne him,”** meaning that their children follow his lineage. **And anywhere that we do not read: “If he has,” we** likewise **do not read: “And they have borne him,”** as the offspring inherit their mother’s status.

The *gemara* now examines the case of non-Jewish tribal identity in detail, returning to the teaching of Ravin. *Mai be-umot halech achar ha-zachar?* — What is the precise case where among non-Jews the child's tribal identity follows the father?

The *gemara* cites a *baraita*: *minayin le-echad min ha-umot she-ba al Kena'anit ve-holid ben she-ata rashai liknoto be-eved?* — From where do we know that if a man from the nations (not one of the seven Canaanite nations) fathered a child with a *Kena'anit* woman, that child may be purchased as a slave and is not subject to the prohibition of destruction that applies to the seven nations? The *talmud lomar*: *ve-gam mi-benei ha-toshavim ha-garim imachem, me-hem tiknu* — from those who dwell as sojourners in your land, you may purchase. The *baraita* understands the distinction between "sojourners" and "native" to be defined by the father. If the father is from outside *Kena'an*, the child is not a *Kena'ani*, even if the mother is.

Conversely: if a *Kena'ani* man fathered a child with a non-*Kena'anit* woman — *yachol af eved she-ba al shifcha min ha-umot ve-holid ben she-ata rashai liknoto be-eved?* — perhaps that child is also purchasable as a non-*Kena'ani*? The *talmud lomar*: *asher holidu be-artzechem* — those born in your land, meaning those born into a foreign land whose father identifies them as belonging elsewhere. *Min ha-noladim be-artzechem — aval lo min ha-garim be-artzechem* — you may purchase those born here among sojourners, but not those who genuinely dwell in the land as part of the *Kena'ani* nations. And who belongs to the *Kena'ani* nations? Those whose father is *Kena'ani* — the child follows the father.

The *gemara* acknowledges that what we have done here is read the assumption of paternal identity into the *pesukim* and then read it back out again as a proof — the *pesukim* speak of "born in your land" versus "sojourning in your land" without explicitly mentioning the father, and the determination of which category a child belongs to is being derived from the principle that follows the father. Nonetheless, this is the *tanna'im's* reading, and it establishes that for non-Jewish national identity, status is defined by the father.

It is worth pausing to note what is implicit but foundational in this entire discussion: the reason an *eved Ivri* — a Hebrew slave — who marries a *shifcha Kenaanit* produces children who remain slaves and stay with the master is that the verse states *ha'ishah v'yeladeha tiyeh l'adoneha* — the woman and her children shall belong to her master. The children follow the mother's status precisely because there is no *kiddushin* binding the father to the mother, and so the children cannot be attached to the father's identity. The same logic runs through the *gemara's* treatment of a *nochrit*: *lo tafsei bah kiddushin* — *kiddushin* does not take effect with a non-Jewish woman — and therefore *valdah kemotah*, her child follows her status. The principle that the child follows the mother wherever *kiddushin* is absent is thus not a separate rule but the direct consequence of the absence of any valid marital bond between the parents. For non-Jewish national identity, by contrast, there is no question of *kiddushin* at all — the entire framework is different — and so the default rule, as the *baraita* derives it, runs in the opposite direction: the child follows the father.

The discussion of what happens when such individuals convert — for instance, an *Edomi* convert and a *Moavi* convert, or an *Ammoni* convert, each of whom carries different *halachic* consequences regarding entry into the Jewish community — will continue in the next *shiur*.
