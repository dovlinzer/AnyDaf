# Sukkah 2 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Masechet Overview

### Five Chapters Intro

We begin a new *masechta* today: *Sukkah*. *Sukkah* consists of five chapters and is, for the most part, logically organized. One might expect it to open with a discussion of the *mitzvah* of sitting in a *sukkah*, but in fact it begins with the structural requirements of the *sukkah* itself, devoting the first two chapters to that subject. Toward the end of the second chapter it turns to the *mitzvah* of dwelling in a *sukkah*, and then the third chapter is devoted to *lulav* and *esrog*, with the fourth and fifth chapters taking up *Hallel* and related topics. The first three chapters are certainly the primary ones in terms of both the objects of the *mitzvah* and the *mitzvos* themselves that apply on *Sukkos*.

### Rishonim & Sources

In contrast to *Yoma*, where much of the material is no longer practically relevant, *Sukkah* is almost entirely applicable today, and as a result it attracts considerably more *Rishonim*. Beyond *Rashi* and *Tosafot*, there is a *Ritva* on *Sukkah*, as well as extensive discussions by the *Rif*, the *Ramban*, and the *Baal HaMaor*. And that is just on the first two chapters. The third chapter — on *lulav* and *esrog* — inspired entire monographs: the *Ra'avad* composed a *Hilchos Lulav*, and the *Ramban* wrote a *Hilchos Lulav v'Esrog* devoted to the laws of the four species. So there is substantially more *Rishonim* on this *masechta* than on *Yoma*, as one might expect given its ongoing practical relevance.

### Three Criteria

Let us turn now to *daf* 2a. The opening *mishna* reads:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סוּכָּה שֶׁהִיא גְּבוֹהָה לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה — פְּסוּלָה. וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה מַכְשִׁיר. וְשֶׁאֵינָהּ גְּבוֹהָה עֲשָׂרָה טְפָחִים, וְשֶׁאֵין לָהּ (שְׁלֹשָׁה) דְּפָנוֹת, וְשֶׁחֲמָתָהּ מְרוּבָּה מְצִלָּתָהּ — פְּסוּלָה.
>
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **A *sukka*,** i.e., its roofing, which is the main and most crucial element of the mitzva, **that is more than twenty cubits high is unfit. Rabbi Yehuda deems it fit.** Similarly, a *sukka* **that is not** even **ten handbreadths high, and** one **that does not have three walls, and** one **whose sunlight** that passes through its roofing **is greater than its shade are unfit.**

So we have three primary criteria. The first concerns height: the *sukkah* must be at least ten handbreadths tall but no more than twenty *amos* high. The second is structural: it requires a minimum of three walls. The third is functional: it must provide shade.

Conspicuously absent from this *mishna* are the horizontal dimensions — there is no mention of minimum area or of minimum length and width. Also absent is any discussion of what materials are valid for walls, or for the *sechach* itself. That absence is itself worth noting.

One point worth thinking about regarding the third criterion: when the *mishna* says that the *sukkah* is invalid if the sun is greater than the shade — is this a statement about the *sechach* itself, about its quality and density? Or is it a statement about the objective reality of being in the *sukkah* at a given moment, regardless of the *sechach*? One can imagine a *sukkah* with perfectly kosher *sechach* where, due to the angle of the sun at a particular time of day, more sunlight enters than shade is produced. Is it the quality of the *sechach* that is being assessed, or the actual state of affairs inside the *sukkah* at any given moment? These are questions the *masechta* will explore.

### Psul vs. Takanta

The *Gemara* now raises a comparative question. It notes:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ תְּנַן הָתָם: מָבוֹי שֶׁהוּא גָּבוֹהַּ מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה — יְמַעֵט. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: אֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ. מַאי שְׁנָא גַּבֵּי סוּכָּה דְּתָנֵי פְּסוּלָה, וּמַאי שְׁנָא גַּבֵּי מָבוֹי דְּתָנֵי תַּקַּנְתָּא?
>
> **Translation:** GEMARA: **We learned** a similar *halakha* in a mishna **there,** in tractate *Eiruvin* (2a): In the case of **an alleyway that is higher than twenty cubits,** i.e., the beam that was placed across the end of an alleyway that opens into a public domain in order to permit carrying within the alleyway on Shabbat is higher than twenty cubits, **one** must **diminish** the height of the beam in order to permit carrying within the alleyway. **Rabbi Yehuda says he need not** do so, and although the beam lies higher than twenty cubits, the alleyway is qualified to permit carrying within. Given the seeming similarity between the two cases, that of the *sukka* and that of the alleyway, the Gemara asks: **What is different with regard to a *sukka* where** the mishna **teaches** that it is **unfit, and what is different with regard to an alleyway where** the mishna **teaches** the method of **rectification,** that one must diminish the height of the cross beam? Why was a solution not suggested in the case of a *sukka*?

A brief clarification of what a *mavoi* is: it is a dead-end alleyway into which multiple courtyards open. The courtyards themselves contain houses. The *mavoi* has three walls but is open on one side toward the public domain. In order to permit carrying within the *mavoi*, it requires both a communal sharing arrangement (*shituf mevo'os*) and a structural marker at its open end — either a vertical post (*lechi*) or a horizontal beam (*korah*). This beam must be within twenty *amos* of the ground; if it is higher, it must be lowered.

The *Gemara* observes the striking parallel: both *Sukkah* and *Eruvin* open with essentially the same *mishna* and essentially the same debate — a twenty-*amos* limit that Rabbi Yehuda contests in both cases. The question the *Gemara* then presses is: why not say "invalid" in both cases, or "fix it" in both?

### Rashi: Reality Layer

The *Gemara* answers:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סוּכָּה דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא, תָּנֵי פְּסוּלָה. מָבוֹי דְּרַבָּנַן, תָּנֵי תַּקַּנְתָּא.
>
> **Translation:** With regard to ***sukka*,** since it is a mitzva **by Torah law,** the mishna **teaches** that it is **unfit,** as, if it is not constructed in the proper manner, no mitzva is fulfilled. However, with regard to **an alleyway,** where the entire prohibition of carrying is only **by rabbinic law,** the mishna **teaches** the method of **rectification,** as the cross beam comes only to rectify a rabbinic prohibition but does not involve a mitzva by Torah law.

*Rashi* offers a profound explanation. He writes: the twenty-*amos* measure is a tradition received from Sinai as a matter of biblical law. The *mishna*, therefore, is not legislating; it is reporting an objective fact. The *sukkah* that exceeds twenty *amos* is, from the perspective of the *Torah*, simply and objectively invalid. The *mishna* uses the language *pesula* because it is describing an objective metaphysical reality: in the *Torah*'s framework, that structure is not a *sukkah*.

The *mavoi*, by contrast, is a rabbinic creation from beginning to end. Biblically, three walls are sufficient for a domain, and no *korah* is required at all. The rabbis decided to require it, and the rabbis decided it must be within twenty *amos*. When the rabbis are themselves the legislators, they cannot say "invalid" in the same categorical way, because there is no pre-existing objective reality they are reporting. They are the ones who instituted the requirement, and what they can say is: "We want it fixed." As *Rashi* puts it, who invalidated it before this *mishna* was taught? It was not invalid before the rabbis said so. The *mishna* on the *mavoi* is the very inauguration of that requirement. So it says: lower it. It cannot declare something invalid when it is the first word on the matter.

This is a striking idea. It evokes a kind of *halakhic* realism — a platonic structure in which the *Torah*'s laws correspond to objective *halakhic* realities that exist independently. The *mishna* on *sukkah* is not creating the invalidation; it is discovering and reporting it. The *mishna* on *mavoi*, by contrast, is the creative act itself. It would be odd to call a thing invalid at the very moment one is first calling it into existence.

One might ask whether this distinction carries through consistently — does the *Gemara* always use the language of invalidity for biblical laws and the language of correction for rabbinic ones? The honest answer is no. Neither *Rashi*'s explanation nor *Tosafot*'s is universally applied across the *Talmud*. This is the explanation for this specific context.

### Tosafot: Brevity

*Tosafot* offers two quite different explanations for the same phenomenon.

The first is pragmatic. When a *mishna* says "invalid," there is no ambiguity — it is invalid in all circumstances, even after the fact (*bedi'eved*). One cannot rationalize: "Well, perhaps in practice it is acceptable." But if the *mishna* says "lower it" — *yima'et*, fix it — one might reason: "The *mishna* told me to fix it, but since I did not get around to it, perhaps it is still acceptable after the fact." That potential for misinterpretation is a real risk. When the law is biblical, the *mishna* will not risk that misreading; it uses the unambiguous language of invalidity. When the law is rabbinic, the *mishna* can afford to risk the misinterpretation, because the practical guidance offered by "lower it" — telling the person how to address the situation — is worth something.

The second explanation is stylistic conciseness. The *Gemara* states: the *sukkah* *mishna* has many parameters to enumerate. It would have to say: if it is too high, lower it; if it is too low, raise it; if the *sechach* is insufficient, add more; if there are too few walls, add more walls. The whole list. Rather than spell out remedies for each problem, the *mishna* simply says *pesula* — invalid — covering everything in one word. The *mavoi* case in this *mishna* addresses only one scenario (height). There it is possible to say concisely: lower it. This follows the general principle articulated at the opening *sugya* of *Pesachim* that one should always teach in brief.

There is also an interesting structural observation worth making here. Many *masechtos* open with what, based on a letter of Rav Sherira Gaon, scholars identify as *Savoraic* material — that is, text added in the period immediately after the *Gemara*'s final redaction, though the point applies to the opening of a *masechta* more broadly. Such openings characteristically contain no named statements by *Tannaim* or *Amoraim*; they are *stam*, anonymous. They tend to focus on comparative analysis of *mishnayos*, playing with parallels and linguistic distinctions, rather than advancing new *halakhic* rulings. The opening of *Kiddushin*, for example, spends considerable time on why the *mishna* uses the passive form *nikneit* rather than the language of *kiddushin*, and why it speaks from the woman's perspective rather than the man's. Such openings often serve a pedagogical function: easing the student in while also introducing themes that will recur throughout the *masechta*. Here, the parallel between *sukkah* and *mavoi* — both with the same twenty-*amos* limit, both with Rabbi Yehuda disagreeing — is not merely a curiosity. It raises a question that will resonate throughout the *masechta*: is there a single shared source underlying both requirements and both debates?

## 20-Amah Sources

### Rava: Awareness

where do these laws come from? The *Gemara* now turns to the biblical and conceptual foundations of the twenty-*amos* requirement.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבָּה, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: "לְמַעַן יֵדְעוּ דוֹרוֹתֵיכֶם כִּי בַסּוּכּוֹת הוֹשַׁבְתִּי אֶת בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל", עַד עֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה, אָדָם יוֹדֵעַ שֶׁהוּא דָּר בַּסּוּכָּה, לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה — אֵין אָדָם יוֹדֵעַ שֶׁדָּר בַּסּוּכָּה, מִשּׁוּם דְּלָא שָׁלְטָא בַּהּ עֵינָא.
>
> **Translation:** **Rabba said** that it is derived **as the verse states: "So that your future generations will know that I caused the children of Israel to reside in *sukkot*** when I took them out of the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 23:43). In a *sukka* **up to twenty cubits** high, even without a concerted effort, **a person is aware that he is residing in a *sukka*.** His eye catches sight of the roofing, evoking the *sukka* and its associated mitzvot. However, in a *sukka* that is **more than twenty cubits high, a person is not aware that he is residing in a *sukka* because his eye does not** involuntarily **catch sight** of the roof, as at that height, without a concerted effort one would not notice the roofing.

This requires a brief explanation of a key principle that will govern the entire *masechta*: for *Chazal*, the *ikar* of the *sukkah* is the *sechach*. The walls are necessary, but the defining element of the *sukkah* — the element for which it is named — is the *sechach*. *Rashi* states this explicitly in his comment just before the *Gemara* begins: the *sukkah* is named for the *sechach*. This means that any verse referring to *sukkah* will be understood as referring primarily to the *sechach*, not the walls.

So when Rava says the eye does not see it — what the eye fails to see is the *sechach*. The walls may be perfectly visible; they dominate the visual field. But if the *sukkah* is more than twenty *amos* tall and the *sechach* is at the top, a person standing inside will see only walls. The *sechach* is too far above to enter naturally into one's awareness. And since the verse commands that future generations should know they are dwelling in *sukkos*, Rava reads this as a functional requirement: the *sechach* must be within the range of natural perception. If one must crane one's neck to see it, the awareness the verse demands is absent.

One might note that the plain sense (*pshat*) of the verse is historical: it is an educational imperative to remember what God did for Israel in the wilderness. Rava could be understood as saying that this historical awareness cannot be accessed unless one is presently conscious of sitting under *sechach*. But *Rashi* and the subsequent *Gemara* appear to read Rava as deriving an actual structural requirement directly from the verse — that the *sechach* must be perceptible — not merely as a means to an end.

It is worth noting here as well the connection to a broader theme. The idea that the *sukkah* commemorates the clouds of glory (*ananei kavod*) that accompanied Israel in the wilderness is evocative in this context — one naturally imagines those clouds as being quite high up. We will return to the *ananei kavod* theme later; for now, Rava's derivation focuses squarely on the perceptibility of the *sechach*.

### Rav Zeira: Shade

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי זֵירָא אָמַר מֵהָכָא: "וְסוּכָּה תִּהְיֶה לְצֵל יוֹמָם מֵחוֹרֶב", עַד עֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה אָדָם יוֹשֵׁב בְּצֵל סוּכָּה, לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה — אֵין אָדָם יוֹשֵׁב בְּצֵל סוּכָּה אֶלָּא בְּצֵל דְּפָנוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira said** that it is derived **from here:** The verse states: **"And there shall be a *sukka* for shade in the daytime from the heat,** and for refuge and cover from storm and from rain" (Isaiah 4:6). In a *sukka* **up to twenty cubits** high, **a person is sitting in the shade of the *sukka*,** i.e., the shade of the roofing; in a *sukka* that is **more than twenty cubits high, a person is not sitting in the shade of** the roofing of **the *sukka* but rather in the shade of the walls** of the *sukka*, as their considerable height provides constant shade, rendering the shade of the roofing irrelevant.

The full verse in its context is a Messianic vision: in the age of redemption, God will create over all of Mount Zion a cloud by day and a fiery glow by night — evoking the imagery of the exodus — and a protective covering, a *sukkah*, will provide shade from the heat and shelter from the rain.

It is worth pausing over the context, because the verse is not referring to the *mitzvah* of *sukkah* in any direct sense. It is part of an eschatological portrait in *Yeshayahu*, where the prophet foresees that the glory of Mount Zion will be covered, and then: "and a *sukkah* shall be for shade in the day from the heat, and for a canopy, and for shelter and a refuge from storm and rain."

Even setting aside the Messianic context, this verse is already doing something powerful: it connects the *sukkah* of the *mitzvah* to a Messianic vision of divine protection, and it evokes the *anan* — the cloud that accompanied Israel through the wilderness. For those who know their *Tanach* as they should, encountering this verse at the outset of *Sukkah* introduces quietly but significantly the theme of *sukkah ke-neged ananei kavod* — the *sukkah* as representing the clouds of glory. The verse from *Yeshayahu* thus serves double duty in the *Gemara*: it supports a legal derivation, and it sounds a thematic note that will recur.

Now for the legal application. Rav Zeira derives from this verse: up to twenty *amos*, a person is sitting in the shade of the *sukkah* — meaning, the *sechach* is actually providing shade. Above twenty *amos*, the person is not sitting in the shade of the *sukkah* (the *sechach*), but rather in the shade of the walls. The walls, being so tall, are what shade the space below, while the *sechach* — small and high above — contributes nothing effective. The *sukkah* verse in *Yeshayahu* uses the word *tzel* — shade — and the legal requirement is that the *sechach* must actually function as the source of shade.

### Abaye: Mountains

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי: אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה, הָעוֹשֶׂה סוּכָּתוֹ בְּעַשְׁתְּרוֹת קַרְנַיִם, הָכִי נָמֵי דְּלָא הָוֵי סוּכָּה?
>
> **Translation:** **Abaye said to him: But if** it is **so** that one is required to sit in the shade of the roofing of the *sukka*, then in the case of **one who makes his *sukka* in Ashterot Karnayim,** which is located between two mountains that prevent sunlight from reaching there, **so too,** it **is not a** fit ***sukka*,** since he is not sitting in the shade of the roofing.

### Zeira: Schach Quality

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: הָתָם, דַּל עַשְׁתְּרוֹת קַרְנַיִם — אִיכָּא צֵל סוּכָּה. הָכָא, דַּל דְּפָנוֹת — לֵיכָּא צֵל סוּכָּה.
>
> **Translation:** Rabbi Zeira **said to him:** The two cases are not comparable; **there,** if one theoretically **removes** the **Ashterot Karnayim** mountains that obstruct the sunlight, **there is** still the **shade of the** roofing of the ***sukka*.** In that case, the *sukka* is properly constructed and there are only external factors that affect the sunlight. However, **here,** in the case of a *sukka* that is more than twenty cubits high, if one theoretically **removes the walls** of the *sukka*, **there is no shade** provided **by the** roofing of the ***sukka*,** since throughout the day sunlight will enter the *sukka* beneath the roofing from where the walls used to be.

This is a crucial distinction. The argument is not simply that the walls happen to be providing more shade than the *sechach* in practice. The argument is that when the *sukkah* exceeds twenty *amos*, the *sechach* is so high that it is incapable of providing shade regardless of the walls — even in the abstract, even if you were to look at the *sechach* in isolation. When sunlight strikes at any angle other than directly overhead (and we can largely discount the moment of absolute zenith), a small patch of *sechach* situated twenty or more *amos* above the ground will cast its shade well outside the interior of the *sukkah*. The shade produced by the *sechach* falls elsewhere. Remove the walls, and you have no shade underneath — the shade is somewhere else entirely. In the mountain case, the *sechach* is perfectly capable of providing shade; it is just being supplemented by the mountains. In the too-tall *sukkah*, the *sechach* is not capable of providing shade in its given position, full stop.

One might ask whether this should depend on the area of the *sechach*: if the *sechach* is very wide, surely it can shade the space beneath it even at a great height? The *Gemara* will address this shortly.

### Rava: Temp Structure

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרָבָא אָמַר, מֵהָכָא: "בַּסּוּכּוֹת תֵּשְׁבוּ שִׁבְעַת יָמִים". אָמְרָה תּוֹרָה: כׇּל שִׁבְעַת הַיָּמִים צֵא מִדִּירַת קֶבַע וְשֵׁב בְּדִירַת עֲרַאי. עַד עֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה אָדָם עוֹשֶׂה דִּירָתוֹ דִּירַת עֲרַאי, לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה — אֵין אָדָם עוֹשֶׂה דִּירָתוֹ דִּירַת עֲרַאי אֶלָּא דִּירַת קֶבַע.
>
> **Translation:** **Rava said** that the *halakha* is derived **from here: "In *sukkot* shall you reside seven days"** (Leviticus 23:42). **The Torah said:** For **the entire seven days, emerge from the permanent residence** in which you reside year round **and reside in a temporary residence,** the *sukka*. In constructing a *sukka* **up to twenty cubits** high, **a person** can **render his residence a temporary residence,** as up to that height one can construct a structure that is not sturdy; however, in constructing a *sukka* **above twenty cubits high, one cannot render his residence a temporary residence; rather,** he must construct a sturdy **permanent residence,** which is unfit for use as a *sukka*.

### Abaye: Materials

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי: אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה, עָשָׂה מְחִיצוֹת שֶׁל בַּרְזֶל וְסִיכֵּךְ עַל גַּבָּן, הָכִי נָמֵי דְּלָא הָוֵי סוּכָּה?
>
> **Translation:** **Abaye said to him: But if** that is **so,** then if **he constructed** a *sukka* with **steel partitions and placed roofing over them, so too,** there, say that **it would not be a** fit ***sukka*,** as any *sukka* constructed as a permanent residence would be unfit. However, there is no opinion that deems a *sukka* of that sort unfit.

### Rava: Dimensions

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ, הָכִי קָאָמֵינָא לָךְ: עַד עֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה דְּאָדָם עוֹשֶׂה דִּירָתוֹ דִּירַת עֲרַאי, כִּי עָבֵיד לֵיהּ דִּירַת קֶבַע, נָמֵי נָפֵיק. לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה, דְּאָדָם עוֹשֶׂה דִּירָתוֹ דִּירַת קֶבַע, כִּי עָבֵיד לֵיהּ דִּירַת עֲרַאי, נָמֵי לָא נָפֵיק.
>
> **Translation:** Rava **said to him** in response that **this is what I am saying to you:** In a case where one constructs a *sukka* **up to twenty cubits** high, a height **that a person** typically constructs **a temporary residence, when he constructs** a structure of that height that is sturdy like **a permanent residence, he also fulfills** his obligation. However, in a case where one constructs a *sukka* **more than twenty cubits high,** a height **that a person** typically constructs **a permanent residence,** even **when he constructs it** in a less sturdy fashion **like a temporary residence, he does not fulfill his obligation.**

The resolution is elegant: the *halacha* of temporary versus permanent, like most *halacha*, is operationalized through quantifiable criteria — specifically, dimensions — rather than building materials. What distinguishes a *dirat arai* from a *dirat keva* in the law's framework is the scale of the structure, not what it is made of. A structure under twenty *amos* can function as a *dirat arai* in terms of its proportions, whatever material is used. A structure above twenty *amos* takes on the character of a permanent building by its very dimensions alone.

## Tosafot: Permanence

### Tosafot: Nails

This leads to a natural question that *Tosafot* raises. We have said that the wall material is irrelevant — even brick walls are acceptable. But what about the *sechach* itself? Can the *sechach* be made permanent? Specifically, *Tosafot* asks: could one not take planks of wood (which are valid *sechach* if the wood is from something that grew from the ground and is no longer attached) and nail them down permanently, so that no rain would enter? Would that be biblically valid?

### Tosafot: Non-Permanent

*Tosafot* answers: using your actual house is clearly invalid, as the *Torah* says *sukkah* and not a house that is established all year round. But what about building a new structure with a permanent wood roof? *Tosafot* writes: granted, we are not concerned if the *walls* are made permanent, because as we have established, wall material is not what defines the *sukkah*. But the *sechach*, which is the defining element of the *sukkah* (and from which the *sukkah* takes its name), the *sukkah* is not valid unless the *sechach* is made temporary.

*Tosafot* does not spell out a precise quantifiable threshold for what makes *sechach* "permanent" in this sense. The question of when nailed planks cross the line from permissible *sechach* to an invalid permanent roof is one that cannot be fully quantified. But the principle is that at some point, if the roof is constructed in a manner that makes it genuinely permanent, it ceases to be *sechach* and becomes a house. The *sukkah* is no longer a *sukkah*; it is simply a building.

### Rain Sign

One of *Tosafot*'s criteria for identifying *sechach* that has crossed the line into permanent structure is rain impermeability. The verse from *Yeshayahu* that Rav Zeira cited includes, at its end, the phrase "and for shelter and refuge from storm and rain." One might have thought that this describes a virtue of the *sukkah* — it keeps out the rain. But *Tosafot* argues the opposite: the fact that a Messianic *sukkah* is described as providing shelter from rain is precisely because in the present context, a *sukkah* that is impervious to rain is not a valid *sukkah* at all. If the *sechach* blocks out rain entirely, it is a sign of a permanent roof, a *dirat keva*, not a *dirat arai*. This is why the rain that falls on *Sukkos* is called a *siman kelalah ba-chag* — a sign of a curse on the festival. If it were possible to build a *sukkah* that keeps rain out entirely, there would be no curse in rain on the festival. The very fact that rain is an unwelcome visitor demonstrates that a valid *sukkah* must be open to rain.

This is a structurally interesting point: *Tosafot* uses the theme of the *sukkah*'s vulnerability to rain not merely as a poetic observation about the nature of the *mitzvah*, but as a legal criterion. A *sukkah* that keeps rain out entirely has, by that very quality, identified itself as a permanent structure, and permanent *sechach* is invalid. The emphasis on the *sechach* — *ikar ha-sukkah al shem ha-sechach* — means that the *sechach* cannot be permanent even while the walls can.

## Debate Limits

### Yoshiya: Wall Gap

Having established the three distinct rationales for the twenty-*amos* limit — Rava's awareness/visibility argument, Rav Zeira's shade argument, and Rava's *dirat arai* argument — the *Gemara* now examines how each model generates different qualifications of the debate.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן אָזְלָא הָא דְּאָמַר רַבִּי יֹאשִׁיָּה אָמַר רַב: מַחְלוֹקֶת בְּשֶׁאֵין דְּפָנוֹת מַגִּיעוֹת לַסְּכָךְ, אֲבָל דְּפָנוֹת מַגִּיעוֹת לַסְּכָךְ — אֲפִילּוּ לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה כְּשֵׁרָה.
>
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks: **In accordance with whose** opinion **is that which Rabbi Yoshiya said** that **Rav said:** The **dispute** between Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis with regard to the fitness of a *sukka* more than twenty cubits high is specifically **in** a case **where the walls** of the *sukka* **do not reach** up to **the roofing; however,** in a case where **the walls** of the *sukka* **reach** up to **the roofing,** the Rabbis concede that **even** if the roofing is **more than twenty cubits high, it is fit.**

### Eye Following

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן? כְּרַבָּה, דְּאָמַר מִשּׁוּם דְּלָא שָׁלְטָא בַּהּ עֵינָא, וְכֵיוָן דִּדְפָנוֹת מַגִּיעוֹת לַסְּכָךְ, מִשְׁלַט שָׁלְטָא בַּהּ עֵינָא.
>
> **Translation:** It is **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabba, who says** that the reason that a *sukka* that high is unfit is **because the eye does not** automatically **catch sight of** the roofing. **And since the walls** of the *sukka* **reach the roofing, the eye catches sight** of the roofing, as the person will follow the walls all the way up to the roofing despite their considerable height. However, if the roofing is not contiguous with the top of the walls, a person does not notice it without a concerted effort.

This qualification makes no sense on the other two theories. Whether the walls reach the *sechach* or not is irrelevant to whether the *sechach* provides shade in practice, and irrelevant to whether the dimensions suggest a permanent or temporary structure.

### Huna: Area

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן אָזְלָא הָא דְּאָמַר רַב הוּנָא אָמַר רַב: מַחְלוֹקֶת בְּשֶׁאֵין בָּהּ אֶלָּא אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת עַל אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת, אֲבָל יֵשׁ בָּהּ יוֹתֵר מֵאַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת עַל אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת — אֲפִילּוּ לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה כְּשֵׁרָה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **In accordance with whose** opinion **is that which Rav Huna said** that **Rav said:** The **dispute** between Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis with regard to the fitness of a *sukka* more than twenty cubits high is specifically **in** a case **where there is not** an area of **four cubits by four cubits in** the *sukka*; **however,** in a case where **there is** an area of **more than four cubits by four cubits in** the *sukka*, the Rabbis concede that **even** if the roofing is **more than twenty cubits high, it is fit.**

### Shade Proportional

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן? כְּרַבִּי זֵירָא, דְּאָמַר: מִשּׁוּם צֵל הוּא, וְכֵיוָן דִּרְוִיחָא — אִיכָּא צֵל סוּכָּה.
>
> **Translation:** It is **in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Zeira, who says that** a *sukka* that high is unfit **due to** the **shade** that is provided by the walls and not by the roofing; **and since** the *sukka* in this case **is spacious** and has a large area, **there is shade from the** roofing of the ***sukka*** and not only from the walls.

### Proportionality

One might ask: should this not be fully proportional? If a *sechach* four by four *amos* wide can be at twenty *amos* height, should a *sechach* twenty by twenty *amos* be permitted at a proportionally greater height? Should there be a sliding scale?

*Tosafot* raises this point and offers two responses. The first is that perhaps it is indeed proportional — as the *sechach* grows wider, greater heights become permissible. *Tosafot* does not rule this out entirely.

But the more characteristic rabbinic answer, which *Tosafot* develops by reference back to *Yoma*, is this: it would always be proportional in principle. But the *Gemara* also says: the Rabbis knew that even at a height of a thousand *amos*, once the *sechach* is more than four by four *amos*, it is impossible that no shade at all would enter. And once it is less than four by four, even at twenty *amos* it will fail.

*Tosafot* supports this with a reference to *Yom Kippur*: just as in that context the Rabbis taught that once you have the right minimum quantity — a pressed date — it has an effect even for *Og King of Bashan* who ate it, and a little less than that has no effect for anyone — so too here. Once you cross the threshold of four by four *amos*, there will always be some shade regardless of height. Below that threshold, height above twenty *amos* will always be a problem. The Rabbis knew this and set a fixed number rather than a proportional formula.

### Chana: Minimum

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן אָזְלָא הָא דְּאָמַר רַב חָנָן בַּר רַבָּה אָמַר רַב: מַחְלוֹקֶת בְּשֶׁאֵינָהּ מַחְזֶקֶת אֶלָּא כְּדֵי רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ, אֲבָל מַחְזֶקֶת יוֹתֵר מִכְּדֵי רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ — אֲפִילּוּ לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה כְּשֵׁרָה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **In accordance with whose** opinion **is that which Rav Ḥanan bar Rabba said** that **Rav said:** He says the debate applies only when the *sukkah* **only** large **enough** to **hold one's head, most of his** body, **and his table,** as, if the *sukkah* were smaller, it would not qualify as a *sukkah*; **however,** in a case where **it** is sufficiently large to **hold more than one's head, most of his** body, **and his table, even if it is more than twenty cubits high, it is fit.**

A word of clarification: why would one's "head and most of one's body" not simply mean a person sitting up? The answer lies in the *halacha* that eating in the *sukkah* is ideally done in a reclining position. So the minimum *sukkah* must accommodate one's head and most of one's body while reclining, plus a small tray table. The *Gemara* later establishes this minimum as approximately seven by seven handbreadths (*shiva al shiva tefachim*) — roughly two feet by two feet. This is far smaller than four *amos* by four *amos* (roughly six feet by six feet), which is about sixteen times the area.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן — דְּלָא כְּחַד.
>
> **Translation:** **In accordance with whose** opinion is it? It is **not in accordance with** the opinion **of** any **one** of them. This statement cannot be explained according to any of the rationales for the fact that a *sukka* more than twenty cubits high is unfit.

### Chicken Coop

*Tosafot* proposes an independent logic for this position. *Tosafot* suggests: a chicken coop. If the *sukkah* is at the absolute minimum size — just barely accommodating one's head, most of one's body, and a table — and it is also extremely tall, the result is an absurd structure that no one would ever recognize as a dwelling of any kind. It is too narrow and too tall. Once it is even slightly larger than the minimum, however, it escapes the category of a structural absurdity and can be recognized as some kind of dwelling, even if a small one. The problem of exceeding twenty *amos* applies only when the structure, combined with its tiny footprint, cannot even pass as a dwelling. Once it is a bit larger, the problem disappears.

### Gemara: Two Models

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא לְדֵיקֶא שֶׁל דְּרַבִּי יֹאשִׁיָּה, פְּלִיגִי דְּרַב הוּנָא וְרַב חָנָן בַּר רַבָּה, דְּאִינְהוּ קָא יָהֲבִי שִׁעוּרָא בִּמְשָׁכָא, וְאִיהוּ לָא קָא יָהֵיב שִׁעוּרָא בִּמְשָׁכָא. אֶלָּא רַב הוּנָא וְרַב חָנָן בַּר רַבָּה, נֵימָא בְּהֶכְשֵׁר סוּכָּה קָמִיפַּלְגִי, דְּמָר סָבַר הֶכְשֵׁר סוּכָּה בְּאַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת, וּמָר סָבַר הֶכְשֵׁר סוּכָּה בְּמַחְזֶקֶת רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ?
>
> **Translation:** **Granted, according to the one,** Rabbi Yoshiya, **who said that** it is specifically **in** a case **where the walls** of the *sukka* **do not reach** up to **the roofing** that there is a **dispute** between Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis, the *baraita* can be explained as dealing with a *sukka* of that type, as **it is customary for a queen to reside in a *sukka* in which the walls do not reach** up to **the roofing,** [continuing from next section]. it makes sense that Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba's qualifications disagree with each other: Rebbi Yoshiyah's qualification (whether the walls reach the *sechach*) is about a different dimension of the structure entirely — it speaks to the vertical relationship between wall and *sechach* — while Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba both speak to the horizontal dimensions of the *sukkah*. Those two can coexist as independent debates. But the *Gemara* then asks: perhaps Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba are not debating the reason for the twenty-*amos* requirement at all. Perhaps they both agree on the reason, and what they are really debating is simply: what is the minimum valid size of a *sukkah*? One holds the minimum is four by four *amos*. The other holds it is only *rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano*. They both agree that the twenty-*amos* debate applies only to a minimum-size *sukkah*, and they are simply disagreeing about what minimum size is.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא, דְּכוּלֵּי עָלְמָא הֶכְשֵׁר סוּכָּה בְּמַחְזֶקֶת רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ. וְהָכָא בְּהָא קָמִיפַּלְגִי: דְּמָר סָבַר בְּמַחְזֶקֶת רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ מַחְלוֹקֶת, אֲבָל יוֹתֵר מִרֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ — דִּבְרֵי הַכֹּל כְּשֵׁרָה, וּמָר סָבַר: מִרֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ עַד אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת מַחְלוֹקֶת, אֲבָל יוֹתֵר מֵאַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת — דִּבְרֵי הַכֹּל כְּשֵׁרָה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this: everyone agrees the minimum *sukkah* is *rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano*, *shiva tefachim*. The debate between Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba is not about the minimum size of a *sukkah*; it is about when height above twenty *amos* becomes a problem. According to one, the debate about height applies to any size from the bare minimum (*shiva al shiva tefachim*) upward, but once it is larger, everyone agrees it is valid. The other holds: yes, I agree that once it is *rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano* it qualifies as a *sukkah*, but the debate does not end there. Anywhere from the minimum up until four by four *amos*, there is a debate; once it is more than four *amos*, everyone agrees it is valid.

The *Gemara* thus insists on its framing: the various qualifications proposed by Rebbi Yoshiyah, Rav Huna, and Rav Chana bar Rabba each track one of the underlying rationales for the twenty-*amos* limit. Rebbi Yoshiyah's qualification (wall-meets-*sechach*) follows Rava's awareness model. Rav Huna's qualification (four *amos* of area) follows Rav Zeira's shade model, since a larger *sechach* provides shade even at great height. And the *sugya* continues to press toward identifying which of these models best accounts for the full range of qualifications the *Amora'im* propose.

## Hilni Precedent

### Hilni's High Sukkah

The *Gemara* now introduces a *baraita* that presents a historical case, and Rabbi Yehuda uses it as a proof for his position:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵיתִיבִי: סוּכָּה שֶׁהִיא גְּבוֹהָה לְמַעְלָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה — פְּסוּלָה, וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה מַכְשִׁיר עַד אַרְבָּעִים וַחֲמִשִּׁים אַמָּה. אָמַר רַבִּי יְהוּדָה: מַעֲשֶׂה בְּהֵילֶנִי הַמַּלְכָּה בְּלוֹד שֶׁהָיְתָה סוּכָּתָהּ גְּבוֹהָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה, וְהָיוּ זְקֵנִים נִכְנָסִין וְיוֹצְאִין לְשָׁם וְלֹא אָמְרוּ לַהּ דָּבָר.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara **raises an objection** from a *baraita*: **A *sukka* that is more than twenty cubits high is unfit. Rabbi Yehuda deems** a *sukka* **fit** even if it is **up to forty or fifty cubits** high. **Rabbi Yehuda said:** There was **an incident involving Queen Helene in Lod where her *sukka* was more than twenty cubits high, and the Elders were entering and exiting** the *sukka* **and did not say anything to her** about the *sukka* not being fit. This is Rabbi Yehuda's proof: the Sages saw the structure and made no objection, which he takes as implicit endorsement that a *sukkah* higher than twenty *amos* is valid.

### Queen Status

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרוּ לוֹ: מִשָּׁם רְאָיָה? אִשָּׁה הָיְתָה וּפְטוּרָה מִן הַסּוּכָּה.
>
> **Translation:** The Rabbis **said to him:** Is there **proof from there? She was,** after all, **a woman and** therefore **exempt from the** mitzva of ***sukka*.** The elders may simply have kept their silence because it was not their place to correct a queen about an obligation that did not apply to her.

### Seven Sons

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר לָהֶן: וַהֲלֹא שִׁבְעָה בָּנִים הֲווֹ לָהּ, וְעוֹד: כׇּל מַעֲשֶׂיהָ לֹא עָשְׂתָה אֶלָּא עַל פִּי חֲכָמִים.
>
> **Translation:** Rabbi Yehuda **said to them** in response: **Didn't she have seven sons** and therefore require a fit *sukkah*? **And furthermore, she performed all of her actions only in accordance with** the directives of **the Sages.** And moreover, she did everything in accordance with the rulings of the *Sages*.

### Probability Logic

The *Gemara* interjects: why does Rabbi Yehuda need to add the second argument about her piety, once he has already mentioned the seven sons? The *Gemara* explains:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְמָה לִי לְמִיתְנֵי "וְעוֹד כׇּל מַעֲשֶׂיהָ לֹא עָשְׂתָה אֶלָּא עַל פִּי חֲכָמִים"? הָכִי קָאָמַר לְהוּ: כִּי תֹּאמְרוּ בָּנִים קְטַנִּים הָיוּ, וּקְטַנִּים פְּטוּרִין מִן הַסּוּכָּה — כֵּיוָן דְּשִׁבְעָה הֲווֹ, אִי אֶפְשָׁר דְּלָא הָוֵי בְּהוּ חַד שֶׁאֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ לְאִמּוֹ.
>
> **Translation:** Before analyzing the objection being raised from the *baraita*, the Gemara seeks to understand its content. **Why do I** need Rabbi Yehuda **to teach: And furthermore, she performed all of her actions only in accordance with** the directives of **the Sages?** His first contention was sufficient. The Gemara answers that **this is what** Rabbi Yehuda **is saying to them: If you say** that Helene's sons **were minor sons and minors are exempt from the** mitzva of ***sukka*,** and that is why the Elders said nothing; **since they were seven** sons, then **it is not possible that there was not** at least **one among them who no longer needed his mother** to look after him. The *halakha* is that a minor who no longer needs his mother has reached the age of training and is required to fulfill the mitzva of *sukka* by rabbinic law. Even if she gave birth to them in consecutive years, the oldest would be seven years old, and at that age a child does not need his mother to constantly look after him.

Rabbi Yehuda does not actually know this by probability; he knew the story and knew the facts. It is the *Gemara* itself that is reasoning probabilistically, establishing that Rabbi Yehuda's counter-argument was necessarily sound — with seven sons, at least one must have reached the threshold of *chinuch*.

### Piety Argument

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי תֵּימְרוּ קָטָן שֶׁאֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ לְאִמּוֹ מִדְּרַבָּנַן הוּא דְּמִיחַיַּיב, וְאִיהִי בִּדְרַבָּנַן לָא מַשְׁגְּחָה, תָּא שְׁמַע: וְעוֹד, כׇּל מַעֲשֶׂיהָ לֹא עָשְׂתָה אֶלָּא עַל פִּי חֲכָמִים.
>
> **Translation:** **And if you say** that **a child who no longer needs his mother is obligated** in the mitzva of *sukka* only **by rabbinic law, and Queen Helene did not observe rabbinic law, come** and **hear** that which Rabbi Yehuda said: **And furthermore, she performed all of her actions only in accordance with** the directives of **the Sages.**

The *Gemara* is thus using Queen Hilni's case as a test for the various theories about the twenty-*amos* limit. Having established that the case involved obligated parties — children of *chinuch* age, and a queen who scrupulously followed even rabbinic rulings — we cannot dismiss the elders' silence as irrelevant. The case will be used on the following *daf* to probe which of the competing rationales best accounts for the parameters of the debate.

It is worth noting what the *Gemara* is doing here as a piece of Talmudic method. Rabbi Yehuda clearly knew the historical facts of Queen Hilni's household. He would not have needed to count to seven to know whether any of her sons had reached the age of *chinuch*. The *Gemara*'s probabilistic reasoning — *keivan de-shiva havu* — is not Rabbi Yehuda's argument; it is the *Gemara* independently verifying that Rabbi Yehuda's retort was well-grounded. This is a characteristic Talmudic move: reading the logic back into the dispute in order to test its foundations, even when the original disputant was working from specific knowledge rather than inference.
