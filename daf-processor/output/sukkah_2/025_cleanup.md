# Sukkah 2 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Masechet Overview

### Five Chapters Intro

We begin a new *masechta* today: *Sukkah*. *Sukkah* consists of five chapters and is, for the most part, logically organized. One might expect it to open with a discussion of the *mitzvah* of sitting in a *sukkah*, but in fact it begins with the structural requirements of the *sukkah* itself, devoting the first two chapters to that subject. Toward the end of the second chapter it turns to the *mitzvah* of dwelling in a *sukkah*, and then the third chapter is devoted to *lulav* and *esrog*, with the fourth and fifth chapters taking up *Hallel* and related topics. The first three chapters are certainly the primary ones in terms of both the objects of the *mitzvah* and the *mitzvos* themselves that apply on *Sukkos*.

### Rishonim & Sources

In contrast to *Yoma*, where much of the material is no longer practically relevant, *Sukkah* is almost entirely applicable today, and as a result it attracts considerably more *Rishonim*. Beyond *Rashi* and *Tosafot*, there is a *Ritva* on *Sukkah*, as well as extensive discussions by the *Rif*, the *Ramban*, and the *Baal HaMaor*. And that is just on the first two chapters. The third chapter — on *lulav* and *esrog* — inspired entire monographs: the *Ra'avad* composed a *Hilchos Lulav*, and the *Ramban* wrote a *Hilchos Lulav v'Esrog* devoted to the laws of the four species. So there is substantially more *Rishonim* on this *masechta* than on *Yoma*, as one might expect given its ongoing practical relevance.

### Three Criteria

Let us turn now to *daf* 2a. The opening *mishna* reads:

<!-- Sukkah she-hi gevoha lemala me'esrim ama pesula --> A *sukkah* that is higher than twenty *amos* is invalid. <!-- Rebbi Yehuda machshir --> Rabbi Yehuda says it is valid.

<!-- Ve-she'eina gevoha asara tefachim --> If it does not have a minimum height of ten handbreadths; <!-- ve-she'ein la shalosh o shelosha defanos --> if it does not have three walls; <!-- ve-she-chamasa meruba metzilasa --> and if the sunlight inside the *sukkah* is greater than the shade — *pesula*, it is invalid.

So we have three primary criteria. The first concerns height: the *sukkah* must be at least ten handbreadths tall but no more than twenty *amos* high. The second is structural: it requires a minimum of three walls. The third is functional: it must provide shade.

Conspicuously absent from this *mishna* are the horizontal dimensions — there is no mention of minimum area or of minimum length and width. Also absent is any discussion of what materials are valid for walls, or for the *sechach* itself. That absence is itself worth noting.

One point worth thinking about regarding the third criterion: when the *mishna* says that the *sukkah* is invalid if the sun is greater than the shade — is this a statement about the *sechach* itself, about its quality and density? Or is it a statement about the objective reality of being in the *sukkah* at a given moment, regardless of the *sechach*? One can imagine a *sukkah* with perfectly kosher *sechach* where, due to the angle of the sun at a particular time of day, more sunlight enters than shade is produced. Is it the quality of the *sechach* that is being assessed, or the actual state of affairs inside the *sukkah* at any given moment? These are questions the *masechta* will explore.

### Psul vs. Takanta

The *Gemara* now raises a comparative question. It notes: <!-- tenan hasam --> we have taught elsewhere, in the very first *mishna* of *Eruvin*: <!-- mavoi she-hu gavoah me'esrim ama yima'et --> an alleyway whose beam (*korah*) is higher than twenty *amos* must be lowered. <!-- Rebbi Yehuda omer eino tzarich --> Rabbi Yehuda says it need not be lowered.

A brief clarification of what a *mavoi* is: it is a dead-end alleyway into which multiple courtyards open. The courtyards themselves contain houses. The *mavoi* has three walls but is open on one side toward the public domain. In order to permit carrying within the *mavoi*, it requires both a communal sharing arrangement (*shituf mevo'os*) and a structural marker at its open end — either a vertical post (*lechi*) or a horizontal beam (*korah*). This beam must be within twenty *amos* of the ground; if it is higher, it must be lowered.

The *Gemara* observes the striking parallel: both *Sukkah* and *Eruvin* open with essentially the same *mishna* and essentially the same debate — a twenty-*amos* limit that Rabbi Yehuda contests in both cases. The question the *Gemara* then presses is: <!-- mai shna gabei sukkah detani psulah, umai shna gabei mavoi detani takanta --> how is it different by *sukkah* that the *mishna* uses the language of *invalidity* (*pesula*), while by the *mavoi* it uses the language of *repair* (*yima'et*, lower it)? Why not say "invalid" in both cases, or "fix it" in both?

### Rashi: Reality Layer

The *Gemara* answers: <!-- sukkah de'oraisa tani psulah --> *sukkah* is a biblical obligation, so the *mishna* says "invalid." <!-- Mavoi derabbanan tani takanta --> the *mavoi* is rabbinic, so the *mishna* says "fix it." But what is the logic behind that distinction? Why should the biblical or rabbinic character of the law affect which language is used?

*Rashi* offers a profound explanation. He writes: <!-- de'esrim ama hilchesa gmiri lah mide'oraisa ve-kodem she-nishnes ha-mishna ne'emar shiur mi-Sinai --> the twenty-*amos* measure is a tradition received from Sinai as a matter of biblical law. The *mishna*, therefore, is not legislating; it is *reporting an objective fact*. The *sukkah* that exceeds twenty *amos* is, from the perspective of the *Torah*, simply and objectively invalid. The *mishna* uses the language *pesula* because it is describing an objective metaphysical reality: in the *Torah*'s framework, that structure is not a *sukkah*.

The *mavoi*, by contrast, is a rabbinic creation from beginning to end. Biblically, three walls are sufficient for a domain, and no *korah* is required at all. The rabbis decided to require it, and the rabbis decided it must be within twenty *amos*. When the rabbis are themselves the legislators, they cannot say "invalid" in the same categorical way, because there is no pre-existing objective reality they are reporting. They are the ones who instituted the requirement, and what they can say is: "We want it fixed." As *Rashi* puts it, <!-- delo shayach le-misni bei lashon psulah de-man paslah --> who invalidated it before this *mishna* was taught? It was not invalid before the rabbis said so. The *mishna* on the *mavoi* is the very inauguration of that requirement. So it says: lower it. It cannot declare something invalid when it is the first word on the matter.

This is a striking idea. It evokes a kind of *halakhic* realism — a platonic structure in which the *Torah*'s laws correspond to objective *halakhic* realities that exist independently. The *mishna* on *sukkah* is not creating the invalidation; it is discovering and reporting it. The *mishna* on *mavoi*, by contrast, is the creative act itself. It would be odd to call a thing invalid at the very moment one is first calling it into existence.

One might ask whether this distinction carries through consistently — does the *Gemara* always use the language of invalidity for biblical laws and the language of correction for rabbinic ones? The honest answer is no. Neither *Rashi*'s explanation nor *Tosafot*'s is universally applied across the *Talmud*. This is the explanation for this specific context.

### Tosafot: Brevity

*Tosafot* offers two quite different explanations for the same phenomenon.

The first is pragmatic. When a *mishna* says "invalid," there is no ambiguity — it is invalid in all circumstances, even after the fact (*bedi'eved*). One cannot rationalize: "Well, perhaps in practice it is acceptable." But if the *mishna* says "lower it" — *yima'et*, fix it — one might reason: "The *mishna* told me to fix it, but since I did not get around to it, perhaps it is still acceptable after the fact." That potential for misinterpretation is a real risk. When the law is biblical, the *mishna* will not risk that misreading; it uses the unambiguous language of invalidity. When the law is rabbinic, the *mishna* can afford to risk the misinterpretation, because the practical guidance offered by "lower it" — telling the person how to address the situation — is worth something.

The second explanation is stylistic conciseness. The *Gemara* states: <!-- sukkah she-nafishi mileiah tani pasul --> the *sukkah* *mishna* has many parameters to enumerate. It would have to say: if it is too high, lower it; if it is too low, raise it; if the *sechach* is insufficient, add more; if there are too few walls, add more walls. The whole list. Rather than spell out remedies for each problem, the *mishna* simply says *pesula* — invalid — covering everything in one word. <!-- Mavoi de-lo nafishi mileiah tani takanta --> the *mavoi* case in this *mishna* addresses only one scenario (height). There it is possible to say concisely: lower it. This follows the general principle articulated at the opening *sugya* of *Pesachim* that one should always teach in brief.

There is also an interesting structural observation worth making here. Many *masechtos* open with what, based on a letter of Rav Sherira Gaon, scholars identify as *Savoraic* material — that is, text added in the period immediately after the *Gemara*'s final redaction, though the point applies to the opening of a *masechta* more broadly. Such openings characteristically contain no named statements by *Tannaim* or *Amoraim*; they are *stam*, anonymous. They tend to focus on comparative analysis of *mishnayos*, playing with parallels and linguistic distinctions, rather than advancing new *halakhic* rulings. The opening of *Kiddushin*, for example, spends considerable time on why the *mishna* uses the passive form *nikneit* rather than the language of *kiddushin*, and why it speaks from the woman's perspective rather than the man's. Such openings often serve a pedagogical function: easing the student in while also introducing themes that will recur throughout the *masechta*. Here, the parallel between *sukkah* and *mavoi* — both with the same twenty-*amos* limit, both with Rabbi Yehuda disagreeing — is not merely a curiosity. It raises a question that will resonate throughout the *masechta*: is there a single shared source underlying both requirements and both debates?

## 20-Amah Sources

### Rava: Awareness

<!-- Mina hani mili --> where do these laws come from? The *Gemara* now turns to the biblical and conceptual foundations of the twenty-*amos* requirement.

<!-- Amar Rava de'amar kra --> Rava says, the verse states: <!-- lema'an yedu doroseichem ki ba-sukkos hoshavti es bnei Yisrael --> "in order that your generations should know that I caused the children of Israel to dwell in booths." <!-- Ad esrim ama adam yodeia she-hu dar ba-sukkah --> up to twenty *amos*, a person is aware that he is dwelling in a *sukkah*. <!-- Lema'ala me-esrim ama ein adam yodeia she-hu dar ba-sukkah --> higher than twenty *amos*, a person is not aware that he is dwelling in a *sukkah*. Why? <!-- Mishum de-lo shalta bei eina --> because the eye does not take it in.

This requires a brief explanation of a key principle that will govern the entire *masechta*: for *Chazal*, the *ikar* of the *sukkah* is the *sechach*. The walls are necessary, but the defining element of the *sukkah* — the element for which it is named — is the *sechach*. *Rashi* states this explicitly in his comment just before the *Gemara* begins: <!-- ve'al shem ha-sechach keruya sukkah --> the *sukkah* is named for the *sechach*. This means that any verse referring to *sukkah* will be understood as referring primarily to the *sechach*, not the walls.

So when Rava says the eye does not see it — what the eye fails to see is the *sechach*. The walls may be perfectly visible; they dominate the visual field. But if the *sukkah* is more than twenty *amos* tall and the *sechach* is at the top, a person standing inside will see only walls. The *sechach* is too far above to enter naturally into one's awareness. And since the verse commands that future generations should *know* they are dwelling in *sukkos*, Rava reads this as a functional requirement: the *sechach* must be within the range of natural perception. If one must crane one's neck to see it, the awareness the verse demands is absent.

One might note that the plain sense (*pshat*) of the verse is historical: it is an educational imperative to remember what God did for Israel in the wilderness. Rava could be understood as saying that this historical awareness cannot be accessed unless one is presently conscious of sitting under *sechach*. But *Rashi* and the subsequent *Gemara* appear to read Rava as deriving an actual structural requirement directly from the verse — that the *sechach* must be perceptible — not merely as a means to an end.

It is worth noting here as well the connection to a broader theme. The idea that the *sukkah* commemorates the clouds of glory (*ananei kavod*) that accompanied Israel in the wilderness is evocative in this context — one naturally imagines those clouds as being quite high up. We will return to the *ananei kavod* theme later; for now, Rava's derivation focuses squarely on the perceptibility of the *sechach*.

### Rav Zeira: Shade

<!-- Ve-Zeira amar meihacha --> Rav Zeira says, from this verse in *Yeshayahu* (Isaiah 4): <!-- ve-sukkah tihyeh le-tzel yomam meichorev --> "and a *sukkah* shall be for shade in the day from the heat." The full verse in its context is a Messianic vision: in the age of redemption, God will create over all of Mount Zion a cloud by day and a fiery glow by night — evoking the imagery of the exodus — and a protective covering, a *sukkah*, will provide shade from the heat and shelter from the rain.

It is worth pausing over the context, because the verse is not referring to the *mitzvah* of *sukkah* in any direct sense. It is part of an eschatological portrait in *Yeshayahu*, where the prophet foresees that the glory of Mount Zion will be covered: <!-- uvara Hashem al kol machon har Tziyon ve'al mikraeha anan yomam ve'ashan ve-nogah esh lehavah laylah --> "And God will create over all of Mount Zion and over its assemblies a cloud by day and smoke, and the brightness of a flaming fire by night." And then: <!-- ve-sukkah tihyeh le-tzel yomam meichorev ve'al kol kavod chuppah u-lema-chseh u-le-mistor mi-zerem u-mi-matar --> "and a *sukkah* shall be for shade in the day from the heat, and for a canopy, and for shelter and a refuge from storm and rain."

Even setting aside the Messianic context, this verse is already doing something powerful: it connects the *sukkah* of the *mitzvah* to a Messianic vision of divine protection, and it evokes the *anan* — the cloud that accompanied Israel through the wilderness. For those who know their *Tanach* as they should, encountering this verse at the outset of *Sukkah* introduces quietly but significantly the theme of *sukkah ke-neged ananei kavod* — the *sukkah* as representing the clouds of glory. The verse from *Yeshayahu* thus serves double duty in the *Gemara*: it supports a legal derivation, and it sounds a thematic note that will recur.

Now for the legal application. Rav Zeira derives from this verse: <!-- ad esrim ama adam yoshev be-tzel sukkah --> up to twenty *amos*, a person is sitting in the shade of the *sukkah* — meaning, the *sechach* is actually providing shade. <!-- Lema'ala me-esrim ama ein adam yoshev be-tzel sukkah ela be-tzel defanot --> above twenty *amos*, the person is not sitting in the shade of the *sukkah* (the *sechach*), but rather in the shade of the walls. The walls, being so tall, are what shade the space below, while the *sechach* — small and high above — contributes nothing effective. The *sukkah* verse in *Yeshayahu* uses the word *tzel* — shade — and the legal requirement is that the *sechach* must actually function as the source of shade.

### Abaye: Mountains

<!-- Amar lei Abaye --> Abaye challenged Rav Zeira: <!-- ela me'ata, oseh sukkato be-ashrot karnayim, hachi nami de-lo havei sukkah --> if the problem is that the walls rather than the *sechach* are providing the shade, then consider someone who builds his *sukkah* between two very high mountains. Are you going to say that it is not a valid *sukkah* because the mountains are already shading the valley?

### Zeira: Schach Quality

<!-- Amar lei --> Rav Zeira responded: <!-- hatam dal ashrot karnayim ika tzel sukkah --> there, if you remove the mountains, the *sechach* still provides shade. The *sechach* itself, intrinsically, has the quality to shade the space beneath it — the mountains are merely extrinsic additions to what the *sechach* is doing on its own. <!-- Hacha dal defanot leika tzel sukkah --> here, by contrast, if you remove the walls, you have no shade from the *sechach* at all.

This is a crucial distinction. The argument is not simply that the walls happen to be providing more shade than the *sechach* in practice. The argument is that when the *sukkah* exceeds twenty *amos*, the *sechach* is so high that it is incapable of providing shade regardless of the walls — even in the abstract, even if you were to look at the *sechach* in isolation. When sunlight strikes at any angle other than directly overhead (and we can largely discount the moment of absolute zenith), a small patch of *sechach* situated twenty or more *amos* above the ground will cast its shade well outside the interior of the *sukkah*. The shade produced by the *sechach* falls elsewhere. Remove the walls, and you have no shade underneath — the shade is somewhere else entirely. In the mountain case, the *sechach* is perfectly capable of providing shade; it is just being supplemented by the mountains. In the too-tall *sukkah*, the *sechach* is not capable of providing shade in its given position, full stop.

One might ask whether this should depend on the area of the *sechach*: if the *sechach* is very wide, surely it can shade the space beneath it even at a great height? The *Gemara* will address this shortly.

### Rava: Temp Structure

<!-- Ve-Rava amar meha --> Rava (in a second formulation distinct from his verse-based derivation above) offers yet another source: <!-- ba-sukkot teshu shivat yamim --> "you shall dwell in *sukkos* for seven days." <!-- Amra Torah, kol shivat ha-yamim, tze midirat keva ve-shev be-dirat arai --> the *Torah* commands: for all seven days, leave your permanent dwelling and dwell in a temporary structure. The word *sukkah* implicitly contrasts with your house, your permanent residence. The *sukkah* must be, by definition, a *dirat arai* — a temporary dwelling.

<!-- Ve'ad esrim ama adam oseh dirato dirat arai --> up to twenty *amos*, it is possible to construct a dwelling that has the character of a temporary structure. <!-- Lema'ala me-esrim ama, de-adam oseh dirato dirat keva --> above twenty *amos*, the scale and construction required to support such a structure make it inherently a permanent dwelling. <!-- Ein adam oseh dirato dirat arai --> no one builds a temporary dwelling that tall. <!-- Ela dirat keva --> it must be a permanent structure, and it is therefore invalid.

### Abaye: Materials

<!-- Amar lei Abaye --> Abaye challenged this formulation: <!-- asu mechitzot shel barzel ve-sichach gaban, hacha nami dlav sukkah? --> if the requirement is genuinely that the *sukkah* be a *dirat arai*, a temporary structure, then what if someone constructed a *sukkah* of normal dimensions but used metal walls? Is that not a permanent structure? Would you say that building a perfectly standard-sized *sukkah* out of brick and mortar — going out after *Yom Kippur* and constructing four solid walls with cement, then placing *sechach* on top — is invalid because it is a *dirat keva* rather than a *dirat arai*?

### Rava: Dimensions

<!-- Amar lei, hachi ka'amina lach --> Rava clarified: this is what I mean. <!-- Ad esrim ama, de-adam oseh dirato dirat arai --> up to twenty *amos*, the dimensions are such that a structure *can* be a temporary dwelling. <!-- Ki avid dirat keva nami nafik --> so even if you use the materials of a permanent dwelling (metal walls, brick, cement), you are still *yotzei*, because the *dimensions* are within the range of a temporary structure. <!-- Lema'ala me-esrim ama, de-adam oseh dirato dirat keva --> but above twenty *amos*, those dimensions fall exclusively within the range of a permanent structure. <!-- Ki avid dirat arai nami lo nafik --> even if you build your walls out of straw like the first little pig, and it is above twenty *amos*, you are not *yotzei*.

The resolution is elegant: the *halacha* of temporary versus permanent, like most *halacha*, is operationalized through quantifiable criteria — specifically, dimensions — rather than building materials. What distinguishes a *dirat arai* from a *dirat keva* in the law's framework is the scale of the structure, not what it is made of. A structure under twenty *amos* can function as a *dirat arai* in terms of its proportions, whatever material is used. A structure above twenty *amos* takes on the character of a permanent building by its very dimensions alone.

## Tosafot: Permanence

### Tosafot: Nails

This leads to a natural question that *Tosafot* raises. We have said that the wall material is irrelevant — even brick walls are acceptable. But what about the *sechach* itself? Can the *sechach* be made permanent? Specifically, *Tosafot* asks: <!-- ve-halo yachol likvoa ha-nessarim ba-masmerim --> could one not take planks of wood (which are valid *sechach* if the wood is from something that grew from the ground and is no longer attached) and nail them down permanently, <!-- shelo yerdu geshamim ba-sukkah --> so that no rain would enter? Would that be biblically valid?

### Tosafot: Non-Permanent

<!-- Tosafot --> answers: <!-- u-veita vadai pasul mi-de'oraita --> using your actual house is clearly invalid, as the *Torah* says *sukkah* and not a house that is established all year round. But what about building a new structure with a permanent wood roof? *Tosafot* writes: <!-- de-nehi de-lo chaishinan bi-defanot i avid lehu keva --> granted, we are not concerned if the *walls* are made permanent, because as we have established, wall material is not what defines the *sukkah*. <!-- Mikol makom ba-sechach she-ikar ha-sukkah al shem ha-sechach --> but the *sechach*, which is the defining element of the *sukkah* (and from which the *sukkah* takes its name), <!-- lo mishtarya ha-sukkah ad de-avid lah arai --> the *sukkah* is not valid unless the *sechach* is made temporary.

*Tosafot* does not spell out a precise quantifiable threshold for what makes *sechach* "permanent" in this sense. The question of when nailed planks cross the line from permissible *sechach* to an invalid permanent roof is one that cannot be fully quantified. But the principle is that at some point, if the roof is constructed in a manner that makes it genuinely permanent, it ceases to be *sechach* and becomes a house. The *sukkah* is no longer a *sukkah*; it is simply a building.

### Rain Sign

One of *Tosafot*'s criteria for identifying *sechach* that has crossed the line into permanent structure is rain impermeability. The verse from *Yeshayahu* that Rav Zeira cited includes, at its end, the phrase <!-- u-le-mistor mi-zerem u-mi-matar --> "and for shelter and refuge from storm and rain." One might have thought that this describes a *virtue* of the *sukkah* — it keeps out the rain. But *Tosafot* argues the opposite: the fact that a Messianic *sukkah* is described as providing shelter from rain is precisely because in the present context, a *sukkah* that is impervious to rain is not a valid *sukkah* at all. If the *sechach* blocks out rain entirely, it is a sign of a permanent roof, a *dirat keva*, not a *dirat arai*. This is why the rain that falls on *Sukkos* is called a *siman kelalah ba-chag* — a sign of a curse on the festival. If it were possible to build a *sukkah* that keeps rain out entirely, there would be no curse in rain on the festival. The very fact that rain is an unwelcome visitor demonstrates that a valid *sukkah* must be open to rain.

This is a structurally interesting point: *Tosafot* uses the theme of the *sukkah*'s vulnerability to rain not merely as a poetic observation about the nature of the *mitzvah*, but as a legal criterion. A *sukkah* that keeps rain out entirely has, by that very quality, identified itself as a permanent structure, and permanent *sechach* is invalid. The emphasis on the *sechach* — *ikar ha-sukkah al shem ha-sechach* — means that the *sechach* cannot be permanent even while the walls can.

## Debate Limits

### Yoshiya: Wall Gap

Having established the three distinct rationales for the twenty-*amos* limit — Rava's awareness/visibility argument, Rav Zeira's shade argument, and Rava's *dirat arai* argument — the *Gemara* now examines how each model generates different qualifications of the debate.

<!-- Ke-man azla ha de-amar Rebbi Yoshiyah? --> According to whom does the following ruling of Rebbi Yoshiyah go? <!-- Amar Rav, machloket be-she'ein defanos megi'os la-sechach --> the whole debate in the *mishna* (between the *Sages* and Rabbi Yehuda) applies only where the walls do not reach all the way up to the *sechach*. There is a gap between the top of the wall and the *sechach*.

<!-- Aval defanos megi'os la-sechach, afilu lema'ala me-esrim ama kesherah --> but if the walls extend all the way up to the *sechach*, even higher than twenty *amos*, everyone would agree it is valid.

### Eye Following

<!-- Ke-man? --> Like whom does this go? <!-- Ke-Rabbah --> it can only make sense according to Rava's first rationale, <!-- de-amar mishum de-lo shalta bei eina --> that the problem is the eye does not naturally see the *sechach*. <!-- Ve-cheivan de-defanos megi'os la-sechach, mishlach shalta bei eina --> if the walls go all the way up to the *sechach*, the eye naturally follows the wall upward, and it will reach the point where wall meets *sechach*, bringing the *sechach* into view. By contrast, if the wall ends and there is a gap of open air before the *sechach*, the eye stops at the top of the wall and does not travel further to find the *sechach*. If the wall meets the *sechach* directly, however high, the gaze is drawn continuously upward until it takes in the *sechach*.

This qualification makes no sense on the other two theories. Whether the walls reach the *sechach* or not is irrelevant to whether the *sechach* provides shade in practice, and irrelevant to whether the dimensions suggest a permanent or temporary structure.

### Huna: Area

<!-- Ke-man azla ha de-amar Rav Huna amar Rav? --> According to whom goes the statement of Rav Huna in the name of Rav? <!-- Machloket be-she'ein bah ela arba amos --> the whole debate applies only when the *sukkah* is no more than four by four *amos* in area. <!-- Aval yesh bah yoter me-arba amos al arba amos, afilu lema'ala me-esrim ama kesherah --> but if the *sukkah* is more than four by four *amos*, even higher than twenty *amos* is valid.

### Shade Proportional

<!-- Ke-man? Ke-Rebbi Zeira --> this can only make sense according to Rav Zeira, <!-- de-amar mishum tzel --> that the problem is whether the *sechach* provides shade in practice. <!-- Ve-cheivan de-rivcha, ika tzel sukkah --> since the *sechach* is wide enough, even at a great height it provides shade. The intuition is clear: a small patch of *sechach* high up will let sunlight come through at most angles; but a large expanse of *sechach* at the same height will block more of the sunlight, and shade will fall beneath it.

### Proportionality

One might ask: should this not be fully proportional? If a *sechach* four by four *amos* wide can be at twenty *amos* height, should a *sechach* twenty by twenty *amos* be permitted at a proportionally greater height? Should there be a sliding scale?

*Tosafot* raises this point and offers two responses. The first is that perhaps it is indeed proportional — as the *sechach* grows wider, greater heights become permissible. *Tosafot* does not rule this out entirely.

But the more characteristic rabbinic answer, which *Tosafot* develops by reference back to *Yoma*, is this: <!-- yesh lomar, de-le'olam tzarikh ha-rochav le-fi ha-govah le-fi cheshbon arba amot le-esrim --> it would always be proportional in principle. But the *Gemara* also says: <!-- i nami, kim lehu le-rabanan de-afilu be-govah elef, de-afilu be-govah elef, i efshar she-lo yikanes tzel sechach purta --> the Rabbis knew that even at a height of a thousand *amos*, once the *sechach* is more than four by four *amos*, it is impossible that no shade at all would enter. And once it is less than four by four, even at twenty *amos* it will fail.

*Tosafot* supports this with a reference to *Yom Kippur*: just as in that context the Rabbis taught that once you have the right minimum quantity — a pressed date — it has an effect even for *Og King of Bashan* who ate it, and a little less than that has no effect for anyone — so too here. Once you cross the threshold of four by four *amos*, there will always be some shade regardless of height. Below that threshold, height above twenty *amos* will always be a problem. The Rabbis knew this and set a fixed number rather than a proportional formula.

### Chana: Minimum

<!-- Ke-man azla ha de-amar Rav Chana bar Rabba amar Rav? Machloket be-she'eina machzeket ela kedei rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano --> According to whom goes the statement of Rav Chana bar Rabba in the name of Rav? He says the debate applies only when the *sukkah* holds no more than the absolute minimum: a person's head, most of his body, and his small table. <!-- Aval machzeket yoter mi-kedei rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano, afilu lema'ala me-esrim ama kesherah --> if it is even slightly larger than that minimum, even above twenty *amos* is valid.

A word of clarification: why would one's "head and most of one's body" not simply mean a person sitting up? The answer lies in the *halacha* that eating in the *sukkah* is ideally done in a reclining position. So the minimum *sukkah* must accommodate one's head and most of one's body while reclining, plus a small tray table. The *Gemara* later establishes this minimum as approximately seven by seven handbreadths (*shiva al shiva tefachim*) — roughly two feet by two feet. This is far smaller than four *amos* by four *amos* (roughly six feet by six feet), which is about sixteen times the area.

<!-- Ke-man azla? --> Like whom does Rav Chana bar Rabba's formulation go? <!-- De-lo ke-chad --> it does not fit any of our three models. If the concern is the *sechach* providing shade, then something just slightly larger than the absolute minimum (*rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano*) — still far less than four by four *amos* — would not be large enough to provide shade at twenty *amos* height. And if the concern is permanent versus temporary structure, it is hard to see how being just above the bare minimum matters.

### Chicken Coop

*Tosafot* proposes an independent logic for this position. *Tosafot* suggests: <!-- lul shel tarnigolim --> a chicken coop. If the *sukkah* is at the absolute minimum size — just barely accommodating one's head, most of one's body, and a table — and it is also extremely tall, the result is an absurd structure that no one would ever recognize as a dwelling of any kind. It is too narrow and too tall. Once it is even slightly larger than the minimum, however, it escapes the category of a structural absurdity and can be recognized as some kind of dwelling, even if a small one. The problem of exceeding twenty *amos* applies only when the structure, combined with its tiny footprint, cannot even pass as a dwelling. Once it is a bit larger, the problem disappears.

### Gemara: Two Models

<!-- Bishlama le-deRebbi Yoshiyah, pligi de-Rav Huna ve-Rav Chana bar Rabba --> it makes sense that Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba's qualifications disagree with each other: Rebbi Yoshiyah's qualification (whether the walls reach the *sechach*) is about a different dimension of the structure entirely — it speaks to the vertical relationship between wall and *sechach* — while Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba both speak to the horizontal dimensions of the *sukkah*. Those two can coexist as independent debates.

But the *Gemara* then asks: perhaps Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba are not debating the *reason* for the twenty-*amos* requirement at all. Perhaps they both agree on the reason, and what they are really debating is simply: <!-- be-hechsher sukkah ka-mifligi --> what is the minimum valid size of a *sukkah*? <!-- Mar savar hechsher sukkah be-arba amot --> one holds the minimum is four by four *amos*. <!-- U-mar savar hechsher sukkah be-machzeket rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano --> the other holds it is only *rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano*. They both agree that the twenty-*amos* debate applies only to a minimum-size *sukkah*, and they are simply disagreeing about what minimum size is.

The *Gemara* rejects this: <!-- de-kulei alma hechsher sukkah be-rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano --> everyone agrees the minimum *sukkah* is *rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano*, *shiva tefachim*. The debate between Rav Huna and Rav Chana bar Rabba is not about the minimum size of a *sukkah*; it is about when height above twenty *amos* becomes a problem.

<!-- De-mar savar machzeket rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano pligi --> according to one, the debate about height applies to *any* size from the bare minimum (*shiva al shiva tefachim*) upward, but <!-- aval yoter me-rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano, divrei ha-kol kesherah --> once it is larger, everyone agrees it is valid. <!-- U-mar savar --> the other holds: yes, I agree that once it is *rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano* it qualifies as a *sukkah*, but the debate does not end there. <!-- Mi-rosho ve-rubo ve-shulchano ad arba amot pligi --> anywhere from the minimum up until four by four *amos*, there is a debate; <!-- aval yoter me-arba amot, divrei ha-kol kesherah --> once it is more than four *amos*, everyone agrees it is valid.

The *Gemara* thus insists on its framing: the various qualifications proposed by Rebbi Yoshiyah, Rav Huna, and Rav Chana bar Rabba each track one of the underlying rationales for the twenty-*amos* limit. Rebbi Yoshiyah's qualification (wall-meets-*sechach*) follows Rava's awareness model. Rav Huna's qualification (four *amos* of area) follows Rav Zeira's shade model, since a larger *sechach* provides shade even at great height. And the *sugya* continues to press toward identifying which of these models best accounts for the full range of qualifications the *Amora'im* propose.

## Hilni Precedent

### Hilni's High Sukkah

The *Gemara* now introduces a *baraita* that presents a historical case, and Rabbi Yehuda uses it as a proof for his position:

<!-- Amar Rebbi Yehuda, ma'aseh be-Hilni ha-malka, be-Lod --> there was an incident with Queen Hilni in Lod, <!-- she-haysa sukkasa gevoha me-esrim ama --> her *sukkah* was higher than twenty *amos*. <!-- Ve-hayu zkeinim nichnasim ve-yotzim le-sham ve-lo amru lah davar --> and elders would enter and exit there and said nothing to her. This is Rabbi Yehuda's proof: the Sages saw the structure and made no objection, which he takes as implicit endorsement that a *sukkah* higher than twenty *amos* is valid.

### Queen Status

<!-- Amru lo, misham ra'aya? --> They challenged him: you want to bring proof from there? <!-- Isha haysa --> she was a woman, <!-- petura min ha-sukkah --> women are exempt from the *mitzvah* of *sukkah*. The elders may simply have kept their silence because it was not their place to correct a queen about an obligation that did not apply to her.

### Seven Sons

<!-- Amar lahen --> Rabbi Yehuda responded: <!-- ve-lo shiva banim havu lah? --> but she had seven sons with her in the *sukkah*! And moreover, <!-- ve-od kol ma'aseh lo asta ela al pi chachamim --> she did everything in accordance with the rulings of the *Sages*.

### Probability Logic

The *Gemara* interjects: <!-- lama li le-misni ve-od kol ma'aseh lo asta ela al pi chachamim --> why does Rabbi Yehuda need to add the second argument about her piety, once he has already mentioned the seven sons? The *Gemara* explains: <!-- hachi ka'amar lehu --> this is what Rabbi Yehuda was anticipating. <!-- Ki teimru banim ketanim hayu, u-ketanim pturim min ha-sukkah --> you might say: all seven boys were young children, and young children are exempt from *sukkah*. To which he answers: <!-- keivan de-shiva havu, i efshar de-lo hava behu chad she-eino tzarich le-imo --> since there were seven of them, it is statistically impossible that not one of them had reached the age of no longer needing his mother in the night. And that — no longer needing his mother — is the *Gemara*'s definition (elaborated later) of when a minor becomes obligated through *chinuch* to sit in a *sukkah*.

Rabbi Yehuda does not actually know this by probability; he knew the story and knew the facts. It is the *Gemara* itself that is reasoning probabilistically, establishing that Rabbi Yehuda's counter-argument was necessarily sound — with seven sons, at least one must have reached the threshold of *chinuch*.

### Piety Argument

But then one might say: even if there was a child obligated through *chinuch*, that is only a rabbinic obligation. <!-- Ve-ki teimru katan she-eino tzarich le-imo mi-derabbanan hu de-michayev, ve-i derabbanan lo mashgecha --> perhaps Queen Hilni was not scrupulous about rabbinic requirements. To this Rabbi Yehuda responds: <!-- ta shema, ve-od kol ma'aseh lo asta ela al pi chachamim --> come and hear: she did everything according to the *Sages*. Her piety encompassed rabbinic law as well.

The *Gemara* is thus using Queen Hilni's case as a test for the various theories about the twenty-*amos* limit. Having established that the case involved obligated parties — children of *chinuch* age, and a queen who scrupulously followed even rabbinic rulings — we cannot dismiss the elders' silence as irrelevant. The case will be used on the following *daf* to probe which of the competing rationales best accounts for the parameters of the debate.

It is worth noting what the *Gemara* is doing here as a piece of Talmudic method. Rabbi Yehuda clearly knew the historical facts of Queen Hilni's household. He would not have needed to count to seven to know whether any of her sons had reached the age of *chinuch*. The *Gemara*'s probabilistic reasoning — *keivan de-shiva havu* — is not Rabbi Yehuda's argument; it is the *Gemara* independently verifying that Rabbi Yehuda's retort was well-grounded. This is a characteristic Talmudic move: reading the logic back into the dispute in order to test its foundations, even when the original disputant was working from specific knowledge rather than inference.
