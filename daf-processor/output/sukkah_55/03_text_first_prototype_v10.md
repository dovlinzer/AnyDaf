## One Shir, One Tekia



### Rosh Chodesh Unity

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּיוּבְתָּא דְּרַבִּי אַחָא בַּר חֲנִינָא! תְּיוּבְתָּא. וְהָא רַבִּי אַחָא בַּר חֲנִינָא קְרָא וּמַתְנִיתָא קָאָמַר?
> **Translation:** The Gemara concludes: This *baraita* is **a conclusive refutation** of the opinion **of Rabbi Aḥa bar Ḥanina,** as on Shabbat the song for Shabbat was recited alone without the song for the Festival, and similarly, separate trumpet blasts are not sounded for the various additional offerings. Rabbi Aḥa’s opinion was rejected based on several sources. The Gemara asks: **But didn’t Rabbi Aḥa say a verse and a *baraita*** in support of his opinion? How can those citations be rejected?

We pick up with three lines from the top of 55a, continuing the discussion of what would be done for each separate *musaf* on a day when multiple *musafim* coincide. The question has two intertwined dimensions: would they perform separate blasts of the trumpets (*chatzotzrot*) for each *musaf*, and would they recite a separate *shir shel yom* for each? The claim under scrutiny is that yes, separate sets would be performed — but the *Gemara* now moves toward decisively closing that off.

*Meitvei* — the *Gemara* raises a challenge: *d'tani Rabba Bar Shmuel* — as Rabba bar Shmuel taught in a *baraita*: *Yachol k'shem she-tokin al Shabbat bifnei atzmah v'al Rosh Chodesh bifnei atzmo* — perhaps just as you have a separate set of blasts for the *Shabbat musaf* by itself and for *Rosh Chodesh* by itself, when they coincide you would likewise have separate blasts for each? *Talmud lomar u'vroshei chodsheichem* — the verse teaches otherwise: *Ani Rosh Chodesh* — I am [the single] *Rosh Chodesh*. While the precise derivation from this verse will be explored momentarily, the intent of the *baraita* is unmistakable: you do not perform separate trumpet blasts for each separate *musaf*. *Tiyuvta d'Rabbi Akha tiyuvta* — this stands as a complete refutation of Rabbi Acha's position.

### Abaye: All Unified

The *Gemara* now examines how the verse actually proves this. *Amar Abaye amar Kra* — *Abaye* explained: the verse says *u'vroshei chodsheichem* — *hukshu kol hachodashim kulam zeh lazeh* — all instances of *Rosh Chodesh* are placed in one unified category. Whether it is an ordinary *Rosh Chodesh*, a *Rosh Chodesh* that falls on *Shabbat*, or a *Rosh Chodesh* that is also *Rosh Hashanah* — they are all treated as a single category, and you perform only one set of blasts.

This derives from the verse in Numbers that discusses the trumpet blasts: *u'v'yom simchatchem u'v'moadeichem u'v'roshei chodsheichem u'tekatem bachatzotzrot al oloteichem v'al zivchei shalmeichem* — on your days of rejoicing, your appointed festivals, and your *Rosh Chodesh* occasions, you shall sound the trumpets over your offerings. The phrase *u'vroshei chodsheichem* groups all *Rosh Chodesh* occasions together as a single category — and therefore even a *Shabbat Rosh Chodesh* or a *Rosh Hashanah Rosh Chodesh* receives only one set of blasts.

### Rav Ashi: Ktiv/Kri

*Rav Ashi* offered a different reading, playing on a *kri u'ksiv* discrepancy in the same verse. *Rav Ashi amar: ksiv chodsh'chem* — the word is written without a *yud*, rendering it singular, *v'ksiv u'vroshei* — yet it is read in a plural form. *Eizu chodesh she-yesh lo shenei rashim* — which month has two heads? *Hevei omer zeh Rosh Hashanah* — it must be *Rosh Chodesh* that is simultaneously *Rosh Hashanah*. *Amar Rachmana chodsh'chem* — yet the Torah calls it singular, *chada hi* — treat it as one. The written form insists on singularity even when the day carries a double identity.

### One Set Determined

The *pshat* is thus clear: even when multiple *musafim* coincide, you perform only a single set of blasts. There is some variation in how the verse yields this conclusion — whether by reading *u'vroshei chodsheichem* as a unified category (*Abaye*) or by the *kri u'ksiv* pointing to the doubled day being treated as singular (*Rav Ashi*) — but the ruling itself is not in doubt.

It is worth pausing on a practical implication that the gemara's discussion surfaces here. The principle of one set of blasts, and by extension one *shir shel yom*, applies specifically to *Musaf*. For the *Tamid*, the regular daily psalm for that day of the week would still have been recited in the usual way. This creates a real distinction that carries forward into our own *davening*, where the question of which *shir shel yom* to say on days that overlap — *chol hamoed*, *Rosh Chodesh*, *Yom Tov* — is resolved differently across communities. The *minhag haGra*, for instance, is to say only the special psalm designated for the occasion rather than the weekday one, while on *Rosh Chodesh* the practice in many communities is in fact to say both. The gemara now proceeds to lay out precisely which psalm was designated for each day of *chol hamoed Sukkot*.

## Six Psalms of Chol HaMoed

*V'od tanya* — a further *baraita* is introduced. *Bechol hamoed ba-rishon mah hayu omrim* — what would they recite on the various days of *chol hamoed Sukkot* as the *shir shel yom*? It is worth noting, by way of background, that the *minhag haGra* — and apparently the *minhag Sefardim* as well — is that on *Yom Tov* and *chol hamoed* one recites the special psalm associated with that day rather than the psalm for that day of the week. Some communities may recite both, but the *minhag haGra* is to recite only the special one.

There is also a broader point at stake in this *baraita*: if one held that each *musaf* generates its own separate *shir shel yom*, then on a day with multiple *musafim* one would recite both and no selection would be necessary. The fact that the *baraita* requires choosing confirms that even when multiple *musafim* coincide, only one *shir shel yom* is recited — because the *shir* and the trumpet blasts are intertwined.

### Day 1: Havu L'Hashem

The psalm recited on the first day of *chol hamoed* is Psalm 29 — *Havu L'Hashem Bnei Eilim*. Its connection to *Sukkot* is transparent: *Kol Hashem Al Hamayim Keil Hakavod Hir'im Hashem Al Mayim Rabim* — the voice of God over the waters, God thundering over the mighty waters. The theme of water is a central motif of *Sukkot*, the time when the world is judged concerning rainfall.

### Day 2: V'Larasha

On the second day they would recite *V'larasha Amar Elokim* — the opening of Psalm 50. The full psalm is worth examining. It opens with a divine summons: *Keil Elokim Hashem diber vayikra aretz mimizrach shemesh ad mevo'o* — God, the Lord, spoke and called the earth from the rising to the setting of the sun. It proceeds to declare that God does not need Israel's sacrifices on their own account — *Lo al zvachecha ochichecha ve'olotecha lenegdi tamid* — yet it simultaneously demands that one fulfill vows: *Zvach L'Elokim todah veshalem L'Elyon nedarecha* — offer thanksgiving to God and pay your vows to the Most High.

*Rashi* focuses on the verse about paying vows. The time of the *Regalim* was the deadline by which one was obligated to fulfill vows made during the year; allowing multiple *Regalim* to pass without fulfillment transgresses the prohibition of *bal te'acher* — delaying the discharge of one's vows. So *Rashi* explains that this psalm emphasizes the obligation of presenting one's outstanding vows upon arriving at the Temple. One could alternatively read the emphasis as falling on the broader ethical and spiritual dimension of the psalm — that God cares more about genuine religious conduct than about the mechanical offering of sacrifices. In either reading, the connection to *Sukkot* specifically is indirect; it is more generally about arriving at the Temple and fulfilling one's obligations.

### Day 3: Mi Yakum

On the third day: *Mi Yakum Li Im Mere'im* — Psalm 94. This psalm opens with a call for divine vengeance — *Keil Nekamot Hashem Keil Nekamot Hofia, Hinasei Shofet Haaretz Hashev Gmul Al Ge'im* — God of retribution, appear; rise up, O Judge of the earth, render recompense to the arrogant. It then addresses the suffering of Israel under foreign oppression: *Amcha Hashem Yedake'u Venachalatcha Ye'anu, Almana Veger Yaharogu Vitomim Yeratzachu* — Your people, O Lord, they crush; Your heritage they oppress; they slay the widow and the stranger and murder the orphans. It asserts divine awareness: *Hashoteil Ozen Halo Yishma Im Yotzer Ayin Halo Yabit* — does the One who implanted the ear not hear? Does the One who formed the eye not see? And it culminates with the blessing of the one whom God corrects: *Ashrei Hagever Asher Teyasrenu Kah Umitoratcha Telamdenu* — happy is the man whom You discipline, O Lord, and whom You instruct from Your Torah.

*Rashi* connects this psalm to *Sukkot* in two ways. First, it alludes to the oppression of Israel under foreign powers — the Greeks and Romans — during the era of the Temple and between the two Temples; the *Sukkot* period is one in which Israel nevertheless persisted and was sustained. Second, the declaration that God knows what lies in one's heart — *Binu Boarim Ba'am Uchsilim Matay Tashkilu* — addresses those who might think they can evade their agricultural obligations at this season: the giving of tithes, the gifts to the poor, *leket*, *shichcha*, and *pe'ah*, all of which reach their culmination at harvest time. The message is that God sees and knows, and one cannot escape the consequences of failing to fulfill these duties. Admittedly, as *Rashi* concedes, the direct connection to *Sukkot* in these middle psalms is fairly general.

### Day 4: Binu Bo'arim

On the fourth day: *Binu Boarim Ba'am* — this is the second half of Psalm 94, returning to the theme that God is aware of what is in one's heart. *Rashi* applies this again to the concern about performing the agricultural obligations at harvest time, when people might presume that their compliance or non-compliance goes unobserved. Those who think they can neglect the gifts to the poor — God knows, and they should understand this. It is worth noting that the fourth day's psalm is thus a continuation of the third day's, rather than an entirely independent selection; the two halves of Psalm 94 are distributed across consecutive days, with the first half assigned to the third day and this second half to the fourth.

### Day 5: Hasiroti

On the fifth day: *Hasiroti missevel shikmo* — Psalm 81. This psalm is far more transparently connected to the themes of *Sukkot*. It opens: *Harninu L'Elohim uzeinu hari'u L'Elohei Yaakov* — sing joyfully to God our strength, shout to the God of Jacob. It then continues: *Se'u zimrah utnu tof kinnor na'im im navel tiku vachodesh shofar bakesseh l'yom hagenu* — raise a song, beat the drum, the sweet harp with the lute, sound the *shofar* at the new moon, at the full moon on our festival day. This verse encompasses all the musical instruments of the *Simchat Beit HaShoeva*, and the phrase *bakesseh l'yom hagenu* alludes famously to *Rosh Hashanah*. The psalm also recalls the exodus: *hama'alcha me'eretz Mitzrayim* — I brought you up from the land of Egypt — an explicit reference to *Yetziat Mitzrayim*. And it declares: *Harchav picha va'amaleihu* — open your mouth wide and I will fill it — a promise of sustenance and future blessing. *Rashi* states explicitly that this verse is about water and rain: you open your mouth and God will give you blessing. The connection to *Sukkot* and the prayer for rain is direct and manifest.

### Day 6: Yamutu

On the sixth day: *Yamutu kol mosdei aretz* — Psalm 82. The psalm opens with divine judgment in the heavenly court: *Bekerev Elohim yishpot* — God stands in the divine assembly. It then turns to a call for justice: *Shiftu dal veyatom ani varash hatzdiku, Paletu dal ve'evyon miyad resha'im hatzilu* — render justice to the poor and orphan, vindicate the afflicted and impoverished, rescue the poor and needy from the hands of the wicked. It concludes with the unsettling observation that despite their grandeur, even those who have been called *Elohim* will die like humans: *achen ke'adam temutun uke'achad hasarim tipolu* — yet you shall die like men and fall like any prince. The concern for the poor and vulnerable — *ani varash hatzdiku* — connects directly to the obligations of giving *terumot* and *ma'asrot*, *leket*, *shichcha*, and *pe'ah* that characterize this season of ingathering.

### Shabbat Pushes Off

*Ve'im chal Shabbat be'echad mehem* — if *Shabbat* falls on one of the days of *chol hamoed*, the *Shabbat* psalm takes precedence over the *chol hamoed* psalm for that day. This should be distinguished from the earlier ruling regarding *Rosh Chodesh*: there, *Rosh Chodesh* takes precedence over *Shabbat* in the choice of *shir shel yom*, because it is important to announce the *Rosh Chodesh*. Between *Shabbat* and *chol hamoed Sukkot*, however, *Shabbat* takes precedence.

When *Shabbat* displaces one of the six *chol hamoed* psalms, the result is a domino effect: *yamutu yidcheh* — the last psalm, *yamutu*, is the one that gets pushed off entirely. So if *Shabbat* falls on day three, the psalm that would have been said on day three is said on day four, day four's psalm is said on day five, and so on — and the psalm of the sixth day, *yamutu*, is never recited that year. The name *yamutu* — "they will be moved" — lends itself to the slight wordplay of being the one that gets "moved away."

The significance of this ruling for the larger argument should not be missed: the fact that one must choose which psalm to displace confirms that on any given day — even one with multiple *musafim*, such as *Shabbat chol hamoed* — there is only one *shir shel yom* and only one set of trumpet blasts. If there were a separate set for each *musaf*, you would recite both psalms and no displacement would be necessary.

## Tiyuvta d'Rav Acha



### Rav Acha's Position

*Tiyuvta de-Rav Acha bar Chanina tiyuvta* — this *baraita* constitutes another refutation of *Rav Acha bar Chanina*'s position. If multiple *musafim* each generated their own independent *shir shel yom* and set of blasts, then on *Shabbat chol hamoed* one would simply recite all of them; the domino effect and the displacement of *yamutu* would never arise. The very fact that the *baraita* describes displacement proves that even a day with multiple *musafim* receives only one *shir shel yom* and one set of trumpet blasts.

The *Gemara* asks: *Ve-Rav Acha bar Chanina kra masnisa kama*? — but *Rav Acha bar Chanina* had a verse and a *baraita* of his own. The verse states *B'nei Aharon ha-Kohanim yitke'u ba-chatzotzrot*, and there was a *baraita* indicating that the trumpet blasts correspond to the *musafim* — which sounds as though a multiplication of *musafim* generates a multiplication of blasts. If we conclude that only one set of blasts and one *shir* is performed, how do we account for that *baraita*?

## Shir Shel Yom: Intermed.



### Mnemonics: Humbahi

*Rav Safra manach be-simani* — *Rav Safra* provided a mnemonic to remember the order of the six psalms recited during *chol hamoed Sukkot*: *Humbahi*. Each letter stands for the opening word of one of the six psalms in order: *Havu l'Hashem* (Psalm 29), *U'lerasha amar* (Psalm 50), *Mi yakum li* (Psalm 94), *Binu bo'arim* (the second half of Psalm 94), *Hasiroti* (Psalm 81), *Yamutu* (Psalm 82). This is the *Humbahi* sequence of *Rav Safra*, where *beis* precedes *heh* — that is, *Binu* comes before *Hasiroti*.

*Rav Pappa manach be-simana* — *Rav Pappa* had a slightly different mnemonic: *Humbabi*. In *Rav Pappa*'s ordering, the *heh* and the second *beis* are reversed — *Hasiroti* comes before *Binu bo'arim*. The sequence sounds like Hammurabi: *Humbabi*.

*Ve-simanch* — how do you remember which mnemonic belongs to which *Amora*? *Ambuha de-sifrei* — the crowds that gather at the schoolteachers at drop-off and pick-up time. *Sifrei* resonates with *Safra*, and *Safra* said *Humbahi* — with the *heh* (*Hasiroti*) at the end — as opposed to *Rav Pappa*'s *Humbabi*. The crowd (*ambuha*) of the schoolteachers (*sifrei*) reminds you that it is *Rav Safra* who has the *Humbahi* order.

## Tiyuvta d'Rav Acha (II)



### Ravina: Length Not Qty

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבִינָא: לוֹמַר שֶׁמַּאֲרִיכִין בִּתְקִיעוֹת. רַבָּנַן דְּקֵיסָרִי מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי אַחָא אָמְרִי: לוֹמַר שֶׁמַּרְבֶּה בְּתוֹקְעִין.
> **Translation:** **Ravina said:** The verse and the *baraita* that he cited do not teach that trumpet blasts were sounded for each additional offering. Rather, they come **to say that one extends** the duration **of the blasts** to honor the added additional offerings, but does not sound even one additional blast. **The Sages of Caesarea, in the name of Rabbi Aḥa, said:** The verse and the *baraita* cited by Rabbi Aḥa come **to say that one increases** the number of **trumpeters** to honor the added additional offerings, but not the number of blasts sounded.

*Amar Ravina* — *Ravina* resolved the difficulty: *lomar she-ma'arichim bi-tkiyot* — what the *baraita* means when it says the trumpet blasts correspond to the *musafim* is not that you perform an additional set of blasts for each *musaf*, but that when you perform the single set of blasts you play the notes longer to signal that it is a doubled day. The quantity of sets remains one; only the duration increases.

### More Blowers, One Set

*Rabbanan de-Keisari mishmei de-Rav Acha amri* — the rabbis of *Keisari* said in the name of *Rav Acha*: *lomar she-marbin be-tokim* — you add more players, more people sounding the trumpet. Rather than performing another complete set of blasts, you increase the number of those who blow, while still performing only a single unified set. The identity of the day remains singular; what multiplies is the grandeur of execution, not the number of performances.

## Safek Yom: Which Musaf



### Two-Day Yom Tov Problem

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַאֲנַן, דְּאִית לַן תְּרֵי יוֹמֵי, הֵיכִי עָבְדִינַן? אַבָּיֵי אָמַר: שֵׁנִי יִדָּחֶה.
> **Translation:** Apropos the psalms recited during the Festival, the Gemara asks: **And we,** outside Eretz Yisrael, **who have two days** of Festival due to the uncertainty, as well as uncertainty with regard to each of the intermediate days, **how do we conduct** ourselves with regard to the mention of the additional offerings in the additional prayer of the Festival during the intermediate days, and with regard to Torah reading on those days? **Abaye said:** Mention of **the second** day **will be superseded.** Since the first of the intermediate days outside Eretz Yisrael is the third day of the Festival in Eretz Yisrael, the additional offering for the third day alone is mentioned, and the offerings for the fourth day on the fourth day, etc. No mention is made of the second day outside Eretz Yisrael.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רָבָא אָמַר: שְׁבִיעִי יִדָּחֶה. תַּנְיָא כְּוָתֵיהּ דְּרָבָא: אִם חָל שַׁבָּת לִהְיוֹת בְּאֶחָד מֵהֶן, ״יִמּוֹטוּ״ יִדָּחֶה.
> **Translation:** **Rava said:** Mention of the **seventh** day **will be superseded.** On the first of the intermediate days outside Eretz Yisrael, the third day of the Festival, the passage beginning “And on the second day” (Numbers 29:17) is mentioned in the additional *Amida* prayer and read in the Torah, and on each succeeding day the succeeding passage is mentioned and read. There is no mention of the seventh day on the eighth day, as that is no longer *Sukkot* but rather the Eighth Day of Assembly. The Gemara notes: A *baraita* **was taught in accordance with** the opinion of **Rava: And if Shabbat occurs on any** of the intermediate days of the Festival, the song of the seventh day of *Sukkot*: “All the foundations of the earth **are moved,” is superseded.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַתְקִין אַמֵּימָר בִּנְהַרְדְּעָא, דִּמְדַלְּגִי דַּלּוֹגֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites a third opinion: **Ameimar instituted in** his city of **Neharde’a that** during the intermediate days, **one repeats** the second of the additional offerings that he mentioned the day before and adds the additional offerings of the subsequent day. On the first of the intermediate days in the Diaspora, one mentions the additional offerings of both the second and third days of *Sukkot*. On the second of the intermediate days, one repeats the verses of the third day of *Sukkot* and adds the verses of the fourth day.

Having settled that multiple *musafim* on the same day yield only one *shir shel yom* and one set of blasts, the *Gemara* raises an analogous problem by analogy: *Va-anan de-it lan trei yomei heichi avidnan* — we who observe two days of *Yom Tov*, what do we do?

The question is not quite identical to the *Shabbat*-plus-*chol hamoed* case. Here the doubling is of a different kind. The specific question concerns the *musaf* prayer of *Shemoneh Esreh* on *chol hamoed Sukkot* in a diaspora community that observes two days of *Yom Tov*. When we arrive at the first day of *chol hamoed*, it is simultaneously day two of *Sukkot* by the true reckoning — and yet by virtue of *sfeika de-yoma* it is also treated as a potential day one of *Sukkot* (since the second day of *Yom Tov* was observed as a doubtful day). The *musaf* prayer on *chol hamoed* contains verses from the Torah enumerating the special offerings for each day of the festival, and each day's verses are different. Which day's verses do we recite in the *musaf Shemoneh Esreh*?

### Abaye: Sheni Yidachei

*Abaye amar sheni yidachei* — *Abaye* says: push off the second day of *Yom Tov*. That is to say, since we know that the second day of *Yom Tov* was observed only because of *sfeika de-yoma*, we return now to the true reckoning. Accordingly, the first day of *chol hamoed* is treated as day three of *Sukkot*, and we recite the verses of day three. From that point the days proceed as three, four, five, six, seven — the normal true count. Day two is simply set aside.

### Rava: Shvi'i Yidachei

*Rava amar shevi'i yidachei* — *Rava* disagrees. *Rava* maintains that we continue from where we are: having treated the second day of *Yom Tov* as a genuine day, we now act as though we are at the beginning of *chol hamoed*, and the first day of *chol hamoed* is treated as day two of *Sukkot*. From there the days proceed two, three, four, five, six — and day seven is never reached in the *musaf* recitation. It simply falls off.

*Abaye*'s position has a certain intuitive logic: it restores alignment with the true count. *Rava*'s position, by contrast, maintains continuity from where we have been and accepts the consequence that the seventh day's verses are never said. The *Gemara* notes that undermining the status of the second day of *Yom Tov* — as *Abaye* might seem to do — is itself a concern.

### Baraita Backs Rava

*Tanya k'vatiei d'Rava* — a *baraita* is adduced in support of *Rava*: *im chal Shabbat be'echad meihen, yamotei yidacheh* — if *Shabbat* falls on one of the intermediate days of *chol hamoed*, the last psalm, *yamutu*, is pushed off. The *Gemara* understands this as analogous to *Rava*'s ruling: just as *Shabbat* entering on a day of *chol hamoed* pushes everything down the line until the last psalm falls off, so too the second day of *Yom Tov* pushes the count forward and the seventh day falls off the edge. One might argue the two cases operate on different logic, but the *Gemara* sees them as analogous.

### Ameimar: Both Days

*Atkin Ameimar b'Nehardea d'medalgei dilugei* — *Ameimar* of *Nehardea* instituted a practice of jumping back and forth. Rather than choosing one day's identity and forfeiting the other, on the first day of *chol hamoed* one recites both the verses of day two and the verses of day three — and similarly throughout. This is in fact what we do in practice today.

### Rashi: Torah Reading

*Rashi* addresses a parallel question regarding the Torah reading on *chol hamoed* under two-day *Yom Tov* conditions. *Rashi* rules that on the fourth portion (*revii*) of the Torah reading on the first day of *chol hamoed*, one reads from both the second and third day's sections. For the first three portions, *Rashi* says one reads from the second, third, and fourth day's sections in sequence, then returns to read the second and third. The result for the full reading is: second, third, fourth, and then second and third again.

### Tosafot: Consistency

*Tosafot* takes issue with *Rashi*'s approach on grounds of inconsistency. According to *Rashi*, the reading would be two, three, four, two, three. *Tosafot* prefers a consistent pattern: two, three, two, three throughout. One might ask what we actually do on *Hoshana Rabbah*: there, the reading goes through the very end of the festival — five, six, seven, and then back to six and seven — and cannot go to eight since that would encroach on *Shemini Atzeret*. *Tosafot*'s concern is that *Rashi*'s approach lacks consistency, since the *musaf* recitation and the Torah reading are not identical in their treatment. It is worth noting, however, that the *musaf* does align with the *revii* — the final *kria* of the Torah reading — even if the two tracks are not fully parallel with one another.

## First Day Korbanot



### 13 Bulls, 2 Rams, 1 Goat

The *masechta* now turns to a new *Mishnah* at the top of 55b. *Yom Tov Harishon Shel Chag hayu sham yud-gimmel parim eilim shnayim v'sa'ir echad* — on the first *Yom Tov* of the festival there were thirteen bulls, two rams, and one goat. The goat was the *chatat* offering; the remainder were *olot*. This is drawn directly from the Torah's enumeration in Numbers.

### Sheep Distribution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ יוֹם טוֹב הָרִאשׁוֹן שֶׁל חַג הָיוּ שָׁם שְׁלֹשָׁה עָשָׂר פָּרִים, אֵילִים שְׁנַיִם וְשָׂעִיר אֶחָד. נִשְׁתַּיְּירוּ שָׁם אַרְבָּעָה עָשָׂר כְּבָשִׂים לִשְׁמוֹנֶה מִשְׁמָרוֹת. בְּיוֹם רִאשׁוֹן, שִׁשָּׁה מַקְרִיבִין שְׁנַיִם שְׁנַיִם. וְהַשְּׁאָר, אֶחָד אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: On **the first Festival** day **of *Sukkot* there were thirteen bulls, two rams, and one goat there.** The mishna proceeds to discuss the division of labor for the Festival offerings among the twenty-four priestly watches, all of which serve in the Temple on the pilgrimage Festivals. The sixteen offerings mentioned above were divided among sixteen priestly watches, one offering per watch. **Fourteen sheep remained to** be divided among the **eight** remaining **watches. On the first day** of the Festival, **six** of the eight remaining watches **sacrifice two** sheep **each** for a total of twelve, **and the remaining** two watches sacrifice **one** sheep **each.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בַּשֵּׁנִי, חֲמִשָּׁה מַקְרִיבִין שְׁנַיִם שְׁנַיִם. וְהַשְּׁאָר, אֶחָד אֶחָד. בַּשְּׁלִישִׁי, אַרְבָּעָה מַקְרִיבִין שְׁנַיִם שְׁנַיִם. וְהַשְּׁאָר, אֶחָד אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** **On the second** day of the Festival, i.e., the first day of the intermediate days, when twelve bulls were sacrificed, fifteen of the priestly watches sacrifice the bulls, rams, and goat, **five** of the remaining watches **sacrifice two** sheep **each, and the remaining** four watches sacrifice **one** sheep **each. On the third** day of the Festival, when eleven bulls were sacrificed, fourteen of the priestly watches sacrifice the bulls, rams, and goat, **four** of the remaining watches **sacrifice two** sheep **each, and the remaining** six watches sacrifice **one** sheep **each.**

*Nishtayru sham yud-dalet kvasim l'shmonah mishmarot* — in addition, there were fourteen sheep remaining to be distributed among eight of the twenty-four divisions (*mishmarot*) of priests.

The twenty-four *mishmarot* normally rotated on a weekly schedule, each division serving two weeks out of the year. On the *Regalim*, however, since all Israel was obligated to make the pilgrimage, all twenty-four divisions came together and served simultaneously in the Temple — a very crowded arrangement. The *musaf* animals for each day were distributed one per priestly division, so that each division had the privilege — and the practical benefit — of performing the service of at least one animal. (Beyond the service itself, the performing division would receive at minimum the hide of the animal they brought, since the flesh of an *olah* goes entirely on the altar.)

There were thirty animals on the first day: thirteen bulls, two rams, one goat, and fourteen sheep — totaling thirty. With twenty-four divisions, every division receives one animal and six animals remain. Therefore, six divisions would receive a second animal — two portions instead of one. *Byom rishon shishah makrivim shnayim shnayim v'hashar echad echad* — on the first day, six divisions bring two animals each, and the remainder each bring one.

### Daily Countdown

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בָּרְבִיעִי, שְׁלֹשָׁה מַקְרִיבִין שְׁנַיִם שְׁנַיִם. וְהַשְּׁאָר, אֶחָד אֶחָד. בַּחֲמִישִׁי, שְׁנַיִם מַקְרִיבִין שְׁנַיִם שְׁנַיִם. וְהַשְּׁאָר, אֶחָד אֶחָד. בַּשִּׁשִּׁי, אֶחָד מַקְרִיב שְׁנַיִם. וְהַשְּׁאָר, אֶחָד אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** **On the fourth** day of the Festival, when ten bulls were sacrificed, thirteen of the priestly watches sacrifice the bulls, rams, and goat, **three** of the remaining watches **sacrifice two** sheep **each, and the remaining** eight watches sacrifice **one** sheep **each. On the fifth** day, when nine bulls were sacrificed, twelve watches sacrifice the bulls, rams, and goat, **two** of the twelve remaining watches **sacrifice two** sheep **each, and the remaining** ten watches sacrifice **one** sheep **each. On the sixth** day, when eight bulls were sacrificed, eleven watches sacrifice the bulls, rams, and goat, **one** of the remaining watches **sacrifices two** sheep, **and the remaining** twelve watches sacrifice **one** sheep **each.**

As the days of *Sukkot* proceed, the number of bulls decreases by one each day: twelve, eleven, ten, and so on. Because there is one fewer animal to distribute each day, the number of divisions receiving a double portion likewise decreases by one each day. *Bashaini, hamishah makrivim shnayim shnayim v'hashar echad echad* — on the second day, five divisions receive double. *Bashlishi, arba'ah makrivim shnayim shnayim v'hashar echad echad* — on the third day, four divisions. *Barvi'i, shloshah makrivim shnayim shnayim v'hashar echad echad* — on the fourth day, three. *Bachamishi, shnayim makrivim shnayim shnayim v'hashar echad echad* — on the fifth day, two. *Bashishi, echad makriv shnayim shnayim v'hashar echad echad* — on the sixth day, only one division receives double.

The distribution is managed so that a division that received double on one day does not receive double the following day, and similarly a division that received a bull one day does not receive a bull the next day, since the bulls are the most coveted animals and there are not enough to go around for everyone to receive one repeatedly. One way to visualize this is as two concentric rotating wheels: one wheel tracking which divisions receive the bulls, advancing by the number of bulls distributed each day, and a separate inner wheel tracking which divisions receive the double sheep portion, advancing by the number of doubles each day. The two wheels rotate at different speeds, so the tracking of who receives a bull and who receives a double are independent of each other.

### Day 7: Perfect Split

*Bashvi'i, kulam shavim* — on the seventh day of *Sukkot*, the count works out perfectly. There are seven bulls, two rams, one goat, and fourteen sheep — totaling twenty-four — exactly one animal per division, with no doubles required whatsoever.

Over the seven days of *Sukkot*, the total number of bulls is 70 (13 + 12 + 11 + 10 + 9 + 8 + 7). Since 3 × 24 = 72, distributing bulls three per division over the seven days leaves two divisions that received only two bulls rather than three.

## Shemini Atzeret Lottery



### Rabbi: Fresh Start

*Bashmini* — on *Shemini Atzeret*, one bull is brought. The question is how to determine which division has the privilege of bringing it. *Neima matnisin Rabbi hi velo Rabbanan* — the *Gemara* asks: does our *Mishnah* reflect only the view of *Rabbi* and not that of the *Rabbanan*?

*De'tanya: Par ha-ba b'shemini techilah mafisin alav, Divrei Rabbi* — as taught in a *baraita*: for the bull brought on *Shemini Atzeret*, you begin anew with a completely fresh lottery, taking no account of anything that occurred during the seven days of *Sukkot*. This is the view of *Rabbi*.

### Chachamim: Remaining Two

*V'Chachamim omrim: achas mi-shtei mishmarot she-lo shilshu b'parim osa otah* — the *Chachamim* say: we give the privilege to one of the two divisions that did not manage to bring three bulls over the course of *Sukkot*. Those two divisions were shorted, and *Shemini Atzeret* provides the opportunity to remedy that imbalance.

### Compromise: Lottery

*Afilu teima Rabbanan* — the *Gemara* resolves that even according to the *Rabbanan*, our *Mishnah* can be consistent with their view. When the *Mishnah* says to start a fresh lottery on *Shemini Atzeret*, that does not necessarily mean a lottery among all twenty-four divisions. *Atu shtei mishmarot lav afusei ba'ei* — the two remaining divisions still need a lottery between themselves to determine which one gets the privilege. So the *Mishnah*'s language of conducting a lottery is not incompatible with the *Rabbanan*'s view that the lottery is limited to the two remaining divisions.

### Math: Two Left Out

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בַּשְּׁבִיעִי, כּוּלָּן שָׁוִין. בַּשְּׁמִינִי, חָזְרוּ לַפַּיִיס כְּבָרְגָלִים. אָמְרוּ: מִי שֶׁהִקְרִיב פָּרִים הַיּוֹם — לֹא יַקְרִיב לְמָחָר, אֶלָּא חוֹזְרִין חֲלִילָה.
> **Translation:** **On the seventh** day **they are all equal** and bring one offering each. **On the eighth** day, when there was a completely different configuration of offerings, **they returned to the** standard **lottery** system used to determine which of the priestly watches would sacrifice the offerings, **as** they did **on the other pilgrimage Festivals,** which do not have as many offerings as does *Sukkot*. **They said** about the ordering of the priestly watches: **One who sacrificed bulls today will not sacrifice** bulls **tomorrow; rather,** they will sacrifice one of the other types of offerings. **They rotate,** so that each of the watches will have the opportunity to sacrifice bulls as well as other animals.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ נֵימָא מַתְנִיתִין רַבִּי הִיא וְלָא רַבָּנַן? דְּתַנְיָא: פַּר הַבָּא בַּשְּׁמִינִי — בַּתְּחִילָּה מְפַיְּסִין עָלָיו, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: אַחַת מִשְׁתֵּי מִשְׁמָרוֹת דְּלֹא שִׁלְּשׁוּ בְּפָרִים, עוֹשָׂה אוֹתוֹ.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara suggests: **Let us say** that **the mishna** that says that on the eighth day they returned to the standard lottery of the other pilgrimage Festivals **is** according to **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **and not** according to **the Rabbis. As it is taught** in a *baraita*: To determine the priestly watch that would sacrifice **the bull that comes** as an additional offering **on the Eighth** Day of Assembly, **they draw lots over it from the beginning;** this is **the statement of Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi. **And the Rabbis say: One of the two watches that did not sacrifice three bulls** during *Sukkot* **sacrifices it.** Only two of the twenty-four watches sacrifices two bulls over the course of *Sukkot*. Each of the other watches sacrifices three bulls. Since the mishna mentions that a lottery was held, apparently the mishna is in accordance with the opinion of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא רַבָּנַן, אַטּוּ שְׁתֵּי מִשְׁמָרוֹת לָא אַפּוֹסֵי בָּעֵי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this suggestion: **Even** if **you say** that it is in accordance with the opinion of **the Rabbis, is that to say that the two** remaining **priestly watches are not required to draw lots** to determine which will merit sacrificing the bull?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּמַאן אָזְלָא הָא דְּתַנְיָא: כׇּל הַמִּשְׁמָרוֹת שׁוֹנוֹת וּמְשַׁלְּשׁוֹת, חוּץ מִשְּׁנֵי מִשְׁמָרוֹת שֶׁשּׁוֹנוֹת וְאֵין מְשַׁלְּשׁוֹת? נֵימָא רַבִּי וְלָא רַבָּנַן! אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא רַבָּנַן: מַאי לֹא שִׁלְּשׁוּ — בְּפָרֵי הַחַג.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **In accordance with whose** opinion **is that which is taught** in a *baraita*: **All of the priestly watches** sacrifice a bull **twice and three** times, **except for two watches that** sacrifice a bull **twice and do not** sacrifice a bull **three** times? **Let us say** that it is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **and not** in accordance with the opinion of **the Rabbis,** as they hold that one of those watches sacrifices a third bull on the Eighth Day of Assembly. The Gemara rejects this suggestion: **Even** if **you say** that it is in accordance with the opinion of **the Rabbis; what is** the meaning of the phrase: **They did not** sacrifice a bull **three** times? It means that **with regard to the bulls of the festival** of *Sukkot* they did not sacrifice a bull three times.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַאי קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן! הָא קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן: מִי שֶׁהִקְרִיב פָּרִים הַיּוֹם — לֹא יַקְרִיב לְמָחָר, אֶלָּא חוֹזְרִין חֲלִילָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: With regard to the bulls sacrificed on *Sukkot*, **what does** the *baraita* **teach us?** It is a simple calculation that seventy bulls divided by twenty-four watches leaves two watches that sacrificed only two bulls. The Gemara answers: **This** is what the *baraita* comes to **teach us: One who sacrificed bulls today will not sacrifice** bulls **tomorrow; rather, they rotate.** Therefore, each watch sacrifices at least two bulls, and most of them sacrifice three.

*K'man azla ha de'tanya: kol hamishmarot shonot u-meshaleshot, chutz mi-shtei mishmarot she-shonot ve-ein meshaleshot* — who does this *baraita* accord with? The *baraita* teaches: all the divisions received double and triple (in the distribution of bulls over *Sukkot*), except for two of them which received double but not triple. Does this *baraita* accord only with *Rabbi*?

It is worth pausing to see why the numbers work out this way. Over the seven days of *Sukkot* the bulls descend from thirteen on the first day down to seven on the last, yielding seventy bulls in total. With twenty-four divisions, each division receives three bulls — except for two, since three times twenty-four is seventy-two, leaving exactly two divisions one bull short. The point of starting a fresh lottery on *Shemini Atzeret* is precisely that those two divisions do not get priority the next day; the slate is wiped clean and nothing from the *Sukkot* distribution is carried forward.

*Afilu teima Rabbanan* — again the *Gemara* resolves: it could accord even with the *Rabbanan*. *Mai lo shilshu b'parei ha-chag* — when the *baraita* says they did not receive triple, it means among the bulls brought specifically on *Sukkot* — not counting the bull of *Shemini Atzeret*. On *Shemini Atzeret*, one of the two remaining divisions will receive a bull, bringing one of them to triple after all; but two divisions do not achieve triple from the *Sukkot* bulls alone. *U-mai kamashma lan? Ha kamashma lan: mi she-hikriv parei ha-yom lo yakriv le-machar ela chozer chalilah* — what is this *baraita* teaching us? It teaches that a division which brought a bull today does not bring a bull again tomorrow; the rotation continuously advances.

## 70 Bulls, 70 Nations



### Elazar: 70 Nations

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי (אֶלְעָזָר): הָנֵי שִׁבְעִים פָּרִים כְּנֶגֶד מִי — כְּנֶגֶד שִׁבְעִים אוּמּוֹת. פַּר יְחִידִי לָמָּה — כְּנֶגֶד אוּמָּה יְחִידָה.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Elazar said: These seventy bulls** that are sacrificed as additional offerings over the course of the seven days of *Sukkot*, **to what do they correspond? They correspond to the seventy nations** of the world, and are brought to atone for their sins and to hasten world peace. **Why** is **a single bull** sacrificed on the Eighth Day of Assembly? **It corresponds to the singular nation,** Israel.

*Amar Rabbi Elazar: haneh shiv'im parim k'neged mi?* — Rabbi Elazar asked: these seventy bulls, whom do they represent? *K'neged shiv'im umot* — they correspond to the seventy nations of the world. *Par yechidi lamah li? K'neged uma yechidah* — and what is the single bull of *Shemini Atzeret* for? It corresponds to the singular nation among the seventy — Israel.

### King's Parable

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָשָׁל לְמֶלֶךְ בָּשָׂר וָדָם שֶׁאָמַר לַעֲבָדָיו: עֲשׂוּ לִי סְעוּדָה גְּדוֹלָה. לְיוֹם אַחֲרוֹן אָמַר לְאוֹהֲבוֹ: עֲשֵׂה לִי סְעוּדָה קְטַנָּה כְּדֵי שֶׁאֵהָנֶה מִמְּךָ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites **a parable** about **a king of flesh and blood who said to his servants: Prepare me a great feast** that will last for several days. When the feast concluded, **on the last day, he said to his beloved** servant: **Prepare me a small feast so that I can derive pleasure from you** alone.

*Mashal le-melech basar va-dam she-amar la'avadav asu li se'udah gedolah, u-le-yom acharon amar le-o'havo asu li se'udah ktanah k'dei she-aneh mimcha* — it can be compared to a king of flesh and blood who said to his servants: make for me a great feast. Then, on the last day, he said to his beloved: make for me a small, private meal, so that I can have special, intimate time with you.

This *mashal* is famous well beyond its immediate context. It gives *Shemini Atzeret* its essential identity. After the vast, universalist festival of *Sukkot* — with its seventy bulls for seventy nations, its water libations, its *Simchat Beit HaShoeva* — there comes one quiet, private day for Israel alone with God. The transition out of the entire *Yamim Noraim* period is marked not by fanfare but by intimacy.

### Rashi: Universal Rain

*Rashi* makes the universalist dimension of *Sukkot* explicit. Commenting on the seventy bulls, he writes: *Pareh He-chag shivim hem chutz mi-shel shmini k'neged shivim umot l'chaper aleihem she-yeirdu g'shamim be-chol ha-olam le-fi she-nidonu be-chag al ha-mayim* — the bulls of the festival are seventy, excluding the one of *Shemini Atzeret*, and they correspond to the seventy nations, to atone for them so that rain should fall throughout the entire world, since the world is judged on *Sukkot* concerning water.

This is a striking and profound point. The *haftarot* of *Sukkot* speak of all the nations coming to celebrate *Chag HaSukkot*, and of rain being withheld from those that do not come. *Pesach* is fundamentally particularistic — about *Yetziat Mitzrayim*, about the birth of one people. *Sukkot*, even while it commemorates the clouds of glory that sheltered Israel in the wilderness — *Ki va-sukkot hoshavti* — is not primarily a historical celebration. It is about vulnerability, about dependence on God for sustenance and protection, and that reality is universal. Every nation needs rain. Every human being lives under the same sky. The universalist dimension of *Sukkot* is not incidental; it is inscribed into the very architecture of the *musaf* sacrifices.

### Yochanan: What Was Lost

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: אוֹי לָהֶם לַגּוֹיִים, שֶׁאִבְּדוּ — וְאֵין יוֹדְעִין מַה שֶּׁאִבְּדוּ. בִּזְמַן שֶׁבֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ קַיָּים, מִזְבֵּחַ מְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיהֶן. וְעַכְשָׁיו, מִי מְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיהֶן?!
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan said: Woe unto the nations** of the world **that lost** something **and do not know what they lost. When the Temple is standing, the** seventy bulls sacrificed on the **altar** during the festival of *Sukkot* **atones for them. And now** that the Temple is destroyed, **who atones for them?**

*Amar Rabbi Yochanan: Oy lahem le-ovdei kochavim she-avdu ve-ein yod'im mah avdu. Bizman she-Beit Ha-mikdash kayam mizbe'ach mechaper aleihem. Ve-achshav mi mechaper aleihem?* — Rabbi Yochanan said: woe to the nations of the world, for they have lost something without knowing what they have lost. When the Temple stood, the altar atoned for them. Now, who atones for them?

This is a striking *Gemara*. Israel knows what it has lost with the destruction of the Temple; we work to address that loss through Torah study and good deeds. But the nations benefited from the Temple's atonement without awareness, and without awareness there can be no response to its loss. The posture here is not hostility toward the nations — quite the opposite. It is an expression of genuine concern for the larger world, an extension of the universalist ethos that defines *Sukkot*.

## Three Yearly Equalities



### Shared Portions

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ בִּשְׁלֹשָׁה פְּרָקִים בַּשָּׁנָה הָיוּ כׇּל מִשְׁמָרוֹת שָׁווֹת: בְּאֵימוּרֵי הָרְגָלִים, וּבְחִילּוּק לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **At three times during the year, all** twenty-four **priestly watches** have **equal** status, in that all receive a share in the Temple service independent of the standard order of the watches and all receive a share in the accompanying gifts of the priesthood: **In the portions of the offerings of the Festivals** sacrificed on the altar **and in the distribution of the shewbread** on Shabbat during the Festivals.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בָּעֲצֶרֶת, אוֹמֵר לוֹ: הֵילָךְ מַצָּה, הֵילָךְ חָמֵץ. מִשְׁמָר שֶׁזְּמַנּוֹ קָבוּעַ, הוּא מַקְרִיב תְּמִידִין, נְדָרִים וּנְדָבוֹת, וּשְׁאָר קׇרְבְּנוֹת צִבּוּר — וּמַקְרִיב אֶת הַכֹּל.
> **Translation:** **On *Shavuot*** that coincides with Shabbat, when the two loaves offered on *Shavuot* would be distributed together with the distribution of the shewbread, the priest charged with the distribution **says to** each priest: **Here is *matza*** from the shewbread **for you,** and **here is leavened bread** from the two loaves **for you.** The principle is that **the priestly watch whose time is fixed** during the Festival **sacrifices** the **daily offerings** during the Festival, as well as vow-offerings, **free-will offerings, and all other communal offerings. And** that watch **sacrifices all of them** even during the Festival, when other aspects of the service are shared by all the watches.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ אֵימוּרֵי הָרְגָלִים שֶׁל גָּבוֹהַּ נִינְהוּ! אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא: מַה שֶּׁאָמוּר בָּרְגָלִים.
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara wonders: How is it that the priests divide those **portions of the Festival offerings?** Don’t **they** belong **to God,** and must be placed on the altar? How do the priestly watches share these portions? **Rav Ḥisda said:** The mishna is referring not to portions sacrificed on the altar [*eimurim*] but to **what was stated [*amur*] with regard to the pilgrimage Festivals,** all those portions of the offerings that the Torah commanded to sacrifice at the Temple that are not burnt on the altar and are shared by the priests, e.g., hides of the burnt-offerings of appearance and the breast and thigh of the Festival peace-offerings.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁכׇּל הַמִּשְׁמָרוֹת שָׁווֹת בְּאֵימוּרֵי הָרְגָלִים, תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וּבָא בְּכׇל אַוַּת נַפְשׁוֹ ... וְשֵׁרֵת״. יָכוֹל אַף בִּשְׁאָר יְמוֹת הַשָּׁנָה כֵּן — תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״מֵאַחַד שְׁעָרֶיךָ״, לֹא אָמַרְתִּי אֶלָּא בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁכׇּל יִשְׂרָאֵל נִכְנָסִין בְּשַׁעַר אֶחָד.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught: From where** is it derived **that all of the priestly watches** have **equal** status **in the portions of the Festival offerings? The verse states:** “And if a Levite comes from any of your gates out of all Israel, where he sojourns, **and comes with all the desire of his soul** unto the place that the Lord shall choose; **then he shall minister**…as all his brethren…They shall have like portions to eat, besides the transactions of their fathers’ houses” (Deuteronomy 18:6–8). Based on these verses, one **might** have thought that **this** is the case **even on the rest of the days of the year** and the priestly watches would have equal status. Therefore, **the verse states: From one of your gates. I said** that all have equal status **only when all of Israel enters through one gate,** i.e., on a pilgrimage Festival.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבְחִילּוּק לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים כּוּ׳. תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁכׇּל הַמִּשְׁמָרוֹת שָׁווֹת בְּחִילּוּק לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים —
> **Translation:** The mishna continues: **And in the distribution of the shewbread** on the three pilgrimage Festivals, all twenty-four priestly watches have equal status. **The Sages taught: From where** is it derived **that all of the priestly watches** have **equal** status **in the distribution of the shewbread?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״חֵלֶק כְּחֵלֶק יֹאכֵלוּ״, כְּחֵלֶק עֲבוֹדָה כָּךְ חֵלֶק אֲכִילָה. וּמַאי אֲכִילָה? אִילֵּימָא קׇרְבָּנוֹת, מֵהָתָם נָפְקָא: ״לַכֹּהֵן הַמַּקְרִיב אוֹתָהּ לוֹ תִהְיֶה״! אֶלָּא: לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים.
> **Translation:** **The verse states: “They shall have like portions to eat”** (Deuteronomy 18:8); just **as** all the watches receive an equal **portion of the service, so too** all the watches receive an equal **portion** in the **eating.** The Gemara asks: **What is** the **eating** mentioned in this verse? **If you say** it is the eating of **offerings,** the verse is superfluous, as it is **derived from there:** “And every meal-offering…**shall be the priest’s that offers it”** (Leviticus 7:9), which, although it was written with regard to meal-offerings, applies to all offerings. Moreover, it teaches that a priest who participates in the sacrifice of the offering shares in eating the offering. **Rather,** the verse is referring to **the shewbread** that was not part of the service this Shabbat, as it was baked the previous Shabbat.

The *masechta* now transitions to a new *Mishnah*: *Be-shloshah prakim ba-shanah hayu kol ha-mishmarot shavot be-imurei ha-regalim u-ve-chiluk lechem ha-panim* — at three times during the year, all twenty-four divisions of priests were equal in the special service of the pilgrimage festivals and in the division of the *lechem ha-panim*. The three times are the three *Regalim* — *Pesach*, *Shavuot*, and *Sukkot*.

The *Gemara* immediately notes a linguistic curiosity: *imurei ha-regalim* — "the inner fats of the festivals." But *emurim* — the inner fats — go entirely onto the altar and are not divided among the priests! *Amar Rav Chisda: mashe'amur biRegalim* — it means not the *emurim* themselves, but "the things said" or designated for the *Regalim*, i.e., the special festival offerings. It is an unusual way of expressing this, but the intent is clear: all divisions share equally in bringing the *musaf* offerings of the *Regalim* and in dividing the *lechem ha-panim*.

On *Shavuot* — which in Tannaitic usage is called *Atzeret* — one says to each priest: *Heilech matzah heilech chametz* — here is your *matzah* (a small portion from the *lechem ha-panim*, which is unleavened) and here is your *chametz* (a small portion from the *shtei ha-lechem*, the two loaves brought on *Shavuot*, which are leavened).

### Regular Mishmar Rights

*Mishmar she-zmano kavua hu makriv temidim nedarim u-nedavot* — the division whose regular week it is — the one that would have served that week in the normal rotation even aside from the *regel* — retains its exclusive claim to the regular daily offerings: the *temidim* (morning and evening), the free-will and vow offerings that private individuals bring (*nedarim* and *nedavot*), and other public offerings not unique to the *regel* itself. These are not shared with the other twenty-three divisions.

This exclusivity made the coincidence of one's regular week with a *regel* extraordinarily valuable. If all of Israel is making pilgrimage and presenting the vowed offerings and free-will gifts they have been accumulating throughout the year, the division whose week it happens to be stands to perform an enormous amount of service. Even if the special *regel korbanot* are shared, the tide of personal *korbanot* flowing in from all over *Klal Yisrael* — fulfilling *nedarim* before the *bal te'acher* deadline, offering *nedavot*, performing the *olat re'iya* — falls exclusively to the regular division. And while the flesh of an *olah* goes entirely to the altar, the hide belongs to the officiating priests. The practical windfall of having one's regular week coincide with a *regel* was substantial.

*Ve-sha'ar korbanot tzibbur u-makriv et ha-kol* — the *Mishnah* adds that the regular division also brings all other public *korbanot* not unique to the *regel*, with the *Gemara* to clarify what this includes.

### Devarim 18 Source

*Tanu Rabbanan: minayin shekol hamishmarot shavot be'emurei haRegalim?* — how do we know that all divisions are equal in the service of the *Regalim*? The source is *Devarim* 18: *ubah bekhol avat nafsho vesheret* — and he may come according to his heart's desire and serve. The verse addresses a Levite (understood by the Sages to refer to a priest in the context of Temple service) who arrives at the Temple outside of his assigned week. The simple reading of the verse would permit such service at any time. The Sages read *meAchad she'arekha* — "from one of your gates" — as limiting this permission to the moment when all Israel enters through a single gate, i.e., the *regel*, when everyone makes pilgrimage together.

### Gate Limitation

*Yachol af bish'ar yemot hashanah ken? Talmud lomar me'achad she'arekha* — one might think a priest could come and serve at any time of year, but the verse specifies one of your gates — *lo amarti ela besha'ah shekol Yisrael nikhnasin besha'ar echad* — this applies only when all Israel is entering through one gate together. So the entitlement to serve for all divisions equally is limited to the three *Regalim*.

### Lechem HaPanim Exception

The *Mishnah* also includes the *lechem ha-panim* in the equal division, and this requires its own derivation, since the *lechem ha-panim* is not unique to the *regel*. It is placed on the *shulchan* each week and divided among the serving division at the end of the week. The *regel* week's division has already performed the service of placing the *lechem ha-panim*, so there is no question of their entitlement to eat it. The question is: why do all twenty-four divisions share equally in the *lechem ha-panim* specifically during the *regel*?

*Tanu Rabbanan: minayin shekol hamishmarot shavot bikhiluk Lechem HaPanim?* — the source is the continuation of the verse in *Devarim* 18: *chelek kechelek yokhelu* — they shall eat portion as portion.

### Service Equals Eating

*Talmud lomar chelek kechelek yokhelu: kechelek avodah, kakh chelek akhilah* — just as there is an equal portion in the service, so too there is an equal portion in the eating. Once we establish that all divisions share equally in the *regel* service, it follows that they share equally in the eating as well. But this alone would only entitle them to eat the things they brought as *regel korbanot*. How do we derive that they eat the *lechem ha-panim*, for which they performed no service?

*U-mai akhilah? Ileima korbanot — meihasam nafka. Lakohen hamakriv oto lo tihyeh* — if the "eating" refers to the normal *korban* eating, that is already known from a different verse: the priest who brings a *korban* is entitled to its portions. So the additional eating derived from *chelek kechelek yokhelu* must refer to something for which no service was performed — namely, the *lechem ha-panim*. *Ella Lechem HaPanim* — it is specifically the *lechem ha-panim* that is the subject of this additional derivation.

### Regal-Related Only

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יָכוֹל אַף בְּחוֹבוֹת הַבָּאוֹת שֶׁלֹּא מֵחֲמַת הָרֶגֶל בָּרֶגֶל, תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״לְבַד מִמְכָּרָיו עַל הָאָבוֹת״ — מָה מָכְרוּ הָאָבוֹת זֶה לָזֶה, אֲנִי בְּשַׁבַּתִּי וְאַתָּה בְּשַׁבַּתֶּךָ.
> **Translation:** One **might** have thought that all the watches should be equal **even with regard to obligations that come not due to the Festival** but are brought **on the Festival** nevertheless, as there were many vow-offerings and free-will offerings brought to the Temple that were not part of the Festival rite, but simply the result of people taking advantage of their presence in Jerusalem to fulfill their outstanding obligations. Therefore, **the verse states: “Besides the transactions of their fathers’ houses”** (Deuteronomy 18:8). **What did the forefathers** of each watch **sell each other?** They agreed with regard to the service of the watches: **I** will serve **during my week, and you** will serve **during your week.** Each watch has the right to perform the Temple service during its appointed weeks and to receive all priestly gifts offered during those weeks.

*Yachol af bekhol baz shebo machamat haregel baregel?* — one might think that the equal entitlement in eating extends to absolutely everything that comes in by virtue of the *regel*, even things not specifically designated for the *regel* itself? *Talmud lomar levad mimkarav al ha'avot* — except for that which was sold to the fathers. This phrase refers to the ancestral rotation of divisions: *ani beshabbati v'atah beshabbatekha* — I serve in my week and you serve in yours. The things that belong to a division by virtue of their assigned week in the normal rotation — the *temidim*, the personal *nedarim* and *nedavot* — are not shared with the other divisions. Only what is unique to the *regel* itself is divided equally, with the one additional exception of the *lechem ha-panim*.

It is worth noting that the use of a verse in *Devarim* to establish the mechanics of priestly rotations is somewhat striking, since the system of twenty-four rotating *mishmarot* as formalized by David was not yet in place at the time of *Devarim*. The *Gemara* does not explore this point further here.
