[DAF:66a]
## Rabbah vs. Rav Yosef



### Ye'ush Acquisition

The daf opens in the midst of a *machlokes* between Rabbah and Rav Yosef on whether *ye'ush* — the despair or relinquishment of hope by the original owner — effects a *kinyan* for a *ganav*. Rav Yosef holds that *ye'ush* has no effect whatsoever: the *ganav* does not acquire the stolen object through the owner's despair alone. Rabbah, by contrast, holds that *ye'ush* does create a *kinyan* for the *ganav*. The discussion that preceded this point also touched on *shinui ma'aseh* and *shinui hashem* — physical transformation and change of name — as additional mechanisms that might, independently or in combination with *ye'ush*, effect an acquisition.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַב יוֹסֵף אָמַר: יֵאוּשׁ אֵינוֹ קוֹנֶה, וַאֲפִילּוּ מִדְּרַבָּנַן.
> **Translation:** **And Rav Yosef says: Despair** on the part of the owner over the possibility of recovering his item **does not** cause the thief to **acquire** that item at all, **and** this is the *halakha* **even by rabbinic law.**

[DAF:66b]
### Chametz over Pesach


> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵיתִיבֵיהּ רַב יוֹסֵף לְרַבָּה: גָּזַל חָמֵץ וְעָבַר עָלָיו הַפֶּסַח,
> **Translation:** **Rav Yosef raised an objection to Rabba** from a mishna (96b): If **one robbed** another of **leavened bread and Passover elapsed** in the meantime, so that deriving benefit from it is prohibited,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אוֹמֵר לוֹ: ״הֲרֵי שֶׁלְּךָ לְפָנֶיךָ״. וְהַאי, כֵּיוָן דִּמְטָא עִידָּן אִיסּוּרָא – וַדַּאי מִיָּיאַשׁ; וְאִי סָלְקָא דַעְתָּךְ יֵאוּשׁ קוֹנֶה, אַמַּאי אוֹמֵר לוֹ: ״הֲרֵי שֶׁלְּךָ לְפָנֶיךָ״? דְּמֵי מְעַלְּיָא בָּעֵי שַׁלּוֹמֵי לֵיהּ!
> **Translation:** the robber may nonetheless return the worthless bread to the owner and **say to him: That which is yours is before you,** and no compensation is required. Rav Yosef states his objection: **But** in **this** case, **once the time of** the **prohibition** of leavened bread **arrives,** the owner **certainly despairs** of recovering his now-worthless bread. **And if it enters your mind** to say that the owner’s **despair** causes the robber to **acquire** the stolen item, **why** can **he say to him: That which is yours is before you? He should be required to pay** the owner **full-fledged money** rather than return the worthless food, as the bread now belongs to the robber.

Rav Yosef, who holds *ye'ush* has no effect, raises a challenge against Rabbah, who holds that it does. The case is *gazal chametz v'avar alav ha-pesach* — someone stole another's *chametz* just before Pesach, and the holiday came and went while the *chametz* remained in the *ganav*'s possession. The relevant background is the rabbinic *knas* governing *chametz she'avar alav ha-pesach*: if one possessed *chametz* throughout Pesach without selling it, declaring it ownerless, or otherwise disposing of it, the *chametz* becomes completely worthless after the holiday — forbidden for consumption, sale, or any use. The penalty applies regardless of fault.

In this case, as Pesach approaches, the original owner despairs of recovering his stolen *chametz*. He does not want it back; even if it were returned at that moment, it would be of no use. There is *vadai ye'ush* — certain, unambiguous despair. Rav Yosef's challenge runs as follows: if *ye'ush* is *koneh*, then the *ganav* acquired the *chametz* through the owner's despair, and the *ganav* must therefore pay its value — he cannot simply return it with *harei shelcha lefanecha*, "here is yours before you." Yet in practice, the *ganav* can say *harei shelcha lefanecha* and discharge his obligation by returning the object. This suggests that *ye'ush* is not *koneh*, for if it were, the object would have become the *ganav*'s property and he would owe *damim* rather than being permitted to return the thing itself.

### Neither Wants It

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: כִּי קָאָמֵינָא אֲנָא – זֶה מִתְיָיאֵשׁ וְזֶה רוֹצֶה לִקְנוֹת; הַאי – זֶה מִתְיָיאֵשׁ וְזֶה אֵינוֹ רוֹצֶה לִקְנוֹת.
> **Translation:** Rava **said to** him in response: **When I say** that the thief acquires the stolen item upon the owner’s despair of recovery it is when **this** owner **despairs, and that** thief **wants to acquire** the item. In **this** case, **this** one **despairs but that** one **does not wish to acquire** the worthless bread.

Rabbah responds that this case is atypical and cannot serve as a challenge to his general position. In a normal case of theft, he explains, there are effectively two parties to the event: the *ganav* actively wants to possess the stolen item — that is why he stole it — and at some point the owner despairs of recovering it. The *ye'ush* of the owner combines with the desire of the *ganav* to complete the acquisition. The *chametz* case, however, is entirely different. Not only does the owner not want the *chametz* back — it would be useless to him — but the *ganav* himself does not want it either, since possessing *chametz she'avar alav ha-pesach* would render him in violation of a Torah prohibition. Neither party wants the object, and so the normal dynamic of *ye'ush* cannot operate. The case is therefore sui generis and provides no general proof against Rabbah's position.

### Stolen Korban

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵיתִיבֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי לְרַבָּה: ״קׇרְבָּנוֹ״ – וְלֹא הַגָּזוּל. הֵיכִי דָמֵי? אִילֵּימָא לִפְנֵי יֵאוּשׁ, לְמָה לִי קְרָא? פְּשִׁיטָא!
> **Translation:** **Abaye raised an objection to Rabba** from a *baraita*. The verse: “If his offering is a burnt-offering of the herd” (Leviticus 1:3), indicates that one’s offering must be **“his offering,” but not** an animal **stolen** from another. **What are the circumstances** of the case referred to by the *baraita*? **If we say** it is dealing with a stolen animal that the robber consecrates and sacrificed **before** the owner’s **despair** of recovery, **why do I** need **a verse** to teach this? It **is obvious** that this is disqualified, as one cannot even consecrate an animal that does not belong to him.

Abaye raises a separate challenge, this one from the laws of *korbanot*. The verse states *korbano v'lo ha-gazul* — one may only bring his own sacrifice, not a stolen one. The question is: what case does this verse address? If it speaks of a case *lifnei ye'ush* — before the owner has despaired — then it is entirely obvious that a stolen animal cannot be brought as a *korban*. Would God accept such a thing? No verse would be necessary to teach this. The verse must therefore be speaking of a case *achar ye'ush* — after the owner has already despaired. And yet even then, the verse teaches that the *korban* is invalid, implying that *ye'ush* does not effect a *kinyan* and the stolen animal remains the owner's — which is a direct challenge to Rabbah.

### God's Domain

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא לָאו לְאַחַר יֵאוּשׁ? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ יֵאוּשׁ לָא קָנֵי!
> **Translation:** **Rather, is it not** referring to one who seeks to consecrate and sacrifice a stolen animal **after** the owner’s **despair?** And yet the *baraita* teaches that the animal cannot be consecrated by the thief. **Conclude from** the *baraita* that the owner’s **despair** of recovering a stolen item **does not** cause the thief to **acquire** it, as if it belonged to him he would be able to consecrate and sacrifice it.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רָבָא: וְלִיטַעְמָיךְ, הָא דְּתַנְיָא: ״מִשְׁכָּבוֹ״ – וְלֹא הַגָּזוּל,
> **Translation:** **Rava said to** Abaye in refutation of this proof: **And according to your reasoning,** consider **that which is taught** in a *baraita*: It is written with regard to a *zav*, a man who experiences a gonorrhea-like discharge: “And whoever touches his bedding shall wash his clothes and bathe himself in water and be impure until the evening” (Leviticus 15:5). This indicates that bedding on which a *zav* lies can become ritually impure and impart impurity only if it is **“his bedding,” but not** if it is **stolen** bedding.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הֵיכִי דָמֵי? אִילֵּימָא דִּגְזַל עַמְרָא וְעַבְדֵיהּ מִשְׁכָּב, מִי אִיכָּא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר שִׁינּוּי מַעֲשֶׂה לָא קָנֵי? אֶלָּא מַאי אִית לָךְ לְמֵימַר – דִּגְזַל מִשְׁכָּב דְּחַבְרֵיהּ; הָכִי נָמֵי – דִּגְזַל קׇרְבָּן דְּחַבְרֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **What are the circumstances** of the case discussed in the *baraita*? **If we say that he robbed** another of **wool and made it into bedding, is there anyone who says that** a physical **change** that results from **an action** performed to a stolen item **does not** cause the thief to **acquire it? Rather, what have you to say?** The *baraita* must be speaking of a case in **which** the *zav* **robbed another** of **bedding. So too,** the previous *baraita* is dealing with one **who robbed another** of **an offering,** already consecrated. The reason this animal does not atone for the thief is that a consecrated animal cannot be stolen and become the thief’s property, as it belongs to the Temple treasury wherever it is located.

Rava responds on Rabbah's behalf. He points to a parallel formulation: *mishkavo v'lo ha-gazul* — the bed of a *zav* transmits impurity only if it is his own bed, not a stolen one. What case does *this* verse address? If the *ganav* took someone's raw wool and wove it into a blanket — a classic *shinui ma'aseh* — there is no dispute that it is now his; everyone agrees that a real physical transformation acquires the object for the thief. The verse cannot be addressing such a case. Rather, it must be addressing a case where someone stole an actual bed — no transformation at all — and then lay on it in a state of *tumah*. The verse teaches that this does not convey *tumah* to the bed, because the stolen object is not truly *his*. So too with *korbano v'lo ha-gazul*: the verse is not speaking about someone who stole an ordinary *chullin* animal and attempted to bring it as a *korban* after *ye'ush*, but rather about someone who stole a *korban* that was already consecrated and attempted to offer it. A consecrated animal is already, in a sense, in God's domain — *b'gaza deRachmana* — and the concept of *ye'ush* simply does not apply there. God does not relinquish ownership in the manner that a human owner does. These cases therefore tell us nothing about whether *ye'ush* is *koneh* in ordinary circumstances.

## Tumat Orot



### Thief's Machshavah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵיתִיבֵיהּ אַבָּיֵי לְרַב יוֹסֵף: עוֹרוֹת שֶׁל בַּעַל הַבַּיִת – מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתָן.
> **Translation:** **Abaye raised an objection to Rav Yosef** from a mishna (*Kelim* 26:8): With regard to **hides belonging to a homeowner,** one’s **thought renders them susceptible to** contracting **ritual impurity.** Hides and leather are susceptible to contracting impurity only if they are in a finished state. If a private individual uses a piece of hide or leather for a certain purpose, e.g., as a cot or a table top, and decides that this will be its fixed purpose, it is considered a finished product and is susceptible to contracting impurity.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְשֶׁל עַבְּדָן – אֵין מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתָן.
> **Translation:** **But** with regard to hides **belonging to a leatherworker, thought does not render them susceptible to ritual impurity.** Since this individual sells leather to others, when he uses a piece of leather for a household purpose and decides that this will be its fixed purpose, this is not considered a finished state, as he is likely to change his mind and sell the leather to one who will process it further and put it to a different use.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁל גַּנָּב – מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתָן, שֶׁל גַּזְלָן – אֵין מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתָן.
> **Translation:** The mishna continues: If the hides are those **of a thief,** who has stolen them from another, the thief’s **thought renders them susceptible to ritual impurity.** If they are those **of a robber** his **thought does not render them susceptible to ritual impurity,** because he is not considered the owner of the hide. The difference is that unlike the case of a thief, who steals items stealthily, the identity of a robber, who takes the item openly, is known to the owner, and the owner harbors hope of finding the robber and getting the item back. Consequently, he does not despair of recovering his property.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: חִילּוּף הַדְּבָרִים – שֶׁל גַּזְלָן מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתָן, וְשֶׁל גַּנָּב אֵין מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתָן; לְפִי שֶׁלֹּא נִתְיָיאֲשׁוּ הַבְּעָלִים.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Shimon says** that **the matters are reversed:** In the case **of a robber,** the robber’s **thought renders them susceptible to ritual impurity.** If the hides are those **of a thief, thought does not render them susceptible to ritual impurity, because the owners have not despaired** of recovering them and the thief has not acquired the hide. Rabbi Shimon’s reasoning is that a robber, who seizes items brazenly, is a more difficult criminal to apprehend and bring to justice than a thief.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּשְׁמַע מִינַּהּ, יֵאוּשׁ קֹנֶה! אֲמַר לֵיהּ: הָכָא בְּמַאי עָסְקִינַן – כְּגוֹן שֶׁקִּיצְּעָן.
> **Translation:** **And** although the two *tanna’im* of the mishna disagree as to which situation involves an owner’s despair of recovery, whether robbery or theft, in any event one can **conclude from** this mishna that the **despair** of the owner causes the thief or robber to **acquire** the item, as otherwise the perpetrator’s thoughts about the hide would have no effect on its status with regard to ritual impurity. Rav Yosef **said to** Abaye in response: **With what are we dealing here** in this mishna? It is a case **where** the thief or robber **trimmed** the leather by cutting it. Although trimming a piece of leather is not a sufficient change to constitute an act of acquisition by itself, it is effective in conjunction with the owner’s despair.

Having defended Rabbah's position against challenges, the Gemara now turns to challenge Rav Yosef, who holds that *ye'ush* is not *koneh*. Abaye raises a challenge from the laws of *tumah* as they apply to hides — *tumat orot*.

A raw piece of leather is not *mekabel tumah* in its unprocessed state. Whether it becomes susceptible to *tumah* depends on what the owner intends to use it for and how finished it is. The relevant *mishnah* teaches: *oros shel ba'al ha-bayit machshavah metam'asan* — the hides of a private householder: his *machshavah*, his intention, renders them susceptible to *tumah*. If he decides the hide is finished and ready for use, that thought is sufficient. *Veshel avdan ein machshavah metam'asan* — but the hides of a professional tanner: his *machshavah* does not suffice, because the final determination belongs to the customer who will use the hide, not the craftsman who processed it.

Then comes the critical ruling: *shel ganav machshavah metam'asan* — if a *ganav* stole a hide, his *machshavah* does render it susceptible to *tumah*. *Shel gazlan ein machshavah metam'asan* — but a *gazlan*'s *machshavah* does not. The reasoning offered is that a *gazlan* robs openly and is known; the original owner can identify him, pursue him, and may well recover the stolen property. The owner therefore has not despaired, and since *ye'ush* has not occurred, the property has not been acquired by the *gazlan*, and his *machshavah* carries no legal weight. The *ganav*, by contrast, steals secretly; the owner does not know who took the object, despairs of recovering it, and *ye'ush* has occurred.

Rabbi Shimon reverses the ruling: *shel gazlan machshavah metam'asan* — the *gazlan*'s *machshavah* does render it susceptible to *tumah*, because the owner sees how brazen and powerful the robber is and despairs immediately. *Veshel ganav ein machshavah metam'asan* — the *ganav*'s *machshavah* does not, because the owner has not yet despaired; he hopes the investigation will uncover the thief and the object will be recovered.

The challenge to Rav Yosef is clear: according to both the first *tanna* and Rabbi Shimon, *ye'ush* occurs for at least one of the two — *ganav* or *gazlan* — and in that case the thief's *machshavah* is effective. This presupposes that the thief has acquired the object through *ye'ush*, and his intention consequently carries legal force. But Rav Yosef maintains *ye'ush* is not *koneh* at all. *Shema mina ye'ush kaneh* — one ought to conclude from this *mishnah* that *ye'ush* is indeed *koneh*.

### No Trimming Needed

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְקֵיף לַהּ רַבָּה בַּר רַב חָנָן: וַהֲלֹא עִיצְבָּא שָׁנוּ כָּאן, וְעִיצְבָּא אֵין צְרִיכָה קִיצּוּעַ!
> **Translation:** **Rabba bar Rav Ḥanan objects to this** answer: **But** is it **not** the case that **they taught** this mishna **here** with regard to a leather table **top [*itzba*]? And** a leather table **top does not require trimming,** either due to the fact that its edges are not seen or because it is merely ornamental. Since it does not require trimming, this action should not be considered an act of acquisition at all.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּתְנַן: כׇּל מָקוֹם שֶׁאֵין חֶסְרוֹן מְלָאכָה – מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתוֹ, יֵשׁ חֶסְרוֹן מְלָאכָה – אֵין מַחְשָׁבָה מְטַמְּאָתוֹ; חוּץ מִן הָעֵיצְבָא.
> **Translation:** **As we learned** in the previous mishna (*Kelim* 26:7): **Wherever there is no work lacking** for an item to be considered finished, one’s **thought renders it susceptible to ritual impurity; when there is** still some **work lacking** for the item to be considered finished, **thought does not render it susceptible to ritual impurity, except for** the case of a leather table **top.** Although further work is required to smooth out the edges, one’s thought can render it susceptible to impurity because people do not care if this leather table top has uneven edges. It is with regard to this case of a table top that the mishna distinguishes between a homeowner and a leatherworker. Therefore, Rav Yosef’s explanation is rejected.

Rav Yosef attempts to deflect the challenge by arguing that the case in the *mishnah* is not one of pure *ye'ush* alone, but involves a *shinui ma'aseh* as well. Specifically, he suggests the case concerns *kitze'an* — the *ganav* trimmed the hide a little. The trimming constitutes a minor act of transformation; combined with *ye'ush*, this is what renders the acquisition effective and the *machshavah* operative.

Rabba bar Rav Chanan objects: the *mishnah* about the hides follows immediately upon a longer discussion involving a specific object, an *itzba* — a type of tray or mat. The earlier *mishnah* states the principle: *kol makom she-ein chisron melachah machshavah metam'asan* — wherever no further work remains to be done on the hide, *machshavah* alone renders it susceptible to *tumah*. *Yesh chisron melachah ein machshavah metam'asan* — if work remains to be done, *machshavah* is insufficient. The exception is an *itzba*, which does not require any further *melachah* even where a *chisron melachah* would otherwise be relevant.

The force of the objection is this: Rav Yosef proposed that the case involves trimming — a minor *shinui ma'aseh* — combined with *ye'ush*. But the preceding *mishnah* teaches that for an *itzba* specifically, no *melachah* at all is needed. The case of the *ganav* is drawn from this context, meaning no trimming is required. *Itzba lo tzericha kitzu'a* — the *itzba* needs no trimming. Therefore, *ye'ush* alone appears to be operative, and Rav Yosef's deflection fails.

### Shinui Hashem

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא: הַאי מִילְּתָא קְשִׁי בַּהּ רַבָּה לְרַב יוֹסֵף עֶשְׂרִין וְתַרְתֵּין שְׁנִין, וְלָא אִיפַּרְקָה עַד דְּיָתֵיב רַב יוֹסֵף בְּרֵישָׁא, וּפָרְקַהּ: שִׁינּוּי הַשֵּׁם – כְּשִׁינּוּי מַעֲשֶׂה דָּמֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests a different answer. **Rather, Rava said** that **this matter was a difficulty** posed by **Rabba to Rav Yosef for twenty-two years, and it was not resolved until Rav Yosef sat as the head** of the yeshiva **and resolved it** in the following manner: **A change in name** of an item, representing a change in status, **is similar to** a physical **change** brought about by **an action.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שִׁינּוּי מַעֲשֶׂה מַאי טַעְמָא – הָתָם מֵעִיקָּרָא עֵצִים, הַשְׁתָּא כֵּלִים; שִׁינּוּי הַשֵּׁם נָמֵי – מֵעִיקָּרָא קָרוּ לֵיהּ ״מַשְׁכָּא״, וְהַשְׁתָּא ״אַבַּרְזִין״.
> **Translation:** **What is the reason** that a physical **change** through **an action** causes the thief to acquire the item? It is because **there** the stolen items were **initially** just **wood,** and **now** they are **vessels.** The same can be said for **a change in name as well: Initially they called it a hide, and now** they call it a table **top [*abrazin*].**

Rava then reports a remarkable detail: this very difficulty had been pressing Rav Yosef for twenty-two years — *esrin ve-tartein shanin* — and he had no answer. Only when Rav Yosef became *Rosh Yeshiva* was he finally able to resolve it, suggesting, as the Gemara implies, that his elevated position brought with it a corresponding elevation in *siyata de-shamaya*.

The answer Rav Yosef ultimately gave is: *shinui hashem ke-shinui ma'aseh dami* — a change of name is legally equivalent to a physical change. The case of the *itzba* may require no trimming, but it does involve a *shinui hashem*. Before the *ganav* stole the hide, it was simply a piece of leather — *mashka*, as the Gemara puts it. Now, in the hands of the *ganav*, it is being used and designated as a tray or mat, and is called something different: *arveza*. This change of designation, even without any physical transformation, constitutes a recognized *shinui*. Combined with *ye'ush*, it is sufficient to render the *ganav* the owner and give his *machshavah* legal effect.

The distinction being drawn is precise. If the *ganav* stole a sheep and it is still the identical sheep two days later — same adult ram, same appearance, nothing done to it — there is neither *shinui ma'aseh* nor *shinui hashem*, and Rav Yosef would say *ye'ush* alone accomplishes nothing. But in the case of the hide, even without physical alteration, the object now bears a different name and serves a different function in the household context. That suffices to qualify as a *shinui*, and it is *ye'ush* together with this *shinui hashem* — not *ye'ush* in isolation — that accomplishes the acquisition. The challenge from the *itzba* therefore does not undermine Rav Yosef's core position, because that case was never a case of pure *ye'ush* to begin with.

## Reversible Shinui



### Stolen Beam Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַהֲרֵי מָרִישׁ – דְּאִיכָּא שִׁינּוּי הַשֵּׁם, דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא ״כְּשׁוּרָא״ וְהַשְׁתָּא ״טְלָלָא״; וּתְנַן: עַל הַמָּרִישׁ הַגָּזוּל שֶׁבְּנָאוֹ בְּבִירָה שֶׁנּוֹטֵל דָּמָיו, מִפְּנֵי תַּקָּנַת הַשָּׁבִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks a question with regard to changes in name: **But** what about the case of **a beam, where there is a change in name, as initially,** when it was stolen, it was called **a beam, and now,** after it has been placed in a building, it is called **a joist [*telala*], and** yet **we learned** in a mishna (*Gittin* 55a): **With regard to a stolen beam that** a robber **had built into** a large **building,** the court ruled **that** the owner **should** receive **its** value in **money** rather than demand that the robber demolish the house to return the beam itself, **because of an ordinance** instituted **for the penitent,** i.e., to encourage robbers to admit their crime and reimburse the owner of the stolen item.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** טַעְמָא מִפְּנֵי תַּקָּנַת הַשָּׁבִים,
> **Translation:** This mishna indicates that **the** only **reason** the robber does not have to return the beam itself is **because of** this **ordinance** instituted **for the penitent.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָא לָאו הָכִי – הָדַר בְּעֵינֵיהּ! אָמַר רַב יוֹסֵף: מָרִישׁ – שְׁמוֹ עָלָיו, דְּתַנְיָא: ״צַלְעוֹת הַבַּיִת״ – אֵלּוּ הַמַּלְטְטִין, ״וְהָעֻבִּים״ – אֵלּוּ הַמְּרִישׁוֹת.
> **Translation:** One can infer: **But** if were **not** for **this** decree, the beam would have to be **returned** to the owner **as is,** notwithstanding its change in name. **Rav Yosef said:** This is not a genuine change in name, as **a beam retains its name** even after it is inserted into a building. **As it is taught** in a *baraita* with regard to two obscure terms: The verse states: “And there were narrow windows and palm trees on the one side and on the other side, on the sides of the porch; there were also the *tzalot* of the house, and the *ubbim*” (Ezekiel 41:26). **“*Tzalot* of the house”; these are the casings [*hamaltetin*]. “And the *ubbim*”; these are the beams [*hamerishot*].** This shows that a beam can retain its name even after it has been built into a house.

Having established that *shinui hashem* functions like *shinui ma'aseh*, the Gemara probes the limits of this principle. If a *shinui hashem* is sufficient to effect acquisition together with *ye'ush*, one would expect it to work consistently. But there is a famous case that creates a difficulty — *mareish ha-gazul she-bano be-vira*: a stolen beam that one has built into the roof of one's house.

The Gemara notes that in this case, before Pesach even enters the picture, there is an apparent *shinui hashem*: what was previously a *keshura* — a beam in its raw state at the lumberyard — is now a *tafala*, a rafter integrated into the roof of a building. That is a change of name and function. And yet the well-known *takanah* — *takanat ha-shavim* — presupposes that absent this special enactment to facilitate repentance, the *ganav* would be required to demolish his house and return the actual beam. The *takanat ha-shavim* is needed precisely because the law would otherwise demand the return of the object itself; we relax that requirement only so as not to discourage *baalei teshuvah* from making restitution at all. This implies that *shinui hashem* alone did not transfer ownership to the *ganav* — the beam still belongs to the original owner — which stands in tension with Rav Yosef's answer that *shinui hashem* suffices.

Rav Yosef responds: the beam has not actually undergone a *shinui hashem*. A beam is still called a beam even when it is installed in a roof. The Gemara supports this from the book of Yechezkel, whose description of construction in the third *Beit HaMikdash* uses the term for beams — *marei shem* — even in the context of a completed structure. So the two by four that was at the lumberyard is still called a beam after it is placed in the roof. The name has not changed. Therefore, the beam case does not challenge the position that *shinui hashem* is effective, because in the beam case there is simply no *shinui hashem* to speak of. The case of the *itzba* and the tray, by contrast, does constitute a genuine change of name.

### Reversible Shinui

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי זֵירָא אָמַר: שִׁינּוּי הַחוֹזֵר לִבְרִיָּיתוֹ – בְּשִׁינּוּי הַשֵּׁם – לָא הָוֵי שִׁינּוּי.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Zeira said** a different answer: With regard to **a change in name, a change** in which the item **can revert to its original state is not** considered enough of **a change** for the thief to acquire the stolen item. Since the joist can be removed and revert to being called a beam, it is not considered a true change of name.

Rav Zeira offers an alternative resolution. Rather than arguing that the beam case involves no *shinui hashem* at all, Rav Zeira distinguishes between reversible and irreversible changes of name. The principle he articulates is: *shinui ha-chozer le-veryato be-shinui hashem lo havei shinui* — a *shinui hashem* that can revert to its original state is not a valid *shinui*. In the case of the beam, if the house were to collapse, the beam would simply be the same beam as before. Nothing was done to alter it permanently — it was not dyed, shaped, carved, or molded. It was simply placed in a supporting role. Since the *shinui* is reversible, it is not a legally cognizable *shinui*, and this is why the *takanat ha-shavim* is necessary: without it, the *ganav* would have to return the beam because he never truly acquired it through a reversible change of name. The case of the *itzba* or the tray that is genuinely repurposed and given a new designation, particularly where some work has been done to it, involves an irreversible *shinui hashem* and thus does qualify.

### Pipe Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְשִׁינּוּי הַשֵּׁם שֶׁאֵינוֹ חוֹזֵר לִבְרִיָּיתוֹ, מִי הָוֵי שִׁינּוּי? וַהֲרֵי צִינּוֹר – דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא ״קְצִיצְתָּא״ וְהַשְׁתָּא ״צִינּוֹרָא״, וְתַנְיָא: צִינּוֹר שֶׁחֲקָקוֹ וּלְבַסּוֹף קְבָעוֹ – פּוֹסֵל אֶת הַמִּקְוֶה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And is a change in name** in **which** the item **does not revert to its original state** really considered **a change? But there is** the case of a water **duct, where** it was **initially** called **a log [*ketzitzta*] and now** it is called **a duct, and it is taught** in a *baraita*: **A duct that one hollowed out and afterward attached** to the ground or to a building **invalidates a ritual bath** through the water it channels to the bath. The water in a ritual bath must be gathered directly from rain or a stream, not drawn with vessels. If one hollowed out a log and used it to channel water into the bath, this is considered drawn water, as he used a vessel.

The Gemara then tests Rav Zeira's formulation: if a reversible *shinui hashem* does not qualify, is it conversely true that an irreversible *shinui hashem* always does? The Gemara raises the case of a *tzinor* — a wooden pipe. If one takes a block of wood, hollows it out to make a pipe, and then attaches it to the ground so that rainwater flows through it into a pit serving as a *mikveh*, the pipe is *posel* — it invalidates the *mikveh* — because the water passes through a vessel that carries *torat keli*, the legal status of a vessel, and is thus considered *mayim she'uvim*, drawn water. However, if one first plants the block of wood into the ground and only afterward hollows it out, the resulting pipe does not invalidate the *mikveh*.

The question posed is: if an irreversible *shinui hashem* always effects acquisition — since a hollowed wooden pipe cannot simply un-hollow itself — then in the second case, where the block was first planted in the ground and then hollowed out, there has been an irreversible *shinui hashem* from block to pipe. Should this not also be *posel* the *mikveh*? Why does the order of operations matter?

### Kelli Status

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קְבָעוֹ וּלְבַסּוֹף חֲקָקוֹ – אֵינוֹ פּוֹסֵל אֶת הַמִּקְוֶה.
> **Translation:** By contrast, if **one attached it** first **and afterward hollowed it out, it does not invalidate the ritual bath.** Before the log was hollowed out, it was already attached to and considered part of the ground, and therefore the act of hollowing it out does not turn it into a vessel.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי אָמְרַתְּ שִׁינּוּי הַשֵּׁם מִילְּתָא הִיא, אֲפִילּוּ קְבָעוֹ וּלְבַסּוֹף חֲקָקוֹ נָמֵי לִיפְסֹל!
> **Translation:** The Gemara states its question: **And if you say that a change in name is** considered **a matter** of significance, i.e., it is considered a real change in the item, then **even if one attached** the log first **and afterward hollowed it out,** the ritual bath **should also be invalid.** Since hollowing out the log leads to a change in name, it should be considered a new vessel at that point.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁאנֵי שְׁאִיבָה, דְּמִדְּרַבָּנַן הִיא. אִי הָכִי, אֲפִילּוּ דְּרֵישָׁא נָמֵי! הָתָם אִיכָּא תּוֹרַת כְּלִי עָלָיו בְּתָלוּשׁ, הָכָא אֵין תּוֹרַת כְּלִי עָלָיו בְּתָלוּשׁ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: The *halakha* that **drawn** water may not be used for a ritual bath **is different, as it** applies **by rabbinic law,** and consequently the Sages were lenient in this case. The Gemara asks: **If so,** then **even in the first clause,** where the log was hollowed out before being attached, it should be permitted **as well.** The Gemara answers: There is a difference between the two cases. **There,** in the first clause, the log **had the status of a vessel while** it was still **detached. Here,** in the second case, it did **not have the status of a vessel while** it was **detached,** but only after it became attached to the ground, and therefore the Sages were lenient.

The response distinguishes the two cases not on the basis of *shinui hashem* but on the basis of *torat keli*. The prohibition of *mayim she'uvim* in a *mikveh* is itself rabbinic in origin, and even within that rabbinic framework, the determinative question is whether the object had the status of a *keli* at the relevant moment. In the first case — where the block was hollowed out first and then attached to the ground — before it was affixed to the ground it was already a *keli*, a completed vessel. Water flowing through a *keli* is *she'uvin*. In the second case — where the block was first planted into the ground and only then hollowed out — at the moment of affixing it to the ground, it was still just a block of wood, not a *keli*. It never had *torat keli* at the time it was attached, and therefore the water flowing through it is not considered *she'uvin*.

The upshot is that the *tzinor* case is resolved not by challenging or refining the principle of *shinui hashem*, but by reference to the independent legal category of *keli* status. The position of Rav Yosef remains intact: a case of pure *ye'ush* alone does not transfer ownership, but *ye'ush* combined with a genuine *shinui* — whether *shinui ma'aseh* or an irreversible *shinui hashem* — is effective. The challenges raised from these various cases have all been addressed, and the Gemara affirms that no one has yet mounted a successful challenge against the core of Rav Yosef's position.

## Consecration Cases



### Thief Consecrates

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵיתִיבִי: הַגַּנָּב וְהַגַּזְלָן וְהָאַנָּס – הֶקְדֵּישָׁן הֶקְדֵּשׁ, וּתְרוּמָתָן תְּרוּמָה, וּמַעְשְׂרוֹתָן מַעֲשֵׂר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara **raises an objection** against Rav Yosef’s opinion that the owner’s despair that he will recover his stolen items does not effect acquisition for the thief, from a *baraita*: With regard to **a thief, a robber, and one who forces** people to sell him items against their will, **their consecration** of the items they obtained in these manners is a valid **consecration; and the *teruma* that they separate** from the produce they obtained in these manners is valid ***teruma*; and the tithes that they separate** from those foods are valid **tithes.** Consecrations, *terumot*, and tithes must be performed by the owner of the item in question. Since there was no physical change in these items, this shows that the thief is considered the new owner merely by virtue of the owner’s despair of recovery.


The Gemara raises another potential challenge to Rav Yosef. The *mishnah* states: *ha-ganav ve-ha-gazlan ve-ha-annasin — hikdishan hekdesh, u-terumatan terumah, u-ma'aseratan ma'aser* — a thief, a robber, or one who takes by force: if they consecrate stolen property, it is *hekdesh*; if they separate *terumah* from it, it is valid *terumah*; if they separate *ma'aser*, it is valid *ma'aser*. Prima facie, this is a challenge to Rav Yosef: if *ye'ush* is not *koneh*, how can the *ganav* consecrate or separate *terumah* or *ma'aser* from property that is not his?

### Status Change

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרִי: הָתָם אִיכָּא שִׁינּוּי הַשֵּׁם – דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא ״טִיבְלָא״ וְהַשְׁתָּא ״תְּרוּמָה״, הֶקְדֵּשׁ מֵעִיקָּרָא ״חוּלִּין״ וְהַשְׁתָּא ״הֶקְדֵּשׁ״.
> **Translation:** The Sages **say,** in answer to this question: **There,** in those cases involving consecrations, *terumot*, and tithes, **there is a change in name** of the stolen item, **as initially** the stolen food was called **untithed produce,** i.e., produce from which *teruma* and tithes have not been separated, **and now** it is called ***teruma*** or tithe. With regard to **consecration,** it was **initially** called **non-sacred** property, **and now** it is called **consecrated** property.

Rav Yosef answers: these cases all involve a *shinui hashem*. When one steals *tevel* — untithed produce — and separates *terumah* from it, the *tevel* before separation is called *tevel*, and afterward it is called *terumah*. It is a different legal category entirely, governed by its own rules of sanctity and restriction. When one steals an ordinary *chullin* animal and consecrates it as a *korban*, the animal was *chullin* before and is *hekdesh* afterward — a genuine and irreversible *shinui hashem*. These are therefore not cases of pure *ye'ush*; they are cases of *ye'ush* combined with a *shinui hashem*. Rav Yosef's position remains that in a case of pure *ye'ush* without any *shinui* whatsoever, no acquisition takes place.

## Rav Yonatan Teachings



### Shinui Koneh

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹנָתָן: מִנַּיִין לְשִׁנּוּי שֶׁהוּא קוֹנֶה? שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְהֵשִׁיב אֶת הַגְּזֵלָה״ – מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״אֲשֶׁר גָּזָל״? אִם כְּעֵין שֶׁגָּזַל – יַחֲזִיר, וְאִם לָאו – דָּמִים בְּעָלְמָא בָּעֵי שַׁלּוֹמֵי.
> **Translation:** **Rav Ḥisda says** that **Rabbi Yonatan says: From where** is it derived **that a change** in a stolen item causes the thief to **acquire** it? **As it is stated: “And he shall return the stolen item that he took by robbery”** (Leviticus 5:23). **What** is the meaning when **the verse states** the seemingly superfluous phrase **“that he took by robbery”?** It teaches that only **if** the item is in the same state **as when he took it by robbery** it **he must return** it. **But if not, he is required to pay only money;** the stolen item remains his to keep.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַאי ״אֲשֶׁר גָּזָל״ מִיבְּעֵי לְמַעוֹטֵי גֶּזֶל אָבִיו, שֶׁאֵינוֹ מוֹסִיף חוֹמֶשׁ עַל גֶּזֶל אָבִיו!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks with regard to this derivation: **But this** phrase: **“That he took by robbery,” is required to exclude** the case of an item **stolen by one’s father,** i.e., **that** when a son returns an item or money that his father had stolen, **he does not add one-fifth to** the principal amount of that which **his father stole.** The passage in question addresses one who stole and took a false oath. This individual is obligated to add one-fifth to the amount he stole (see Leviticus 5:21–24). The apparently redundant phrase “that he took by robbery,” teaches that the requirement to add one-fifth applies only to the sinner himself, not to his heirs (see 104b).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם כֵּן, נִיכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא ״וְהֵשִׁיב אֶת גְּזֵילוֹ״, ״אֲשֶׁר גָּזָל״ לְמָה לִי לְמִכְתַּב? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ תַּרְתֵּי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **If so,** if the verse teaches only that *halakha*, **let the Merciful One write** merely: **And he shall return his stolen item,** as from that phrase alone it could be derived that an heir does not have to add one-fifth. **Why do I** need the Torah **to write** the expression **“that he took by robbery”?** One may therefore **conclude two** conclusions **from** this verse: A son need not add one-fifth, and a change in a stolen item effects acquisition for the thief.


The Gemara now presents two teachings transmitted in the name of Rav Chisda in the name of Rav Yonatan, which are striking because they are diametrically opposed — and both are derived from the very same verse.

The first teaching: *minayin le-shinui shehu koneh* — from where do we know that *shinui* effects acquisition for a thief? The source is the verse *ve-heshiv et ha-gezelah asher gazal* — he shall return the stolen object which he stole. The question is: what does the phrase *asher gazal* add? It would seem self-evident that one returns the stolen object that one stole rather than something else. The answer is that the verse is establishing a condition: only if the object remains in the state in which it was stolen — *ke-ein she-gazal* — must it be returned in kind. If not — *ve-im lav* — monetary compensation suffices. The verse is thus teaching that *shinui* creates a new legal reality; the object need not be returned because the *shinui* has transferred ownership, and the *nigzal* receives *damim* instead.

### Shinui Lo Koneh

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיכָּא דְּאָמְרִי, אָמַר רַב חִסְדָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹנָתָן: מִנַּיִין לְשִׁנּוּי שֶׁאֵינוֹ קוֹנֶה – שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְהֵשִׁיב אֶת הַגְּזֵילָה״ – מִכׇּל מָקוֹם. וְהָא כְּתִיב: ״אֲשֶׁר גָּזָל״! הָהוּא מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ – עַל גְּזֵילוֹ שֶׁלּוֹ מוֹסִיף חוֹמֶשׁ, וְאֵין מוֹסִיף חוֹמֶשׁ עַל גֶּזֶל אָבִיו.
> **Translation:** **And there are** those **who say** a different version of the above statement: **Rav Ḥisda says** that **Rabbi Yonatan says: From where** is it derived that **a change** in a stolen item **does not** cause the thief to **acquire** it? **As it is stated: “And he shall return the stolen item that he took by robbery”** (Leviticus 5:23), from which it may be inferred that he must return it **in any case,** even if it has undergone a change. The Gemara asks: **But it is written “that he took by robbery,”** which indicates that, on the contrary, the item need be returned only if it is in a similar state to when it was stolen. The Gemara answers: **That** phrase **is required** to teach another *halakha*: **One adds one-fifth upon** returning **his own stolen** item concerning which he had taken a false oath, **but he does not add one-fifth upon** returning something **stolen by his father.**

The second teaching: *minayin le-shinui she-eino koneh* — from where do we know that *shinui* does not effect acquisition? Also from the same verse: *ve-heshiv et ha-gezelah* — he returns *the* stolen object, in any condition, regardless of transformation. The *ganav* must always return the object itself because *shinui* carries no acquisitive power. The phrase *asher gazal* in this reading is not limiting the return to cases of unchanged objects; rather, it is the source for the law that the heirs of a thief who return stolen property on behalf of their deceased father are not required to add the *chomesh* — the one-fifth surcharge — that the father himself would have owed upon voluntary confession. The verse thus exclusively informs the *chomesh* law, leaving *ve-heshiv et ha-gezelah* to teach an unrestricted obligation to return the stolen object. As the Gemara notes, both sets of laws can be derived simultaneously from the two clauses of the verse, depending on which reading one adopts.

### Ye'ush Irrelevant

Ulla now advances an independent proof that *ye'ush* is not *koneh*. He derives it from the verse: *vahasatem gazul ve-et ha-pisach ve-et ha-choleh* — you bring before God that which is stolen, lame, and sick. God rejects all of these as unfit *korbanot*. Ulla argues that *gazul* must be understood *dumya de-pisach* — by analogy to *pisach*, the lame animal. A lame animal has no remedy; as long as it is lame, it cannot be brought as a *korban*, regardless of any circumstance. So too a stolen animal: it cannot be brought as a *korban* regardless of circumstances — *velo shna lifnei ye'ush velo shna le-achar ye'ush*. Whether *ye'ush* has occurred or not makes no difference. The stolen animal can never be offered, which implies that *ye'ush* does not transfer ownership.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר עוּלָּא: מִנַּיִין לְיֵאוּשׁ שֶׁאֵינוֹ קוֹנֶה? שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וַהֲבֵאתֶם גָּזוּל אֶת הַפִּסֵּחַ וְאֶת הַחוֹלֶה״ – גָּזוּל דּוּמְיָא דְּפִסֵּחַ, מָה פִּסֵּחַ דְּלֵית לֵיהּ תַּקַּנְתָּא כְּלָל,
> **Translation:** **Ulla says: From where** is it derived **that** the owner’s **despair** of recovering his stolen item **does not** cause the thief to **acquire** it? **As it is stated** in the criticism of the Jewish people for bringing inferior animals as offerings: **“And you have brought that which is stolen, and the lame, and the sick”** (Malachi 1:13). From the juxtaposition of these cases it may be derived that **a stolen** animal **is similar to a lame** animal: **Just as a lame** animal **has no rectification at all** with regard to its disqualification as an offering,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַף גָּזוּל דְּלֵית לֵיהּ תַּקַּנְתָּא; לָא שְׁנָא לִפְנֵי יֵאוּשׁ, וְלָא שְׁנָא אַחַר יֵאוּשׁ.
> **Translation:** **so too a stolen** animal **has no rectification** for its disqualification. There is **no difference whether** one is dealing with a stolen animal **before** the owner’s **despair** of recovering it, **and** there is **no difference** if it is **after** the owner’s **despair.** In either case, it is disqualified. This shows that the owner’s despair does not effect acquisition for the thief.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רָבָא אָמַר מֵהָכָא: ״קׇרְבָּנוֹ״ – וְלֹא הַגָּזוּל. אֵימַת? אִילֵּימָא לִפְנֵי יֵאוּשׁ – פְּשִׁיטָא, לְמָה לִי קְרָא?
> **Translation:** **Rava said:** This *halakha* may be derived **from here,** a *baraita*: The verse: “If his offering is a burnt-offering of the herd” (Leviticus 1:3), indicates that one’s offering must be **“his offering,” but not** an animal **stolen** from another. **When,** i.e., in which circumstances, is it necessary to teach this *halakha*? **If we say** it is dealing with a stolen animal that the robber consecrated and sacrificed **before** the owner’s **despair** of recovery, **why do I** need **a verse** to teach this? It **is obvious** that it is disqualified, as one cannot even consecrate an animal that does not belong to him.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא לָאו לְאַחַר יֵאוּשׁ – וּשְׁמַע מִינַּהּ יֵאוּשׁ לָא קָנֵי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rather, is it not** referring to one who seeks to consecrate and sacrifice a stolen animal **after** the owner’s **despair?** And yet the *baraita* teaches that the animal cannot be consecrated by the thief. **Conclude from** the *baraita* that the owner’s **despair** of recovering a stolen item **does not** cause the thief to **acquire** it, as if it belonged to him he would be able to consecrate and sacrifice it. The Gemara affirms: **Conclude from** the *baraita* that it is so.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא רָבָא הוּא דְּאָמַר דִּגְזַל קׇרְבָּן דְּחַבְרֵיהּ! אִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: הֲדַר בֵּיהּ, וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: חַד מִינַּיְיהוּ רַב פָּפָּא אַמְרַהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But Rava was the one who said** that this proof can be refuted, as the *baraita* can be interpreted as dealing with one **who robbed another of an offering** that was already consecrated. Rava apparently contradicts himself. The Gemara answers: **If you wish, say** that Rava **retracted** one of these two statements. **And if you wish, say** instead that **Rav Pappa,** not Rava, **said one of these** two statements.

### Rava Counter


Rava articulates a competing position from a different verse: *korbano v'lo ha-gazul* — his offering, but not a stolen one. The question is again what case the verse is addressing. If it speaks of a case before *ye'ush*, it is entirely obvious — no verse is needed. The verse must therefore be addressing a case after *ye'ush*. And from the fact that even after *ye'ush* the stolen animal cannot be offered, one derives: *shma mina ye'ush lo kani* — *ye'ush* does not effect a *kinyan*.

One might ask: did not Rava earlier explain *korbano v'lo ha-gazul* differently — as referring specifically to a case where the *ganav* stole a consecrated animal (*korban dechavreih*) rather than a *chullin* animal that he later tried to consecrate? The Gemara acknowledges this tension and resolves it either by saying that Rava retracted his earlier position — *hadar beih* — or that one of these two contrary opinions attributed to Rava was in fact stated by Rav Pappa. In any event, what the Gemara is establishing through these latter two teachings is a reinforcement of the view that *ye'ush* is not *koneh*.

## Arba'ah V'Chamishah



### Only Shor/Seh

The *mishnah* states that the law of multiple payments — *tashlumei arba'ah va-chamishah*, paying four or five times the value — applies exclusively to an *ox* and a *sheep*, and to no other animal. One who steals a donkey, a goat, or any other animal and slaughters or sells it does not incur these multiplied payments. The question is why the law is restricted in this way.

One might have thought to derive a broader principle by analogy to *Shabbat*. The verse in *Shabbat* mentions *shorcha va-chamorcha* — your ox and your donkey — but the *Shabbat* prohibition of labor extends to all animals. Similarly, the verse here mentions *shor o seh* — ox or sheep — and perhaps the law of multiple payments should extend to all animals by the same reasoning. Why is *arba'ah va-chamishah* restricted to these two?

### Mentioned Twice

Rabbah offers the textual basis for the restriction. The Torah writes *shor* and *seh* twice in the passage governing these payments. The verse begins: *ki yignov ish shor o seh* — if a man steals an ox or a sheep — and then concludes: *chamisha bakar yeshalem tachat ha-shor ve-arba tzon tachat ha-seh* — he shall pay five cattle in place of the ox and four sheep in place of the sheep. Both the beginning and the end of the *pasuk* specify *shor* and *seh*. *Shor va-seh in, midi acharina lo* — ox and sheep yes, anything else no. The doubling is legally significant; it functions as a limitation.

### Pasuk Analysis

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִדַּת תַּשְׁלוּמֵי אַרְבָּעָה וַחֲמִשָּׁה וְכוּ׳.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara returns to the mishna, which teaches: **But the principle of fourfold or fivefold payment** applies only to the theft of an ox or a sheep, as it is stated: “If a man steal an ox or a sheep, and slaughter it or sell it, he shall pay five oxen for an ox and four sheep for a sheep” (Exodus 21:37).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַמַּאי? נֵילַף ״שׁוֹר״–״שׁוֹר״ מִשַּׁבָּת – מָה לְהַלָּן חַיָּה וְעוֹף כַּיּוֹצֵא בָּהֶן, אַף כָּאן חַיָּה וָעוֹף כַּיּוֹצֵא בָּהֶן!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But why** is the fourfold and fivefold payment limited to oxen and sheep? **Let us derive** otherwise by a verbal analogy of the term **“ox”** in this verse and **“ox” from** a passage dealing with **Shabbat,** where it is stated: “And the seventh day is Sabbath to the Lord your God, you shall not perform any labor, you, your son, and your daughter, and your slave, and your maidservant, and your ox, and your donkey, and all your animals, and the gentile that is within your gates” (Deuteronomy 5:13). **Just as there,** with regard to Shabbat, the *halakha* stated with regard to an ox is not limited to oxen, as **undomesticated animals and fowl are similar to** oxen in that they too are included in this prohibition, as the verse states: “Nor any of your animals,” **so too here,** in the case of theft, one can say that **undomesticated animals and fowl are similar to** oxen in that the fourfold or fivefold payment is incurred for their theft.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא, אָמַר קְרָא: ״שׁוֹר וָשֶׂה״ ״שׁוֹר וָשֶׂה״ שְׁנֵי פְּעָמִים – שׁוֹר וָשֶׂה אִין, מִידֵּי אַחֲרִינָא לָא.
> **Translation:** **Rava said: The verse** dealing with theft **states “ox” and “sheep,” “ox” and “sheep” twice.** This repetition indicates that for **an ox and a sheep, yes,** there is a fourfold or fivefold payment, and **for other items, no,** there is no fourfold or fivefold payment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרִי: הֵי מְיַיתַּר? אִילֵימָא ״שׁוֹר וָשֶׂה״ דְּסֵיפָא מְיַיתַּר, דְּנִיכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא: ״כִּי יִגְנֹב שׁוֹר אוֹ שֶׂה וּטְבָחוֹ וּמְכָרוֹ, חֲמִשָּׁה בָקָר יְשַׁלֵּם תַּחְתָּיו וְאַרְבַּע צֹאן תַּחְתָּיו״; אִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא הָכִי, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא בָּעֵי שַׁלּוֹמֵי תִּשְׁעָה לְכׇל אֶחָד וְאֶחָד!
> **Translation:** The Gemara **says,** with regard to Rava’s assertion that one of the instances of “ox” and “sheep” is superfluous: **Which** instance of the word “ox” or “sheep” **is** the **superfluous** one? **If we say** that the instance of **“ox” and “sheep” at the end** of the verse **is superfluous, due to** the following consideration: **Let the Merciful One write: If one steal an ox or a sheep, and slaughter it or sell it, he shall pay five oxen for it and four sheep for it,** without repeating the words “ox” and “sheep,” this is not possible. **If the Merciful One had written** the verse **like this, I would say** that the thief is **required to pay nine** animals, five oxen and four sheep, **for each and every** animal stolen.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי תֵּימָא הָא כְּתִיב ״תַּחְתָּיו״ ״תַּחְתָּיו״ – חַד ״תַּחְתָּיו״ מְיַיתַּר;
> **Translation:** **And if you would say** that this interpretation is not possible, as the suggestion is that the verse would have **written: For it, for it,** twice, whereas if a payment of nine animals were required for each stolen animal the verse would have written: For it, only once; then **one** mention of: **For it, is superfluous,** to teach us that it is five for an ox and four for a sheep.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַהוּא מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ לִדְרָשָׁה אַחֲרִינָא – דְּתַנְיָא: יָכוֹל גָּנַב שׁוֹר שָׁוֶה מָנֶה, יְשַׁלֵּם תַּחְתָּיו [חֲמִשָּׁה] נְגִידִין? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״תַּחְתָּיו״ ״תַּחְתָּיו״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this: **That** repetition of: For it, **is required for another interpretation,** and cannot teach that it is five for an ox and four for a sheep. **As it is taught** in a *baraita*: One **might** have thought that if one **stole** an expensive **ox worth one hundred dinars he may pay for it** with **lean,** inferior **animals [*negidin*].** To counter this, **the verse states: “For** the ox” and **“for** the sheep,” which indicates that the oxen and sheep used for payment must be similar to the stolen animal in quality. Since one might have erred and understood that the thief is required to pay nine animals, five oxen and four sheep, for each and every animal stolen, that suggested version of the verse is not a possibility. The words “ox” and “sheep” at the end of the verse could not have been omitted, so they are not superfluous.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא ״שׁוֹר וָשֶׂה״ דְּרֵישָׁא מְיַיתַּר – דְּנִכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא: ״כִּי יִגְנֹב אִישׁ וּטְבָחוֹ וּמְכָרוֹ, חֲמִשָּׁה בָקָר יְשַׁלֵּם תַּחַת הַשּׁוֹר, וְאַרְבַּע צֹאן תַּחַת הַשֶּׂה״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Rather,** the words **“ox” and “sheep” in the first** part of the verse **are superfluous, due to** the following consideration: **Let the Merciful One write: If a man steal, and slaughter it or sell it, he shall pay five oxen for the ox, and four sheep for the sheep,** without mentioning “ox” or “sheep” at the beginning of the verse.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא הָכִי, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא: עַד דְּגָנֵיב תְּרֵי וְטָבַח לְהוּ! ״וּטְבָחוֹ״ כְּתִיב – לְחַד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara objects: It is not possible for the verse to have been written this way, as **if the Merciful One had written** it in **this** manner **I would say:** A thief is not required to pay the fourfold or fivefold payment **unless he steals two** animals, an ox and a sheep, **and slaughters them** both. The Gemara responds: One could not interpret the verse in this manner, as it **is written: “And slaughter it.”** The singular pronoun “it” is referring **to** the slaughter of only **one** animal.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵימָא: עַד דְּגָנֵיב תַּרְוַיְיהוּ וּמְזַבֵּין לְהוּ! ״וּמְכָרוֹ״ כְּתִיב – לְחַד.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** one could **say** that a thief is not required to pay the fourfold or fivefold payment **unless he steals both** of these animals, an ox and a sheep, **and sells them** both. The Gemara rejects this suggestion in a similar manner: This interpretation is also not possible, as it is **written: “Or sell it,”** which is referring **to** the sale of only **one** animal.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵימָא: הָוֵה אָמֵינָא עַד דְּגָנֵיב תְּרֵי, וְטָבַח חַד וּמְזַבֵּין חַד! ״אוֹ מְכָרוֹ״ כְּתִיב.
> **Translation:** The Gemara further asks: **But** one could **say** a different interpretation: **I would say** that a thief is not required to pay the fourfold or fivefold payment **unless he steals two** animals, an ox and a sheep, **and slaughters one** of them **and sells** the other **one.** The Gemara rejects this suggestion as well: This interpretation is impossible, as it is **written:** “And slaughter it **or sell it.”** The term “or” indicates that only one of these two actions results in paying the penalty.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַכַּתִּי הֲוָה אָמֵינָא עַד דְּגָנֵיב תַּרְוַיְיהוּ וְטָבַח חַד וּמְשַׁיַּיר חַד, אוֹ מְזַבֵּין חַד וּמְשַׁיַּיר חַד!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But still,** had the verse been worded without mentioning an ox or sheep at the start, **I would say:** A thief does not pay the fourfold or fivefold payment **unless he steals two** animals, an ox and a sheep, **and slaughters one** of them **and leaves** the other **one, or sells one and leaves** the other **one.** It is therefore impossible to omit the words “ox” and “sheep” in the beginning of the verse, which means that these words are not superfluous.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא: ״שׁוֹר״ דְּסֵיפָא וְ״שֶׂה״ דְּרֵישָׁא מְיַיתַּר, דְּנִיכְתּוֹב רַחֲמָנָא: ״כִּי יִגְנֹב אִישׁ שׁוֹר וּטְבָחוֹ וּמְכָרוֹ, חֲמִשָּׁה בָקָר יְשַׁלֵּם תַּחְתָּיו, וְאַרְבַּע צֹאן תַּחַת הַשֶּׂה״; ״שׁוֹר״ דְּסֵיפָא וְ״שֶׂה״ דְּרֵישָׁא לְמָה לִי? שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ: שׁוֹר וָשֶׂה אִין, מִידֵּי אַחֲרִינָא לָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara gives its final explanation of Rava’s statement: **Rather, “ox” at the end** of the verse **and “sheep” in the first** part of the verse **are superfluous, due to** the following consideration: **Let the Merciful One write: If a man steal an ox and slaughter it or sell it, he shall pay five oxen for it, and four sheep for a sheep. Why do I** need the word **“ox” at the end** of the verse **and** the word **“sheep” in the first** part of the verse? **Conclude from** these apparently superfluous words that for **an ox and a sheep, yes,** a thief is obligated to pay the fourfold or fivefold payment, but for stealing **other items, no,** he is not.

The Gemara does not simply accept this argument without scrutiny. If the verse could have been written differently and still conveyed the same laws, then the repetition of *shor* and *seh* is not truly significant — it is merely the most natural way to write the verse, and one cannot derive a limiting principle from it. The Gemara therefore examines whether an alternative formulation would have been both linguistically and legally sufficient.

The Gemara first asks: could the Torah have omitted the second mention of *shor* and *seh* and written simply *chamisha bakar yeshalem tachtav ve-arba tzon tachtav* — five cattle in its place and four sheep in its place — using the pronoun *tachtav* rather than naming the animal again? The answer is no, because *tachtav* referring back to an earlier mention of both *shor* and *seh* would be ambiguous: one might think that for either a stolen *shor* or a stolen *seh* one must pay both five cattle and four sheep — a total of nine — rather than understanding that five applies to the *shor* case and four to the *seh* case separately. Although the Gemara considers whether using *tachtav* twice might solve this ambiguity, it concludes that such a double usage of *tachtav* would itself be needed for a separate *derashah* — teaching that the payments must be made with quality animals, not inferior ones. Thus the end of the verse cannot be reformulated, and the repetition of *shor* and *seh* there is required.

The Gemara then asks: could the Torah have omitted the first mention of *shor o seh* and begun the verse as *ki yignov ish* without specifying the animal, letting the end of the verse clarify what was stolen? The answer is again no: without the opening *shor o seh* and its disjunctive *o* — "or" — one might have thought the law applies only when one steals both an ox and a sheep together, never when one steals either animal alone. Even if one would not think one must slaughter both or sell both, there remains a plausible reading that one must steal both before the multiple-payment law is triggered. The opening specification of *shor o seh* is therefore also necessary.

### Exclusion Proven

Since both the opening *shor o seh* and the concluding *tachat ha-shor... tachat ha-seh* are each independently required to prevent misreadings, neither is simply redundant. The Gemara ultimately concludes that the Torah could alternatively have written the verse using the opening *shor o seh* together with *tachtav* at the end, dispensing with the specific mention of *shor* and *seh* in the concluding clause, and the law would have been adequately conveyed. The fact that the Torah instead chose to repeat *shor* and *seh* explicitly in the concluding clause — beyond what was strictly necessary — constitutes the basis for the limiting *derashah*: *shor va-seh in, midi acharina lo* — only an ox and a sheep incur the multiple payments; no other animal does. Whether one finds the *derashah* fully compelling or not, the underlying *halacha* is clear: *arba'ah va-chamishah* is categorically restricted to *shor* and *seh* alone.
