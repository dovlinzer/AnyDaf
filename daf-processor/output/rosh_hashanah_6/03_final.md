[DAF:5b]
## Delayed Korban Valid



### Becha Chet Baraita

The *gemara* on 6a continues its sustained treatment of *bal te'acher* — the prohibition against delaying the fulfillment of one's vow to bring a *korban*. To orient ourselves: the preceding discussion had introduced a *baraita* that interprets the phrase *becha chet* ("the sin shall be upon you") as teaching that *becha chet v'lo b'korban'cha chet* — the sin is yours, but it does not invalidate the *korban* itself. The *gemara* did not dispute this ruling; rather, it observed that the point seemed obvious, since we already know from the case of a *bechor* that a *korban* brought after the proper time remains *kasher*. The *gemara* then raised a possible distinction: perhaps the *korban* is technically *kasher* but lacks *hartza'ah* — it is not found acceptable, not *b'nei hartza'ah*. Both Rashi and Tosafot note the centrality of *kappara* here, but the concept extends more broadly to the notion that a *korban* must find favor before God, that there is an aspect of *yehi ratzon* — of the offering being desired — which is central to the very act of *avodah*. The *bechor*, by contrast, is primarily about the act of separation and sanctification; the actual offering is secondary. But for other *korbanot*, the question of *hartza'ah* is fundamental.

So the *gemara* sought to resolve the matter by pointing out that the *baraita*'s teaching — that the *korban* is not invalidated — is needed to address precisely this concern. Yet the *gemara* then presses further: even granting that, we might have derived the same ruling from another source.

[DAF:6a]
### Ben Azzai & Pigul

The *gemara* notes that the ruling could have been derived from Ben Azzai. A *baraita* teaches: Ben Azzai says, the verse by *pigul* states *lo yeratzeh hamakriv oto* — the one who brings a *pigul* offering, it shall not be considered favorably for him. The word *oto*, "it," singles this out as the problem. One might have thought, the *baraita* reasons, that since the Torah says *lo te'acher leshalmo* — do not delay fulfilling your vow — someone who delays his *neder* would likewise be in the category of *belo yeratzeh*, his *korban* rendered unacceptable like *pigul*. Therefore the verse says *oto*: it is *pigul* that is *belo yeratzeh*, but the one who merely postpones his vow is not in this category. His delay is a sin upon him, but it does not compromise the acceptability of the *korban*.

Thus, the *gemara* observes, we already know from Ben Azzai's *drasha* that postponing a vow does not invalidate the offering. One might ask why the *gemara* insists that different Tannaitic sources cannot simply derive the same *halacha* from different *drashot* — and indeed, the *gemara*'s move here is to press that every source must be teaching something new. So if the point about the *korban*'s validity is already known, what does the phrase *becha chet* come to teach us?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִדְּבֶן עַזַּאי נָפְקָא, דְּתַנְיָא: בֶּן עַזַּאי אוֹמֵר: ״אוֹתוֹ״
> **Translation:** as this *halakha* **is derived from** that **which ben Azzai** said, **as it is taught** in a *baraita* that **ben Azzai says:** The verse states: “And if any of the flesh of the sacrifice of his peace-offering be eaten at all on the third day, it shall not be accepted, neither shall it be imputed to him who offers **it,** it shall be *piggul*” (Leviticus 7:18).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר? לְפִי שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״לֹא תְאַחֵר לְשַׁלְּמוֹ״, שׁוֹמֵעַ אֲנִי אַף מְאַחֵר נִדְרוֹ בְּ״בַל יֵרָצֶה״, תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״אוֹתוֹ״ — אוֹתוֹ בְּ״לֹא יֵרָצֶה״, וְאֵין מְאַחֵר נִדְרוֹ בְּ״לֹא יֵרָצֶה״,
> **Translation:** For **what** purpose does **the verse state** the word “it”? **Since** elsewhere **it states:** “When you shall vow a vow to the Lord your God, **you shall not delay paying it;** for the Lord your God will surely require it from you, and it would be sin in you” (Deuteronomy 23:22), **I might have interpreted** from this verse that **even one who is late** in paying **his vow** is included in: **It shall not be accepted.** Therefore, **the verse states “it.” It,** an offering disqualified by improper intention [*piggul*], **is** included in the *halakha* of: **“It shall not be accepted,” but** the animal of **one who is late** in paying **his vow is not** included **in** the *halakha* of: **“It shall not be accepted.”**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא: ״בְּךָ חֵטְא״ — וְלֹא בְּאִשְׁתְּךָ חֵטְא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects what was said above; **rather,** the explanation of the verse is as follows. The phrase: “And it would be **sin in you”** comes to teach that there would be a sin in you, **but** there would **not** be **a sin in your wife.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ אָמֵינָא: הוֹאִיל וְאָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן, וְאִי תֵּימָא רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: אֵין אִשְׁתּוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם מֵתָה אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן מְבַקְּשִׁין מִמֶּנּוּ מָמוֹן וְאֵין לוֹ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״אִם אֵין לְךָ לְשַׁלֵּם לָמָּה יִקַּח מִשְׁכָּבְךָ מִתַּחְתֶּיךָ״, אֵימָא בְּהַאי עָוֹן דְּ״בַל תְּאַחֵר״ נָמֵי אִשְׁתּוֹ מֵתָה, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן.
> **Translation:** It was necessary to say that the lateness is not imputed to the other members of one’s household for the following reason: **It might enter your mind to say: Since Rabbi Yoḥanan said, and some say** that it was **Rabbi Elazar** who said: **A person’s wife dies only because** others **demand of him money and he does not have** means with which to pay, **as it is stated** about one who commits himself to guarantee a loan: **“If you have nothing with which to pay, why should he take away your bed from under you?”** (Proverbs 22:27). The verse warns one who takes a loan that incurring debt may result in one losing the very sheets that he sleeps on to his creditor. The Gemara understands this homiletically: Why should you cause God to take away your wife, i.e., she who shares your bed, so that she dies? Consequently, you might **say that** one’s **wife also dies for this transgression** of the prohibition: **You shall not delay,** in that one fails to fulfill his commitment. Therefore, **the verse teaches us** that this is not so. Rather, this sin is imputed to him alone.

### Sin on Person

The *gemara* reinterprets the *drasha* entirely. Rather than *becha chet v'lo b'korban'cha chet* — the sin is on you, not on your *korban* — the verse is now read as *becha chet v'lo b'ish'techa chet*: the sin falls upon you, not upon your wife.

### Wife's Death Context

This interpretation requires context. The *gemara* invokes a teaching of Rabbi Yochanan — or, some say, Rabbi Elazar — that *ein ishto shel adam meitah ela im ken mevakshin mimenu mamon v'ein lo*: a man's wife does not die unless heaven demands from him a monetary obligation that he is not fulfilling. What does this mean in context? Rashi understands it as referring to stolen money left unrepaid. Tosafot, more attuned to the passage at hand, explains it as referring to an unfulfilled vow — *mevakshin mimenu mamon*, heaven is calling in the pledge he made and has not satisfied. The consequence of non-fulfillment is rendered in vivid terms: the one who sleeps beside you is taken from you. One might then have assumed — *havah amina* — that the same punishment applies even to *bal te'acher*, the mere postponement of a vow. Therefore the verse teaches: *becha chet*, the sin focuses on you alone, it is not collective punishment. Non-fulfillment is one matter; postponement is another — and postponement does not trigger collective consequences.

### Collective Punishment

This, of course, is a striking and theologically charged teaching. The original *baraita* had said the sin falls on you and not on your *korban*; the *gemara* now reads it as saying the sin falls on you and not on your wife. That the two readings coexisted and the *gemara* chose to pursue the second is itself noteworthy.

It is worth acknowledging the discomfort this teaching provokes. The *Torah* itself seems to deny collective punishment in the starkest terms: *lo yumtu avot al banim u-vanim lo yumtu al avot* — fathers shall not be put to death for sons, nor sons for fathers; *ish b'chet'o yumatu* — each person is put to death for his own sin.

And yet it would be a mistake to treat these verses as simply closing the door on collective consequence. There are other places in the *Torah*, particularly around *avodah zarah*, where something that looks very much like collective punishment surfaces. *Ve-natati et panai ba-ish ha-hu u-ve-mishpachto* — I will set My face against that man and against his family — is said explicitly of one who gives his children over to the Molech. The family has not sinned, yet the family is implicated. Onkelos, evidently uncomfortable with that implication, translates *mishpachto* there not as "his family" but as "his supporters" — a deliberate reframing designed to preserve the principle of individual accountability. But the plain meaning is there, and it is not an isolated case. By *ir ha-nidachat*, the *Rambam* rules that along with the guilty men, their wives and children are killed as well — a ruling the other *rishonim* find deeply shocking, but which reflects a strand of thinking in which the household unit, not only the individual, is the relevant moral entity. One way to understand this is not as guilt-by-association in the strict sense, but as something closer to the need to cleanse and remove all vestiges of a corruption — *avodah zarah* in particular being treated as a kind of communal contamination whose eradication requires the removal of everything bound up with it. The family is not punished because they share the guilt; they are swept away because, in the *Torah*'s framework around *avodah zarah*, the contamination attaches to the group. This is also, it seems, the animating logic of *ir ha-nidachat*.

This context matters for reading the *gemara* here. The teaching that a man's wife may die on account of an unfulfilled vow is not an aberration in the tradition — it has echoes in other *sugyot*, and it is embedded in *halakha*: the *Mishnah* in *Masechet Ketubot* lists *noderet ve'eina mekayyemet* — she makes vows and does not fulfill them — as grounds for divorce without *ketubah*, precisely because unfulfilled vows bring danger to the household. The *gemara* there draws on the same theological strand operative here. What the *gemara* in our passage is doing is not introducing this idea but limiting it: even granted that the non-fulfillment of a vow can have consequences that extend beyond the individual who vowed, that collective reach does not extend to *bal te'acher* alone — to mere postponement. The verse *becha chet* narrows the exposure.

### Collective Punishment

And yet the tradition is not entirely uniform on this point. There are contexts — particularly around *avodah zarah* — where something resembling collective punishment appears. The verse in Devarim states, in the context of one who gives his children to *Molech*, *v'natati et panai ba-ish ha-hu u-ve-mishpachto* — I shall set My face against that man and against his family. What did his family do? Interestingly, Onkelos there translates *mishpachto* not as "his family" but as "his supporters," evidently seeking to preserve the principle of individual culpability by reading the collective as a community of willing participants. But the plain sense of the verse does point toward something collective. By *ir hanidachat*, the *gemara* resolves the difficulty by saying that the innocent are separated out and only the guilty are killed — but the Rambam famously rules that the guilty men are executed together with their wives and children, which is a position the other *rishonim* find deeply troubling, and which even pertains to a court-imposed punishment, not merely divine retribution.

One student offered a more structural reading: in the case of *avodah zarah*, what is at stake is not personal guilt spreading to family members, but rather the need to eradicate every trace of the corrupt influence. The family, on this reading, is not guilty — but the entire ecosystem connected to the *avodah zarah* must be cleansed, just as *shabbor teshabber matzevoteihem* demands the destruction of all vestiges. This is why *ir hanidachat* exists as a category — not because everyone in the city is equally culpable, but because the contamination must be fully excised. The same logic may explain why the concern extends to the family by *Korach* as well: one does not leave behind a four-year-old who will grow up to seek vengeance and carry on the rebellion. Rashi on the verse *v'ish hu b'mishpachto* takes a different approach, suggesting that the family is implicated because a corrupt figure — Rashi's example is a tax collector — typically has the complicity of the entire household. Rashi thus attempts to reframe the collective punishment as collective guilt.

In any event, the *gemara* here is working within a tradition that acknowledges, particularly in connection with *avodah zarah* and unfulfilled vows, some notion of collective consequences radiating outward from the individual. The *mishna* in Ketubot lists *noderet v'eina mekayyemet* — a wife who makes vows and does not fulfill them — as grounds for divorce without the *ketubah*. One might ask what her unfulfilled vows have to do with the husband. The *gemara* there explains it through this very same idea: unfulfilled vows can bring punishment upon the family. The same theme runs through our passage.

What the *gemara* here accomplishes is to narrow it: whatever applies in the case of outright non-fulfillment of a vow, it does not apply to mere postponement. *Becha chet* — the sin is on you, and not visited collectively. Though even the tradition acknowledges that *ba'avon nedarim*, for the sin of violating vows, a person's children may die — a statement embedded in the *halacha* of Ketubot — the *gemara* limits the collective dimension. Postponement is a sin, but it is an individual one.

## Motza Sefatecha Dras (II)



### Why Need Motza


The *gemara* now turns to a *baraita* on the closing verse of the *bal te'acher* passage: *motza sefatecha tishmor v'asita ka'asher nadarta l'Hashem Elokecha nedavah ka'asher dibarta beficha* — what comes forth from your lips you shall observe and do, as you vowed to Hashem your God, a *nedavah*, as you spoke with your mouth.

The *baraita* proceeds to identify multiple obligations within this verse. *Motza sefatecha* — this is a *mitzvat asei*, a positive commandment. *Tishmor* — this is a *mitzvat lo ta'asei*, a negative prohibition. And *v'asita* — this constitutes an *azharah l'veit din she'yasucha*, a charge to the court to compel the person to act.

But the *gemara* immediately challenges each element. Why do we need a *mitzvat asei* from *motza sefatecha*? We already have *u'vata shama v'havatem shama* — the verse commanding one to come to the place God chooses and bring all one's sacrifices there; that is already an explicit positive obligation. Why do we need *tishmor* as a negative prohibition? We already have the explicit *lo te'acher leshalmo* — do not delay fulfilling it — at the beginning of the passage; that is *bal te'acher* in its plainest form. And why do we need *v'asita* to teach that the court must compel someone to bring his *korban*? This too we already know: *yakriv oto*, as expounded in a *baraita* from Vayikra, teaches *she'kofin oto ad she'yomar rotzeh ani* — you compel him until he says "I am willing." *Yakriv oto* is read not as "he brings it" but as "you cause him to bring it" — the court presses the person into compliance. The resolution of the apparent contradiction between compulsion and *l'rtzono* — that it must be according to his will — is the famous principle that deep down a person wishes to fulfill his obligations; the *yeitzer hara* stands in the way, and the court beats the *yeitzer hara* out of him until his true desire is revealed. The Rambam discusses this principle at length in the context of the coerced *get*.

Tosafot notes that there are other *gemarot* indicating that the court generally compels performance of positive *mitzvot* — one thinks of the case of someone refusing to don *tefillin* or sit in a *sukkah* — and so the question of why we need a special verse for *korbanot* is pointed. The answer is that by *korbanot* one might have thought the Torah's explicit insistence on *l'rtzono* would preclude compulsion; the verse therefore tells us that compulsion applies even here.

The *gemara* then notes that since we apparently derive all three elements from other sources, the passage *motza sefatecha* must be doing something different — namely, extending these obligations beyond the domain of *korbanot*, to include *kodshei bedek habayit* and *tzedakah* as well.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״מוֹצָא שְׂפָתֶיךָ״ — זוֹ מִצְוַת עֲשֵׂה, ״תִּשְׁמוֹר״ — זוֹ מִצְוַת לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה, ״וְעָשִׂיתָ״ — אַזְהָרָה לְבֵית דִּין שֶׁיְּעַשּׂוּךְ, ״כַּאֲשֶׁר נָדַרְתָּ״ — זֶה נֶדֶר, ״לַה׳ אֱלֹהֶיךָ״ — אֵלּוּ חַטָּאוֹת וַאֲשָׁמוֹת עוֹלוֹת וּשְׁלָמִים, ״נְדָבָה״ — כְּמַשְׁמָעוֹ, ״אֲשֶׁר דִּבַּרְתָּ״ — אֵלּוּ קׇדְשֵׁי בֶּדֶק הַבַּיִת, ״בְּפִיךְ״ — זוֹ צְדָקָה.
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states: “That which is gone out of your lips you shall keep and do; as you have vowed as a gift to the Lord your God, which you have promised with your mouth” (Deuteronomy 23:24). **“That which is gone out of your lips”; this is a positive mitzva. “You shall keep”; this is a prohibition,** as the phrase “you shall keep” is a warning to keep oneself from sinning. **“And do”;** this is **an admonition to the court to make you** fulfill your vow. **“As you have vowed”; this** is referring to **a vow-offering. “To the Lord your God”; this** is referring to **sin-offerings, guilt-offerings, burnt-offerings, and peace-offerings,** teaching that one must keep his word and bring them. “As a **gift”;** this is understood **in its literal sense** to be referring to a gift-offering. **“Which you have promised”; this** is referring to **objects consecrated for Temple maintenance. “With your mouth”; this** is referring to vows of **charity,** to which one commits himself with his mouth.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר: ״מוֹצָא שְׂפָתֶיךָ״ — זוֹ מִצְוַת עֲשֵׂה. לְמָה לִי? מִ״וּבָאתָ שָּׁמָּה ... וַהֲבֵאתֶם שָׁמָּה״ נָפְקָא! ״תִּשְׁמוֹר״ — זוֹ מִצְוַת לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה. לְמָה לִי? מִ״לֹּא תְאַחֵר לְשַׁלְּמוֹ״ נָפְקָא!
> **Translation:** The Gemara clarifies the details mentioned in this *baraita*. **The Master said: “That which is gone out of your lips”; this is a positive mitzva. Why do I** need this derivation? **Isn’t** the positive mitzva **derived** from the verse: **“And there you shall come; and there you shall bring** your burnt-offerings and your sacrifices, and your tithes, and the offering of your hand, and your vows, and your gift-offerings, and the firstborn of your herd and of your flock” (Deuteronomy 12:5–6)? The *baraita* continues: **“You shall keep”; this is a prohibition. Why do I** need this derivation; **this is derived** from the verse: **“You shall not delay paying it”** (Deuteronomy 23:22).

## Tzedakah & Bedek (III)



### Categories Drasha


The *baraita* reads the entire clause of *motza sefatecha... ka'asher nadarta... nedavah... ka'asher dibarta beficha* as a series of categories to which the obligations of *bal te'acher* apply. *Ka'asher nadarta l'Hashem Elokecha* — this is a *neder*, the formula *harei alai*, a personal obligation. *L'Hashem Elokecha* — these are *chatat, asham, olah*, and *shlamim*, the various *korbanot* brought to God, whether obligatory or voluntary. *Nedavah kemashmao* — a *nedavah* in the straightforward sense, as distinguished from a *neder*: whereas a *neder* is *harei alai* — I obligate myself — a *nedavah* is *harei zu* — I sanctify this object. *Ka'asher dibarta* — what you spoke: these are *kodshei bedek habayit*, donations to the *beit hamikdash* in a non-*korban* form, where one sanctifies the monetary value of an object and transfers its ownership to the Temple treasury. And finally, *beficha* — with your mouth: *zu tzedakah*, this refers to *tzedakah*.

On the connection of *beficha* to *tzedakah*, Tosafot cites the verse *yatza mipi tzedakah* — "righteousness went forth from My mouth" — which explicitly links the word for *mouth* with *tzedakah*. This conjunction, *beficha zu tzedakah*, carries enormous resonance in later halachic literature and becomes a standard formula in discussions of whether *bal te'acher* applies to charitable pledges. The phrase functions almost as a single unit in that literature, a shorthand for the entire question of how *bal te'acher* governs the unfulfilled *tzedakah* pledge.

It should be noted that this list, as the *gemara* presents it here, is somewhat shorter than the list as it appeared in the preceding sugya. Conspicuously absent is *leket, shikcha, u'peah* — a category that Tosafot had already flagged as difficult from multiple angles: there is no act of speech that creates the obligation (making *beficha zu tzedakah* inapplicable), and the very concept of *bal te'acher* sits uneasily in that context, since either you left the *leket* in the field or you did not. The fact that this category does not appear in the *gemara*'s current formulation is notable, and its omission is not incidental — it was precisely the category that made the least sense from the outset, and the *gemara* here simply does not include it.

The court's role in enforcement applies across this entire list, not only to *korbanot*. The Rambam rules that one who fails to give the appropriate amount of *tzedakah* is subject to compulsion by the court — a ruling that flows directly from this sugya's extension of *kofin oto* to the full scope of *bal te'acher*. This has real weight: we are not speaking only of obligations owed to the Temple treasury, where one might argue the *beit din* acts as a kind of collection agent for *hekdesh*, but of *tzedakah* obligations as well, which might seem more purely personal in nature. Nevertheless, the same coercive mechanism applies. Much of the *halacha* of *tzedakah* is also elaborated at the beginning of Bava Batra.

One question left open by the *gemara* is how to categorize a pledge made to a synagogue or communal institution that does not fit neatly as *tzedakah* to the poor. Does such a pledge fall under *bedek habayit* as an extension of that category, or does it fall under *tzedakah*, or perhaps simply under the general rubric of *neder*? The *gemara* does not address this directly.

## Motza Sefatecha Dras (II)



### Two Stages

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״וְעָשִׂיתָ״ — אַזְהָרָה לְבֵית דִּין שֶׁיְּעַשּׂוּךְ. לְמָה לִי? מִ״יַּקְרִיב אוֹתוֹ״ נָפְקָא, דְּתַנְיָא: ״יַקְרִיב אוֹתוֹ״ — מְלַמֵּד שֶׁכּוֹפִין אוֹתוֹ. יָכוֹל בְּעַל כׇּרְחוֹ — תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״לִרְצוֹנוֹ״, הָא כֵּיצַד? כּוֹפִין אוֹתוֹ עַד שֶׁיֹּאמַר ״רוֹצֶה אֲנִי״.
> **Translation:** **“And do”;** this is **a warning to the court to make you** fulfill your vow. **Why do I** need this derivation? This rule **is derived** from the verse: **“He shall offer it”** (Leviticus 1:3), **as it is taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states: **“He shall offer it,”** which **teaches that he must be forced** to bring his offering. One **might** have thought that he may be forced to bring his offering even **against his will.** Therefore, **the verse states: “In accordance with his will”** (Leviticus 1:3). **How so? The** court **coerces him until he says: I want to** bring the offering. Now, since all of these *halakhot* are already known from other sources, what is the point of this repetition?

The *gemara* now introduces a crucial distinction. After exhausting the question of why each element of *motza sefatecha* is needed when we already have other sources, the *gemara* offers its definitive answer: *chad amar v'lo afrish v'chad afrish v'lo akriv* — one verse speaks to the case where the person has taken a vow but has not yet separated an animal, and the other speaks to the case where the person has already sanctified the animal but has not yet brought it as a *korban*. Both stages involve *bal te'acher*, and each requires its own source.

And *u'tzricha* — you need both. If we only knew that *bal te'acher* applies when the person has taken a vow without yet separating an animal — *mishum delo kaim l'diburei*, because he has not yet acted on his words, his vow remains unsatisfied — one might have thought that once the animal is sanctified, the obligation is effectively in the Temple's possession already, and there is no further *bal te'acher* for the delay in physically delivering it. *Kol heicha de'isa b'igaza de'rachmana isa* — wherever the animal is, it is as if it sits in the divine storehouse. So we need the verse to tell us there is *bal te'acher* even in the second stage.

Conversely, if we only knew that *bal te'acher* applies when the animal has been sanctified and is being held without delivery — *dekamoshi gabei*, because there is something concrete that belongs to *hekdesh* and is being withheld — one might have thought that mere words, a vow without any physical action, are not sufficient to trigger *bal te'acher*. *Dibura lo kelum hu* — words alone amount to nothing in this context; *bal te'acher* requires that there be an actual object being held back from the *beit hamikdash*, somewhat analogous to *lo ta'ashok sechar sachir* — withholding a laborer's wages. Therefore both verses are necessary.

This raises an interesting question that the *gemara* itself does not address: if someone says *harei alai olah* — taking a vow — and then two *regalim* pass, and then he sanctifies an animal, and then two more *regalim* pass, and then he brings it — has he violated *bal te'acher*? Are these two distinct forms of *bal te'acher* such that sanctifying the animal resets the clock on the second stage, or does the original vow define the entire period from start to finish? The *gemara* does not say the clock resets, and presumably it does not, but the existence of two distinct dimensions of *bal te'acher* — the unfulfilled vow and the unsacrificed animal — is worth marking.

### Neder & Nedavah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** חַד, דַּאֲמַר וְלָא אַפְרֵישׁ, וְחַד, אַפְרֵישׁ וְלָא אַקְרֵיב.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **One** set of verses is referring to a case **where one said** that he vowed to bring an offering **but did not** yet **set aside** a specific animal for his vow, and **one** set of verses is referring to a case **where he set aside** a specific animal for his vow **but did not** yet **sacrifice** it on the altar.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּצְרִיכָא, דְּאִי אַשְׁמְעִינַן אֲמַר וְלָא אַפְרֵישׁ, מִשּׁוּם דְּלָא קַיְּימֵיהּ לְדִיבּוּרֵיהּ, אֲבָל אַפְרֵישׁ וְלָא אַקְרֵיב, אֵימָא: כֹּל הֵיכָא דְּאִיתֵיהּ — בֵּי גַזָּא דְּרַחֲמָנָא אִיתֵיהּ. צְרִיכָא.
> **Translation:** **And it is necessary** to teach the *halakha* in both cases, **as had** the Torah **taught us** only about the *halakha* of the case where **one said** that he vowed to bring an offering **but did not** yet **set aside** a specific animal for his vow, one might have said that only in this case has he transgressed **because he did not keep his word; however, if he set aside** a specific animal for his vow **but did not** yet **sacrifice** it on the altar, one might **say** that **anywhere that it is, it is in the treasure house of the Merciful One,** as the world and everything in it belongs to God, and therefore it makes no difference if he delays in bringing it to the Temple. Therefore, **it is necessary** to teach that even when one has set aside a specific animal he transgresses the prohibition.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי אַשְׁמְעִינַן אַפְרֵישׁ וְלָא אַקְרֵיב, דְּקָא מַשְׁהֵי לֵיהּ גַּבֵּיהּ, אֲבָל אָמַר וְלָא אַפְרֵישׁ, אֵימָא דִּיבּוּרָא לֹא כְּלוּם הוּא. צְרִיכָא.
> **Translation:** **And had** the Torah **taught us** only about the case where **one set aside** a specific animal for his vow **but did not** yet **sacrifice** it on the altar, one might have said that only in this case has he transgressed **because he is keeping** the animal **for himself. But if he said** that he vowed to bring an offering **but did not** yet **set aside** a specific animal for his vow, one might **say** that his mere **speech is nothing,** and there is no transgression provided he has not actually set aside an animal. Therefore, **it** is **necessary** to teach the *halakha* in both cases.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִי מָצֵית אָמְרַתְּ דְּאָמַר וְלָא אַפְרֵישׁ? וְהָא נְדָבָה כְּתִיבָא, וּתְנַן: אֵי זֶהוּ נֶדֶר — הָאוֹמֵר: הֲרֵי עָלַי עוֹלָה. וְאֵי זוֹ הִיא נְדָבָה — הָאוֹמֵר: הֲרֵי זוֹ עוֹלָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **How can you say** that the Gemara is dealing with a case **where one** merely **said** that he vowed to bring an offering **but did not** yet **set aside** a specific animal? **Isn’t a gift-offering mentioned** in the verse, **and we learned** in a mishna: **What is a vow-**offering? It is an offering brought by **one who says:** It is incumbent **upon me** to bring **a burnt-offering.** And **what is a gift-offering?** It is an offering brought by **one who says,** concerning a particular animal: **I undertake** to bring **this** animal as **a burnt-offering.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמָה בֵּין נֶדֶר לִנְדָבָה? נֶדֶר, מֵת אוֹ נִגְנַב — חַיָּיב בְּאַחְרָיוּתוֹ. נְדָבָה, מֵתָה אוֹ נִגְנְבָה — אֵינוֹ חַיָּיב בְּאַחְרָיוּתָהּ.
> **Translation:** **And what is the difference between a vow-**offering **and a gift-offering?** With regard to **vow-**offerings, if the animal **died or was stolen,** the one who took the vow **is obligated** to pay **restitution** for **it.** He undertook to bring a burnt-offering without specifying the animal, and therefore until he brings that offering he is not absolved of his obligation. With regard to **a gift-offering,** however, if the animal **died or was stolen, he is not obligated** to pay **restitution** for **it** because he undertook to bring a specific animal, and that is no longer possible. In the case of a gift-offering, then, a specific animal must have already been set aside as an offering.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ, כְּגוֹן דְּאָמַר: הֲרֵי עָלַי עוֹלָה עַל מְנָת שֶׁאֵינִי חַיָּיב בְּאַחְרָיוּתָהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rava said: You** can **find** a case of a gift-offering where a specific animal has not yet been set aside; **for example, where one said:** It is incumbent **upon me** to bring **a burnt-offering on the condition that** after I set an animal aside in fulfillment of my vow, **I will not be liable to replace it** should the animal die or be stolen.

The *gemara* raises a sharp difficulty with this two-stage framework. It notes that the word *nedavah* appears in both of the relevant verses — and by definition, a *nedavah* involves saying *harei zu*, thereby immediately sanctifying the animal. So if a *nedavah* always comes with a sanctified animal, how can one of the verses be addressing the case where the person has only spoken and not yet sanctified anything? *U'nedavah ksiv* — *nedavah* is written in both verses.

The *mishnah* makes the distinction explicit: *eizehu neder? ha'omer harei alai olah* — a *neder* is when one says "I take upon myself an *olah*." *V'eizehu nedavah? ha'omer harei zu olah* — a *nedavah* is when one says "this animal shall be an *olah*," immediately sanctifying the object. The difference has practical consequences: if it is a *neder* and the animal dies or is stolen, the person remains obligated and must bring another, since his personal commitment (*harei alai*) is still outstanding. If it is a *nedavah* and the animal dies or is stolen, he is exempt, since he never took on a personal obligation — he only sanctified the animal, and once it is gone, so is the obligation.

So the *gemara* asks: how can one verse be addressing the pre-sanctification stage when *nedavah* — written in both verses — means the animal has already been sanctified?

Rava resolves this. *Mashkachat lah kegon d'amar: harei alai olah al m'nat she'eini chayav b'achrayushah* — the case is where a person says: I accept upon myself the obligation of an *olah* on the condition that I will not be responsible for it if something happens to it after I sanctify it. This is technically a *neder* — it involves a personal obligation, *harei alai* — but it carries the halachic consequence of a *nedavah* with respect to *achrayut*: if the animal dies, he is not obligated to replace it. Rava is thus saying that the word *nedavah* in the verse that addresses the pre-sanctification stage refers not to a classic *nedavah* — *harei zu* — but to a *neder* that has assumed *nedavah*-like consequences through this stipulation.

In summary: the first verse addresses a *neder* in the classic sense (*harei alai*) or a *neder* qualified to resemble a *nedavah* in its consequences — in both cases, no animal has yet been physically sanctified. The second verse addresses the case where the animal has already been sanctified, whether through a classic *nedavah* (*harei zu*) or through a *neder* that has been actualized by designating a specific animal. Both stages trigger *bal te'acher*, and both require their own scriptural source.

## Tzedakah & Bedek (III)



### Rava Tzedakah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״בְּפִיךְ״ — זוֹ צְדָקָה. אָמַר רָבָא: וּצְדָקָה — מִיחַיַּיב עֲלַהּ לְאַלְתַּר. מַאי טַעְמָא — דְּהָא קָיְימִי עֲנִיִּים.
> **Translation:** § The *baraita* stated: **“With your mouth”; this** is referring to vows of **charity. Rava said:** In the case of vows of **charity, one is liable immediately** if he is late in distributing the charity that he had promised to give. **What is the reason** for this *halakha*? It is **that poor people** to whom the charity may be given **exist** in all places, and so the charity can be distributed to them immediately, unlike an offering, which must be brought to the Temple.

Having established the framework of *bal te'acher*, the *gemara* now turns to Rava's striking position on *tzedakah*. Rava declares: *utzedakah mechayev alah le'alter* — regarding *tzedakah*, one immediately becomes obligated. The violation of *bal te'acher* for *tzedakah* does not wait for three *regalim*; it is instantaneous. *Mai ta'ama? Dekaimi aniyim* — the reason is that the poor are present. The *regel* functions as the defining window of opportunity for *korbanot* — when one is *oleh l'regel* is when one has the opportunity to bring the offering. By *tzedakah*, every day that the poor are around constitutes such an opportunity. Failure to act is therefore an immediate transgression.

The *gemara* responds *pshita* — this is obvious — and one might expect that to end the discussion. But the *gemara* clarifies that it was not obvious: one might have thought that since *tzedakah* appears in the same *drasha* as *korbanot*, it should follow the same schedule — *ad de'avrei alei gimmel regalim ke'korbanot*. Therefore Rava tells us: *hatam hu de'leitnehu de'talinehu Rachmana b'regalim* — there, by *korbanot*, the Torah pegged the obligation to the *regalim*, which is when the opportunity arises. *Aval hacha*, here by *tzedakah*, it is not about *regalim* — *de'shchichi aniyim* — the poor are always present.

---

## Rava Severity (III)



### Rava I: Immediate

This is the first of three escalating statements from Rava about the severity and scope of *bal te'acher*. The immediate obligation for *tzedakah* — *mechayev alah le'alter* — is Rava's most extreme formulation, and it raises practical questions about what "immediately" means. Does one have even a short period to get one's affairs in order before writing the check, as it were? And does the obligation exist continuously, since the poor are always around?

A further question worth flagging is what happens when someone pledges a donation not to the poor but to a synagogue or similar institution. Such a gift is not straightforwardly *tzedakah*, nor is it quite equivalent to *bedek ha'bayit*, the Temple maintenance category that appears on the *gemara*'s list. Whether such a pledge falls under the *bal te'acher* framework at all — whether it is close enough to a *neder* to be included even if it does not map cleanly onto any of the enumerated cases — is not entirely clear. That ambiguity is itself worth noting: Rava's dramatic statement about *tzedakah* does not automatically resolve the status of every charitable-style commitment. How the limitation Tosafot introduces bears on these edge cases will become clearer as that discussion unfolds.

---

## Tzedakah & Bedek (III)



### Tosafot Limitation

Tosafot, commenting on 4a, pulls back from the full force of Rava's position. Tosafot writes: *hani mili dekaimi aniyim, aval lo mechayev lehadori basreihu kol zman she'lo avru aleha shalosh regalim* — this applies when the poor are right in front of you. But if poor people are not directly present, one is not required to go searching for them; the obligation would still be governed by the three-*regalim* framework. Tosafot thus preserves Rava's principle while containing it: the immediacy of the obligation is activated by direct encounter with the poor, not by the abstract existence of poverty in the world. What counts as that direct encounter, however, is itself an open question — whether it means literally seeing the poor standing at one's door, or whether fulfilling the pledge through more indirect means, such as an online donation, would equally satisfy the trigger Tosafot has in mind.

## Rava Severity (III)



[DAF:6b]
### Rava II: One Regel

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פְּשִׁיטָא! מַהוּ דְּתֵימָא: כֵּיוָן דִּבְעִנְיָינָא דְּקׇרְבָּנוֹת כְּתִיבָא, עַד דְּעָבְרִי עֲלַהּ שְׁלֹשָׁה רְגָלִים כְּקׇרְבָּנוֹת, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן: הָתָם הוּא דִּתְלִינְהוּ רַחֲמָנָא בִּרְגָלִים, אֲבָל הָכָא לָא — דְּהָא שְׁכִיחִי עֲנִיִּים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: It is **obvious** that charity must be given to the poor without delay. The Gemara explains: **Lest you say** that **since** the *halakha* pertaining to vows of charity **is written in the passage dealing with offerings,** perhaps one does not transgress the prohibition against delaying **until three Festivals have passed, as** is the *halakha* with regard to **offerings,** therefore Rava **teaches us** that this is not so. Rather, **there,** with regard to the offerings, **the Merciful One made** the timing of the transgression **dependent upon the** time of the **Festivals,** when one must go on pilgrimage to the Temple. **However, here,** with regard to charity vows, this is **not** so **because poor people** who are ready to accept charity **are found** in all places.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: כֵּיוָן שֶׁעָבַר עָלָיו רֶגֶל אֶחָד — עוֹבֵר בַּעֲשֵׂה.
> **Translation:** **Rava said:** Although, according to most opinions one transgresses the prohibition against delaying only after three Festivals have elapsed, **once** even **one Festival has passed** and he has not sacrificed the offerings that he vowed to bring, **he** immediately **violates a positive mitzva.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵיתִיבִי: הֵעִיד רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ וְרַבִּי פַּפְּיָיס עַל וָלָד שְׁלָמִים שֶׁיִּקְרַב שְׁלָמִים. אָמַר רַבִּי פַּפְּיָיס: אֲנִי מֵעִיד שֶׁהָיְתָה לָנוּ פָּרָה שֶׁל זִבְחֵי שְׁלָמִים וַאֲכַלְנוּהָ בַּפֶּסַח, וְאָכַלְנוּ וְלָדָהּ שְׁלָמִים בֶּחָג.
> **Translation:** The Gemara **raises an objection** from the following mishna: **Rabbi Yehoshua and Rabbi Papeyyas testified about** the **offspring of peace-offerings.** They said **that** if the mother animal was consecrated before it conceived or during its pregnancy, the offspring, too, **must be sacrificed as a peace-offering. Rabbi Papeyyas said: I testify that we** once **had a cow that was sacrificed as a peace-offering, and we ate it on Passover [*beFesaḥ*], and we ate its offspring as a peace-offering on the Festival [*beḤag*],** i.e., on *Sukkot*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא בְּפֶסַח לָא אַקְרְבוּהּ, אֵימוֹר דִּמְחוּסָּר זְמַן הֲוָה. אֶלָּא וַלְדַּהּ בַּעֲצֶרֶת הֵיכִי מַשְׁהִי לֵהּ וְעָבְרִי עֲלֵיהּ בַּעֲשֵׂה!
> **Translation:** The Gemara clarifies the details of this story: **Granted, on Passover** itself Rabbi Papeyyas and his family **did not sacrifice** the offspring, as one can **say** that the animal **was lacking** the requisite time, i.e., it was less than eight days old, and it is prohibited to sacrifice such a young animal. **But how could they delay** and not sacrifice the offspring **on *Shavuot*,** the first Festival after Passover, if, according to Rava, **they** would **violate a positive mitzva** as soon as the first Festival passed?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב זְבִיד מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא: כְּגוֹן
> **Translation:** **Rav Zevid said in the name of Rava: For example,** this occurs

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁהָיָה חוֹלֶה בָּעֲצֶרֶת. רַב אָשֵׁי אָמַר: מַאי ״וְאָכַלְנוּ וְלָדָהּ שְׁלָמִים בֶּחָג״ דְּקָתָנֵי — חַג הַשָּׁבוּעוֹת.
> **Translation:** in a case **where** the offspring **was sick on *Shavuot*,** and therefore it could be brought to the altar only on the next Festival. **Rav Ashi said** an alternative explanation: **What is** the statement: **And we ate its offspring as a peace-offering on the Festival,** coming to **teach?** This is not referring to the festival of *Sukkot*, which is the usual meaning of the term the Festival; rather, it is referring to **the festival of *Shavuot*.** Therefore, there is no difficulty here whatsoever.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִידַּךְ: כֹּל הֵיכָא דְּתָנֵי פֶּסַח, תָּנֵי עֲצֶרֶת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** what does **the other** *amora*, Rav Zevid, say to this? He argues that **anywhere that** the *tanna* **teaches** a *halakha* concerning Passover using the term ***Pesaḥ*, he** **teaches** the *halakha* concerning *Shavuot* using the term ***Atzaret***, not the term *Ḥag*. In such a context, the term *Ḥag* is reserved exclusively for *Sukkot*.

The second of Rava's statements: *keivan she'avar alav regel echad, over b'asei* — once a single *regel* has passed without the person fulfilling his vow, he transgresses a positive commandment. The *mitzvat asei* of *u'vata shama v'havatem shama* — the obligation to bring one's sacrifices to the place God chooses — is violated as soon as the first opportunity is missed. Three *regalim* must pass to trigger *bal te'acher* proper, the *lo ta'asei*, but even one *regel* triggers a violation of the *asei*.

The *gemara* challenges this from a *baraita*: the case of the *vlad shlamim*, the offspring of a *shlamim* sacrifice. A witness testified: *hayesa lanu parah shel zivchei shlamim*, we once had a cow that was a *shlamim*, *v'achalnuhu b'Pesach*, we ate it at Pesach, *v'achalnu valdah shlamim b'chag*, and we ate its offspring as a *shlamim* on *Sukkot*. The *gemara* asks: why was the offspring not brought by *Shavuot*? According to Rava, one *regel* was already enough to trigger a violation of the *asei*. Did they not transgress by letting *Shavuot* pass?

Mar Zutra, in the name of Rava, answers: *kegon she'hayesa cholah b'Atzeret* — perhaps the animal was ill at *Shavuot*, and an ill animal may not be brought as a *korban*; therefore the delay was justified. Rav Ashi offers a different resolution: *mai v'achalnu valdah shlamim b'chag* — what does "on the *chag*" mean? He reads *chag* as referring to the next holiday, which would be *Shavuot* itself, not *Sukkot*. The *gemara* objects that this reading is linguistically strained: *kol heicha de'tani Pesach tani Atzeret* — wherever the *tanna* mentions Pesach and then wishes to refer to *Shavuot*, he calls it *Atzeret*. If he simply says *chag*, he means *Sukkot*. So Rav Ashi's reading is rejected, and Mar Zutra's answer — illness at *Shavuot* — stands.

### Rava III: Daily

The third of Rava's statements: *v'amar Rava, keivan she'avru alav gimmel regalim, b'chol yom va-yom over b'bal te'acher* — once three *regalim* have passed, one transgresses *bal te'acher* anew every single day. The rationale is practical as well as moral: if the transgression were a one-time event, once the person is already *over*, there is no further urgency to bring the *korban*. By declaring a fresh violation each day, the Torah maintains constant pressure on the person to remedy the situation.

## Rava Severity (III)



### Baraita Challenge

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: כֵּיוָן שֶׁעָבְרוּ עָלָיו שְׁלֹשָׁה רְגָלִים, בְּכׇל יוֹם וָיוֹם עוֹבֵר בְּ״בַל תְּאַחֵר״. מֵיתִיבִי: אֶחָד בְּכוֹר, וְאֶחָד כׇּל הַקֳּדָשִׁים, כֵּיוָן שֶׁעָבְרוּ עֲלֵיהֶם שָׁנָה בְּלֹא רְגָלִים, רְגָלִים בְּלֹא שָׁנָה — עוֹבֵר בְּ״בַל תְּאַחֵר״.
> **Translation:** **Rava said: Once three Festivals have passed, every day he transgresses** the prohibition: **You shall not delay.** The Gemara **raises an objection** from that which is taught in the following *baraita*: In the case of **both a firstborn** animal **and all consecrated animals, once a year has passed without** three **Festivals**, or **three Festivals have passed without a year** elapsing, the owner **transgresses** the prohibition: **You shall not delay.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַאי מַאי תְּיוּבְתֵּיהּ? אָמַר רַב כָּהֲנָא: מַאן דְּקָא מוֹתֵיב — שַׁפִּיר קָא מוֹתֵיב: מִכְּדֵי תַּנָּא אַלָּאוֵי קָא מְהַדַּר, לִיתְנֵי: בְּכׇל יוֹם וָיוֹם עוֹבֵר בְּ״בַל תְּאַחֵר״.
> **Translation:** The Gemara first expresses its astonishment at the question itself: **What is this objection?** What is the comparison between this ruling and the statement of Rava? **Rav Kahana said: He who raises an objection** here **raises a valid objection,** and this is the way the question should be understood. **Since the *tanna*** of the *baraita* **is looking for prohibitions** to add, and he shows that the prohibition applies in additional cases, then if Rava is correct, **let** the *tanna* of the *baraita* **teach** that once three Festivals have passed **he transgresses** the prohibition: **You shall not delay, every single day.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִידַּךְ — תַּנָּא לְמִיקְבְּעֵיהּ בְּלָאו קָא מְהַדַּר, בְּלָאוֵי יְתֵירִי לָא קָא מְהַדַּר.
> **Translation:** **And** how does **the other** *amora*, Rava, explain the *baraita*? He understands that **the *tanna* is** trying **to establish** the action only **as subject to a prohibition,** i.e., he merely wishes to set the parameters of the prohibition; but once the action is established as prohibited, **he is not looking for additional prohibitions.**


The *gemara* challenges Rava's third statement from the same *baraita* cited earlier: *echad bechor v'echad kol ha-kodashim, keivan she'avru aleihem shana belo regalim, regalim belo shana, over b'val te'acher* — whether it is a *bechor* or any consecrated animal, once a year without *regalim* or *regalim* without a year have passed, one transgresses *bal te'acher*. The *gemara* reads this as implying that the transgression happens once, at the threshold, not repeatedly each day. So is there a contradiction with Rava?

Rav Kahana responds: *man dekamosi shapir kamosi* — whoever raises this question raises it well. *Mikhdei, tana alavai kamahadar* — the *tanna* of that *baraita* is trying to find every possible scenario in which one transgresses. If you were trying to exhaust all the cases, you should have added: and after three *regalim*, every single day. Since he did not say that, presumably the *tanna* does not hold like Rava.

The *gemara* pushes back: *v'idakh, tana le-mikvei b'lav kamahadar* — the other side responds that the *tanna* is only interested in defining the parameters of the *lav*, when it first takes effect. *B'lav yeteira lo kamahadar* — counting the accumulation of subsequent violations is not his concern. He defines the threshold; Rava defines the ongoing consequence. The two positions are not contradictory — they address different questions. Rava's three statements therefore stand unchallenged: *tzedakah* is immediate, an *asei* is violated after one *regel*, and after three *regalim* a new violation accrues every day.

## Calendar Problem



### Lesser Measure

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוּפָא: אֶחָד בְּכוֹר וְאֶחָד כׇּל הַקֳּדָשִׁים, כֵּיוָן שֶׁעָבְרוּ עֲלֵיהֶם שָׁנָה בְּלֹא רְגָלִים, רְגָלִים בְּלֹא שָׁנָה — עוֹבֵר בְּ״בַל תְּאַחֵר״. בִּשְׁלָמָא רְגָלִים בְּלֹא שָׁנָה מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ, אֶלָּא שָׁנָה בְּלֹא רְגָלִים הֵיכִי מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ?
> **Translation:** The Gemara proceeds to discuss **the** matter **itself,** i.e., the *baraita* cited above, which stated: In the case of **both a firstborn** animal **and all consecrated animals, once a year has passed,** even if three **Festivals** have **not** passed, or once three **Festivals have passed,** even if a whole **year** has **not** passed, the owner **transgresses** the prohibition: **You shall not delay. Granted,** it is possible to have three **Festivals without a year; you find it** because three Festivals can pass in half a year, between Passover and *Sukkot*. **But a year without** three **Festivals,** under **what circumstances** can **you find** this case? How can a year pass without three Festivals also passing?

The *gemara* now turns to analyze the *baraita* itself in more detail. *Gufa*: the *baraita* teaches that *bal te'acher* is triggered by the lesser of three *regalim* or one year. This is itself a new idea not previously encountered: *bal te'acher* is defined not by three *regalim* alone, but by whichever threshold arrives first — three *regalim* or a full year. One might ask what the "lesser" even means here, since every ordinary year contains three *regalim* within it. The *gemara* will address how that inverse case is constructed momentarily, as it depends on how one counts the year and when the *regalim* fall. The immediate point of the *baraita*, however, is clear: it does not require three *regalim kesidran* — in their fixed sequence — nor any other more demanding standard. Any three *regalim*, or a year, whichever comes first, is sufficient to trigger *bal te'acher*. *Bishlama regalim belo shana meshakchat lah* — the case of three *regalim* without a full year is easily constructed: if one makes a vow just before Pesach, then *Shavuot* and *Sukkot* follow within the same calendar year, giving three *regalim* in less than twelve months.

### Year Without Regalim

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנִיחָא לְמַאן דְּאִית לֵיהּ כְּסִדְרָן, מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ. אֶלָּא לְמַאן דְּלֵית לֵיהּ כְּסִדְרָן, הֵיכִי מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ?
> **Translation:** The Gemara clarifies the question: **This** works out **well according to the one who is of** the opinion that one transgresses the prohibition against delaying only if the three Festivals have passed **in their** proper **order. You** can **find** a year without three Festivals in their proper order, e.g., if one made his vow shortly before *Shavuot*, in which case the year will end before *Shavuot* the following year, but three Festivals in order will not have elapsed until *Sukkot* of the third year. **But according to the one who is not** of the opinion that the three Festivals must be **in their** proper **order,** under **what circumstances** can **you find** this case of a year without three Festivals?

But *ela shana belo regalim heichi meshakchat lah* — how do you construct the inverse case, a full year without three *regalim*? A normal year always contains three *regalim*. The *gemara* notes that this is easy *l'man de'it lei kesidran* — for the opinion that requires the *regalim* to pass in their proper sequence (*Pesach, Shavuot, Sukkot*). Under that view, a vow taken between *Pesach* and *Sukkot* could result in a year passing before completing the sequence *kesidran*. But for the opinion that any three *regalim* suffice, regardless of order — *l'man deleit lei kesidran* — any three *regalim* in any sequence count. How would you then construct a full year without three *regalim*?

### Rabbi Solar Year

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא לְרַבִּי מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ בְּשָׁנָה מְעוּבֶּרֶת. דְּתַנְיָא: ״שָׁנָה תְּמִימָה״, רַבִּי אוֹמֵר: מוֹנֶה שְׁלֹשׁ מֵאוֹת וְשִׁשִּׁים וַחֲמִשָּׁה יוֹם כְּמִנְיָן יְמוֹת הַחַמָּה,
> **Translation:** The Gemara clarifies again: **Granted, according to Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, **you find** a year without three Festivals **in a leap year, as it is taught** in a *baraita*: In the case of houses of walled cities, with regard to which an owner is given only one year to redeem his home if he sells it, after which it becomes the permanent possession of the purchaser, the verse states: “And if it be not redeemed within the space of **a full year”** (Leviticus 25:30). How is the year determined? **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **says: One counts 365 days like the number of the days in a solar** year, and not the usual lunar year, which is why it is called a full year.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: מוֹנֶה שְׁנֵים עָשָׂר חֹדֶשׁ מִיּוֹם לְיוֹם, וְאִם נִתְעַבְּרָה שָׁנָה — נִתְעַבְּרָה לוֹ. מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ לְרַבִּי דְּאַקְדְּשַׁהּ בָּתַר חַג הַמַּצּוֹת, דְּכִי מְטָא שִׁילְהֵי אֲדָר בָּתְרָאָה — שָׁנָה מַלְיָא, רְגָלִים לָא מְלוּ. אֶלָּא לְרַבָּנַן, הֵיכִי מַשְׁכַּחַתְּ לַהּ?
> **Translation:** **And the Rabbis** disagree and **say: One counts twelve months from day to day,** from the date of the sale until that same date twelve months later, **and if it is a leap year** with an added month, **the leap** month is **for** the seller’s benefit, i.e., he has thirteen months to redeem his house. **You** can **find** a year without three Festivals **according to Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi. How so? It is possible in a case **where one consecrated** the animal **after the festival of Passover, so that once he reaches the end of the second** month of **Adar** in a leap year, **the year is completed,** but **the Festivals are not** yet **completed,** as the third Festival is still to come. **But according to the Sages,** under **what circumstances** can **you find** this case of a year without three Festivals?

The *gemara* answers: *bishlama le-Rebbi, de'meshakchat lah b'shana me'uberet* — for Rebbi, there is a ready answer, derived from a *baraita* about the redemption of a sold house in a walled city, which must be redeemed within a "full year." Rebbi holds: *gimel me'ot v'shishim v'chamisha yamim keminian yemos hachama* — 365 days, according to the count of the solar year. The Sages hold: *monei yud-bet chodesh miyom l'yom* — twelve months from day to day, and if the year was a leap year, the seller gains the extra month.

Under Rebbi's view, a year is 365 solar days, irrespective of the Hebrew calendar. In a leap year, the Hebrew calendar adds an extra month, making the lunar year 383 or 384 days. If one sanctifies an animal right after *Pesach* in a leap year — say, on the first of *Iyar* — and defines the year as 365 days, that span of 365 days will end before the next *Pesach* arrives. Since the leap year pushed the next *Pesach* further away (because it takes approximately 384 days to reach the same date again), 365 days will expire while one is still in *Adar Beit*, before the next *Pesach*. Three *regalim* have not yet recurred — the new *Pesach* has not come — yet 365 days have passed. So according to Rebbi, one can have a full year without three *regalim*.

### Shavuot Fluctuation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּדְתָנֵי רַב שְׁמַעְיָה: עֲצֶרֶת פְּעָמִים חֲמִשָּׁה, פְּעָמִים שִׁשָּׁה, פְּעָמִים שִׁבְעָה. הָא כֵּיצַד? שְׁנֵיהֶן מְלֵאִין — חֲמִשָּׁה, שְׁנֵיהֶן חֲסֵרִין — שִׁבְעָה, אֶחָד מָלֵא וְאֶחָד חָסֵר — שִׁשָּׁה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **As Rav Shemaya taught** in a *baraita*: ***Shavuot* sometimes** occurs on **the fifth** of Sivan, **sometimes** on **the sixth** of Sivan, **and sometimes** on **the seventh. How so?** If **both** the months of Nisan and Iyyar **are full** months of thirty days each, the festival of *Shavuot*, which is celebrated fifty days after the second day of Passover, occurs on the **fifth** of Sivan; if **both** months **are short,** with twenty-nine days each, it occurs on the **seventh** of Sivan; and if **one** of them **is full and the other is short,** it occurs on **the sixth** of Sivan. According to this opinion, if both months were full and the festival of *Shavuot* occurred on the fifth day of Sivan, and one made his vow on the day after *Shavuot*, i.e., the sixth of Sivan, and in the following year both months were short, so that the festival of *Shavuot* occurred on the seventh of Sivan, a whole year would have passed without three Festivals.

But for the Sages, who define a year by the same calendar date twelve (or thirteen) months later, the problem remains: under a normal calendar, the same date always brings you back after three *regalim*. The *gemara* resolves this by citing a teaching of Rabbi Shemaya: *Atzeret pe'amim heh, pe'amim vav, pe'amim zayin* — *Shavuot* can fall on the 5th, 6th, or 7th of Sivan, depending on whether *Nisan* and *Iyar* are both full months (30 days each, giving the 5th), both deficient (29 days each, giving the 7th), or one full and one deficient (giving the 6th).

This fluctuation creates the possibility of a full year without *Shavuot* occurring again on or before its previous date. If one took a vow on the 6th of *Sivan* in a year when *Shavuot* fell on the 5th, and the following year *Shavuot* falls on the 7th, then when the anniversary arrives — the 6th of *Sivan* of the following year — *Shavuot* has not yet occurred that year. The 6th of *Sivan* has come again (completing a full lunar year from the same date), but the festival of *Shavuot* is still one day away. A full year has elapsed without three *regalim* having passed through that date.

### Acherim Fixed

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַאן תַּנָּא דִּפְלִיג עֲלֵיהּ דְּרַב שְׁמַעְיָה — אֲחֵרִים הִיא. דְּתַנְיָא, אֲחֵרִים אוֹמְרִים: אֵין בֵּין עֲצֶרֶת לַעֲצֶרֶת, אֵין בֵּין רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה לְרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה, אֶלָּא אַרְבָּעָה יָמִים בִּלְבַד. וְאִם הָיְתָה שָׁנָה מְעוּבֶּרֶת — חֲמִשָּׁה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara comments: **And who is the *tanna*** who **disagrees with Rav Shemaya** and says that a year has a fixed number of days? **It is *Aḥerim*, as it is taught** in a *baraita*: ***Aḥerim* say: The difference between *Shavuot*** of one year and ***Shavuot*** of the following year, and similarly, **between Rosh HaShana** of one year and **Rosh HaShana** of the following year, **is only four days** of the week. There are 354 days in a lunar year, which are divided into twelve alternating months, six months that are thirty days long and six months that are twenty-nine days long. Therefore, every year is fifty weeks and four days long. **And if it were a leap year,** in which case the year is comprised of 383 days, or fifty-four weeks and five days, there is a difference of **five** days between them.

The *gemara* then asks: who argues with Rabbi Shemaya's position that the date of *Shavuot* can fluctuate? The answer is *Acherim* — identified as Rabbi Meir — who hold that the holidays shift by a fixed number of days each year. A *baraita* teaches: *Acherim omrim, ein bein Atzeret l'Atzeret, ein bein Rosh Hashanah l'Rosh Hashanah ela arba yamim bilvad* — from one year's *Shavuot* to the next, the calendar shifts by exactly four days. This is because a Hebrew year contains 354 days, and dividing by seven leaves a remainder of four. So each subsequent year's holidays fall four weekdays later than the previous year's. *V'im haysa shanah me'uberet, chamisha* — in a leap year, the shift is five days rather than four, because the added month is always a fixed 29-day month, and 29 divided by seven leaves a remainder of one, adding one more day to the shift.

The elegance of the *Acherim* system lies in its perfect regularity — no month-length variation, no fluctuation in the date of *Shavuot*. One might ask why such a clean calendar was not adopted in practice. The answer, as noted, is that other constraints — such as the prohibition against *Rosh Hashanah* falling on certain days of the week — require the flexibility of variable month lengths, and that system is what prevents adoption of the fully fixed calendar that *Acherim* envision.
