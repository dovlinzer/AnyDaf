## Olat Re'iyah vs. Shalmei



### Minimum Comparison

The daf opens by reviewing the debate between *Beit Shammai* and *Beit Hillel* concerning which of two obligatory festival offerings carries the higher minimum: the *olat re'iyah* or the *shalmei chagigah*. Both offerings are required on *yom tov*, and the positions are mirror images of one another: *Beit Shammai* holds that the *olat re'iyah* carries a higher minimum of two silver coins, while the *chagigah* requires only one *ma'ah*; *Beit Hillel* reverses this. Together, both schools require a total outlay of three silver coins — only the allocation between the two *korbanot* differs.

### Divine vs. Human Benefit

What is striking is that underlying this technical dispute lies a deeper philosophical orientation that characterizes many of the debates between *Beit Shammai* and *Beit Hillel* — not merely a question of leniency versus stringency, but a question of emphasis. *Beit Shammai* directs more expenditure toward the *olah*, which is given entirely to God, while *Beit Hillel* directs more toward the *shlamim*, which is shared between God and the worshipper. The degree to which the human being factors into religious experience appears to be at the core of this disagreement.

### Shlamim Before Sinai

One of the proofs marshaled in support of *Beit Hillel* is drawn from the closing verses of *Mishpatim*, which *Chazal* understand to have taken place before *matan Torah*. There it says: *vayizbachu olot vayizbachu zvachim shlamim l'Hashem parim* — they brought *olot* and they brought *shlamim* to God, bulls. *Beit Hillel* argues from this verse that *shlamim* existed as a form of personal offering before *matan Torah*, whereas the *olah* present at that moment did not represent a personal offering in the same sense. This gives precedential weight to the *shalmei chagigah*.

### Both Existed Before Sinai

*Beit Shammai* counters that the verse in fact mentions both an *olah* and a *shlamim*, and that both were private offerings — so no special weight can be granted to the *shlamim* on the basis of this precedent alone.

### Type of Olah Before Sinai

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קָסָבְרִי בֵּית הִלֵּל: עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר — עוֹלַת תָּמִיד הֲוַאי. וּבֵית שַׁמַּאי סָבְרִי: עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר — עוֹלַת רְאִיָּיה הֲוַאי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **Beit Hillel hold** that **the burnt**-offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert** at Mount Sinai **was the daily burnt**-offering, which is a communal offering, as there were no individual burnt-offerings before the giving of the Torah. **And Beit Shammai hold** that **the burnt**-offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert** at Mount Sinai **was a burnt**-offering **of appearance,** which is an individual offering.

The *gemara* then clarifies what precisely is being disputed: the question is whether those *olot* mentioned at the foot of Har Sinai were an *olat re'iyah* — a personal, volitional offering — or an *olat tamid*, the regular communal burnt offering. If the *olah* brought was merely the *olat tamid*, then it carries no weight as a model for a personal *korban*, and *Beit Hillel*'s argument stands: the only personal precedent we have is the *shlamim*. *Beit Shammai*, by contrast, maintains that those *olot* were indeed personal offerings — an *olat re'iyah* — and therefore the personal *olah* also has a pre-Sinaitic precedent, undermining *Beit Hillel*'s reasoning.

It is worth noting how strange the argument structure is in either direction. Generally, what occurred before *matan Torah* is considered legally irrelevant. Yet here, both sides treat the pre-Sinaitic sacrifice as an authoritative model. The argument implicitly restricts its view to the era of *B'nei Yisrael* and yet grants special weight to what occurred before the giving of the Torah — a genuinely unusual move.

## Three vs. Three Dispute



### Olat Re'iyah Position

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: בֵּית שַׁמַּאי, וְרַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר, וְרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל, כּוּלְּהוּ סְבִירָא לְהוּ: עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר — עוֹלַת רְאִיָּיה הֲוַאי. וּבֵית הִלֵּל, וְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא, וְרַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי, כּוּלְּהוּ סְבִירָא לְהוּ: עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר — עוֹלַת תָּמִיד הֲוַאי.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said: Beit Shammai, Rabbi Elazar, and Rabbi Yishmael all hold** that **the burnt**-offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert** at Mount Sinai **was a burnt**-offering **of appearance. And Beit Hillel, Rabbi Akiva, and Rabbi Yosei HaGelili all hold** that **the burnt**-offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert** at Mount Sinai **was a daily burnt**-offering, not an individual offering.

*Abaye* now broadens the discussion considerably. He observes that the dispute between *Beit Shammai* and *Beit Hillel* about the nature of that pre-Sinaitic *olah* is not isolated — it aligns with positions held by other *tanna'im* on related questions. *Beit Shammai*, Rabbi Eliezer, and Rabbi Yishmael all share the view that the *olah* brought by *Yisrael* in the wilderness was an *olat re'iyah* — a personal offering.

### Olat Tamid Position

On the other side, *Beit Hillel*, Rabbi Akiva, and Rabbi Yose HaGlili all hold that the *olah* brought was an *olat tamid* — the communal offering — and not a personal one.

[DAF:6b]
### Rabbi Yishmael: Klal/Prat

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בֵּית שַׁמַּאי — הָא דַּאֲמַרַן. רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל — דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל אוֹמֵר: כְּלָלוֹת נֶאֶמְרוּ בְּסִינַי,
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the source for each opinion. **Beit Shammai** is **that which we said. Rabbi Yishmael, as it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Yishmael says: General statements were said at Sinai,** i.e., Moses received general mitzvot at Sinai, including the Ten Commandments.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּפְרָטוֹת בְּאֹהֶל מוֹעֵד. וְרַבִּי עֲקִיבָא אוֹמֵר: כְּלָלוֹת וּפְרָטוֹת נֶאֶמְרוּ בְּסִינַי, וְנִשְׁנוּ בְּאֹהֶל מוֹעֵד, וְנִשְׁתַּלְּשׁוּ בְּעַרְבוֹת מוֹאָב. וְאִי סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר — עוֹלַת תָּמִיד הֲוַאי,
> **Translation:** **And the details** of the mitzvot, e.g., the particulars of the sacrificial process, were said to Moses at a later time **in the Tent of Meeting. And Rabbi Akiva says:** Both **general statements and the details** of mitzvot **were said at Sinai and** later **repeated in the Tent of Meeting, and** reiterated **a third time** by Moses to the Jewish people **in the plains of Moab,** as recorded in the book of Deuteronomy. **And if it enters your mind** to say that Rabbi Yishmael holds that **the burnt**-offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert** at Mount Sinai **was the daily burnt**-offering, rather than the burnt-offering of an individual, the following question arises:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִי אִיכָּא מִידֵּי דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא לָא בָּעֵי הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ, וּלְבַסּוֹף בָּעֵי הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ?!
> **Translation:** **Is there any** offering **that initially did not require skinning and cutting** into pieces, as these details of the daily burnt-offering were transmitted later in the Tent of Meeting, **and ultimately,** when these details were added, the offering **required skinning and cutting?** It is not plausible that the details of a mitzva would change over time. Therefore, it is clear that according to the opinion of Rabbi Yishmael the Jews did not sacrifice the daily burnt-offering before the giving of the Torah, which means that the burnt-offering sacrificed at Mount Sinai must have been a burnt-offering of appearance.

The *baraita* cited to establish Rabbi Yishmael's position concerns his famous theory of how the Torah was transmitted. Rabbi Yishmael holds: *klalot ne'emru b'Sinai, u'pratot b'Ohel Moed* — the general principles were stated at Har Sinai, but the specific details were only spelled out later, in the *Ohel Moed*, during the ongoing revelation described throughout *Vayikra* and the subsequent books. This continued revelation — *v'no'aditi lecha sham*, I will meet you there, *v'dibarti itcha me'al hakapores* — is what filled in the legal particulars after Sinai.

### Rabbi Akiva: Sinai

Rabbi Akiva disagrees: *klalot u'pratot ne'emru b'Sinai* — both the general principles and the specific details were given at Har Sinai; they were then repeated in the *Ohel Moed*, and repeated a third time in *Arvot Moav*. The repetitions were pedagogical, not revelatory — the content was already fully given at Sinai.

### Hermeneutic Philosophies

This debate inverts what one might expect from these two *tanna'im* in their broader hermeneutic orientations. Rabbi Akiva is generally associated with the method of deriving detailed laws from the smallest textual indicators — every extra letter, every tittle — while Rabbi Yishmael tends toward *pshat* and cautions against over-interpretation, holding that *dibra Torah kilshon bnei adam*. Yet here, it is Rabbi Akiva who says all details were given at Sinai, and Rabbi Yishmael who says they were only given as they appear explicitly in the text, later.

One way to resolve this apparent inversion is to read Rabbi Akiva's position as consistent with his broader hermeneutics: for Rabbi Akiva, "giving the details at Sinai" does not mean spelling them out explicitly, but encoding them in the text through all those minute hints and extra letters — which the *tanna'im* are then licensed to derive. The giving of the details *is* the giving of the text, and the human interpreter then draws out the particulars. For Rabbi Yishmael, by contrast, "giving the details" means actually stating them — and that did not happen until the explicit legislation of the *Ohel Moed*. This reading aligns elegantly with each authority's general interpretive stance.

## Transmission Theory Olat



### The Logical Problem

The alignment becomes significant when applied to the practical question: if the *olah* brought at Har Sinai was an *olat tamid*, and if the details of how to bring an *olah* were only given when they appear explicitly in the Torah — as Rabbi Yishmael holds — then the *olat tamid* was brought at Sinai without adherence to the full procedural requirements. The *gemara* asks: *mi ka midi d'me'ikara lo b'hefshet v'nituach u'levasof baei hefshet v'nituach* — can there be something that was originally performed without flaying and dismembering, and only later was it required to be done that way? The flaying and dismembering of the *olah* are cardinal elements of how an *olah* is brought, and it is inconceivable that the *olat tamid* was brought without them initially and then subsequently modified.

This presents a problem specifically for Rabbi Yishmael: if he also held that the *olah* was an *olat tamid*, he would have to concede that it was brought without its essential procedural details, which seems untenable. This is why the *gemara* initially places Rabbi Yishmael in the camp of *Beit Shammai* — by saying the *olah* was an *olat re'iyah*, a personal offering, he avoids the problem of the *olat tamid* being brought without its full specifications.

### Tosafot's Solution

*Tosafot* sharpens the logic here. They note that the procedural requirement of flaying and dismembering (*hefshet v'nituach*) is not a general rule stated abstractly about all *olot*, but is spelled out specifically in the context of the *olat tamid* itself. It is therefore particularly inconceivable that the *olat tamid* — the very *korban* about which the Torah later explicitly specifies these steps — could have been brought earlier without following them. This would not be as troubling for a private *olah* such as the *olat re'iyah*, since although all *olot* technically require *hefshet v'nituach*, the explicit textual connection is to the *tamid*. The *gemara* finds this reasoning persuasive.

### Elazar: Mitzvah at Sinai

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר — דְּתַנְיָא: ״עוֹלַת תָּמִיד הָעֲשׂוּיָה בְּהַר סִינַי״, רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר אוֹמֵר: מַעֲשֶׂיהָ נֶאֶמְרוּ בְּסִינַי, וְהִיא עַצְמָהּ לֹא קָרְבָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites the source for the opinion of **Rabbi Elazar. As it is taught** in a *baraita*: **“It is a daily burnt-**offering, **which was performed on Mount Sinai”** (Numbers 28:6). **Rabbi Elazar says:** The details of **its performance were said at Sinai, but it itself was not sacrificed** until the Tabernacle was erected. This indicates that the offering brought on Mount Sinai was a burnt-offering of appearance.

A *baraita* is now cited concerning Rabbi Elazar. It invokes the phrase *olat tamid ha'asuyah b'Har Sinai* — "the *olat tamid* performed at Har Sinai" — a phrase that occurs in the liturgy and might seem on its face to be excellent evidence that a *tamid* was indeed offered at the foot of Sinai. Yet Rabbi Elazar reads this in a striking way: *ma'aseha ne'emru b'Sinai v'atzma lo karvah* — the procedure of the *olah* was commanded at Sinai, but the offering itself was not brought there. The phrase *asuyah b'Har Sinai* refers to the commandment being given at Sinai, not to the sacrifice being performed there. For Rabbi Elazar, then, there was no actual *olat tamid* at Sinai — only the command — and therefore the *olah* that was brought, alongside the *shlamim* mentioned in *Mishpatim*, was a personal offering, an *olat re'iyah*.

### Rabbi Akiva's Position

Rabbi Akiva takes the opposite view: *karvah v'shuv lo paskah* — it was offered at Har Sinai and continued without interruption thereafter. For Rabbi Akiva, both the commandment and the act of bringing the *olat tamid* occurred at Sinai.

### Forty-Year Question

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא אוֹמֵר: קָרְבָה, וְשׁוּב לֹא פָּסְקָה. אֶלָּא מָה אֲנִי מְקַיֵּים ״הַזְּבָחִים וּמִנְחָה הִגַּשְׁתֶּם לִי בַמִּדְבָּר אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל״ —
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Akiva says: It was sacrificed** when they stood at Mount Sinai **and** its sacrifice **never ceased.** The Gemara asks: **But** if so, **how do I uphold,** i.e., how does Rabbi Akiva explain the following verse: **“Did you bring to Me sacrifices and offerings for forty years in the wilderness, house of Israel?”** (Amos 5:25). This verse indicates that they did not sacrifice these offerings.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שִׁבְטוֹ שֶׁל לֵוִי שֶׁלֹּא עָבְדוּ עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה, הֵן הִקְרִיבוּ אוֹתָהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **The tribe of Levi, which did not commit** the sin of **idol worship, sacrificed it** from their own funds. Since the rest of the Jewish people did not contribute the funds for the daily burnt-offering, it is as though they did not sacrifice this offering. This concludes the list of sources of the opinions of those Sages who hold that the daily burnt-offering was not sacrificed at Mount Sinai, and the offering that was sacrificed there was a burnt-offering of appearance.

This raises the question of the verse in *Amos* that reads rhetorically: did you bring *zevachim u'minchah* to Me for forty years in the wilderness, *Beit Yisrael*? The implication of the rhetorical question is that they did not. But if the *tamid* was brought starting at Sinai, how can the verse suggest otherwise? The *gemara* resolves this by saying the verse refers to the nation as a whole: only the *shevet Levi*, who had not served idolatry, brought these offerings; the rest of *Yisrael* were disqualified. The forty-year question therefore does not undermine Rabbi Akiva's position. And yet, as the lecturer notes, the verse remains somewhat strange even on its own terms — for even if one accepts Rabbi Elazar's view that the *tamid* did not begin at Sinai, *Yisrael* still brought the *olat tamid* starting from the second year in the wilderness, meaning the rhetorical force of "forty years" is puzzling regardless: at most, you did not bring it for forty years — only thirty-nine.

## Yose HaGlili: Olat Tamid



### Three Regalim Mitzvot

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בֵּית הִלֵּל — הָא דַּאֲמַרַן. רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא — הָא נָמֵי דַּאֲמַרַן. רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי — דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי אוֹמֵר: שָׁלֹשׁ מִצְוֹת נִצְטַוּוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּעֲלוֹתָם לָרֶגֶל: רְאִיָּיה, וַחֲגִיגָה, וְשִׂמְחָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara cites the sources for the opinions that the daily burnt-offering was sacrificed at Mount Sinai. **Beit Hillel: That which we said. Rabbi Akiva: Also that which we said,** in the aforementioned dispute with Rabbi Elazar. **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili: As it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili says: The Jewish people were commanded** to perform **three mitzvot when they ascended** to the Temple **for** the pilgrimage **Festivals:** The burnt-offering of **appearance, and the Festival peace**-offering, **and the** peace-offering of **rejoicing.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יֵשׁ בָּרְאִיָּיה שֶׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן; וְיֵשׁ בַּחֲגִיגָה שֶׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן; יֵשׁ בַּשִּׂמְחָה שֶׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן. יֵשׁ בָּרְאִיָּיה שֶׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן — שֶׁהָרְאִיָּיה עוֹלָה כּוּלָּהּ לַגָּבוֹהַּ, מַה שֶּׁאֵין כֵּן בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן.
> **Translation:** Rabbi Yosei HaGelili continues. **There is** an element **of the** burnt-offering of **appearance that is not** present **in** the other **two; there is** an element **of the Festival peace**-offering **that is not** present **in** the other **two;** and **there is** an element **of the** peace-offering of **rejoicing that is not** present **in** the other **two.** He elaborates: **There is** an element **of the** burnt-offering of **appearance that is not** present **in** the other **two, as the** burnt-offering of **appearance** goes **up entirely to God, which is not so with regard to** the other **two,** as the majority portion of the other two offerings is eaten.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יֵשׁ בַּחֲגִיגָה מַה שֶּׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן, שֶׁחֲגִיגָה יֶשְׁנָהּ לִפְנֵי הַדִּיבּוּר, מַה שֶּׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן. יֵשׁ בַּשִּׂמְחָה מַה שֶּׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן, שֶׁהַשִּׂמְחָה נוֹהֶגֶת בַּאֲנָשִׁים וּבְנָשִׁים, מַה שֶּׁאֵין בִּשְׁתֵּיהֶן.
> **Translation:** **There is** an element **of the Festival peace**-offering **that is not** present **in** the other **two, as the Festival peace**-offering **existed before the speech** of God at Mount Sinai, **which is not so with regard to** the other **two.** Finally, **there is** an element **of the** peace-offering of **rejoicing that is not** present **in** the other **two, as the** peace-offering of **rejoicing is performed by** both **men and by women, which is not so with regard to** the other **two.** This shows that Rabbi Yosei HaGelili holds that the burnt-offering of appearance was not sacrificed at Mount Sinai, which means that the burnt-offering mentioned in that context must have been the daily burnt-offering.

The *baraita* of Rabbi Yose HaGlili articulates three distinct *mitzvot* incumbent upon *Yisrael* when ascending for *aliyah la'regel*: *re'iyah*, appearing before God in the *mikdash*; *chagigah*, celebrating through the *korban chagigah*; and *simchah*, rejoicing through the consumption of the *korban simchah*. Each of these three *mitzvot* possesses a distinctive characteristic not shared by the other two. The *re'iyah* is unique in that its offering is an *olah* — given entirely to God — while the other two are *shlamim*. The *simchah* is unique in that it applies to both men and women, unlike the other two.

### Chagigah Pre-Sinai

What the *chagigah* alone possesses — what neither *re'iyah* nor *simchah* can claim — is that *chagigah yeshnam lifnei ha'dibbur*: the *chagigah* existed before *matan Torah*. The *re'iyah* and the *simchah* did not. This is the same logical move as *Beit Hillel*'s argument: only the *shalmei chagigah* has a pre-Sinaitic precedent, which is why it is accorded special weight.

### Logical Inference

The implicit conclusion from Rabbi Yose HaGlili's *baraita* is that if only the *chagigah* — a *shlamim* — existed before the *dibbur*, then the *olah* mentioned alongside the *shlamim* in *Mishpatim* was not a personal *olat re'iyah* but rather the communal *olat tamid*. Rabbi Yose HaGlili thus aligns with *Beit Hillel*'s reading without stating it explicitly. One might wonder why the *shlamim* at the foot of Sinai should be identified specifically as a *shalmei chagigah* rather than a *shalmei simchah* — the text gives no clear indication — but the *gemara* proceeds on this understanding without offering a resolution.

## Rabbi Yishmael Realigned



### Challenge to Yishmael


With the three-against-three alignment established, the *gemara* now scrutinizes the placement of Rabbi Yishmael among the *Beit Shammai* camp. The argument was that because Rabbi Yishmael holds the details were only given as they appear explicitly in the text, he could not have maintained that the *olat tamid* was brought before those details were given. The problem with bringing the *tamid* without *hefshet v'nituach* seemed to force him to conclude that no *olat tamid* was brought at Sinai, and hence the *olah* must have been a personal *olat re'iyah*. It is worth noting that this difficulty applies with particular force to the *olat tamid* specifically: a private *olah* offered before the *dibbur* failing to observe later-prescribed details is one thing, but the flagship communal offering evolving in its procedural requirements is a far more striking claim. The *gemara* presses back, however, suggesting that perhaps Rabbi Yishmael could have permitted precisely this — that the *olat tamid* was indeed brought at Sinai but in a manner different from its ultimate form, a position that turns out to be demonstrably coherent, as the case of Rabbi Yose HaGlili will show.

### Yose HaGlili Precedent

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל, מַאי טַעְמָא קָא מוֹקְמַתְּ לֵיהּ כְּבֵית שַׁמַּאי? אִי סָלְקָא דַּעְתָּךְ עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר עוֹלַת תָּמִיד הֲוַאי, מִי אִיכָּא מִידֵּי דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא לָא בָּעֵי הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ, וּלְבַסּוֹף בָּעֵי הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ?!
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks a question with regard to Abaye’s explanation. **And** with regard to **Rabbi Yishmael, what is the reason** that **you established** his ruling **in accordance with** the opinion of **Beit Shammai** that the burnt-offering sacrificed at Mount Sinai was a burnt-offering of appearance? The explanation for his opinion was: **If it enters your mind** that the **burnt**-offering **the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert was a daily burnt**-offering, **is there any** offering **that initially did not require skinning and cutting** into pieces **and ultimately required skinning and cutting** into pieces?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי, דְּאָמַר: עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר — עוֹלַת תָּמִיד הֲוַאי: מֵעִיקָּרָא לָא בָּעֵי הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ, וּלְבַסּוֹף בָּעֵי הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ.
> **Translation:** **But wasn’t** it **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili who said: The burnt**-offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert** at Mount Sinai **was the daily burnt**-offering? Nevertheless, he holds that **initially** it **did not require skinning and cutting** into pieces, **and ultimately** it **required skinning and cutting** into pieces.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא, רַבִּי יוֹסֵי הַגְּלִילִי אוֹמֵר: עוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר אֵינָהּ טְעוּנָה הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ, לְפִי שֶׁאֵין הֶפְשֵׁט וְנִיתּוּחַ אֶלָּא מֵאֹהֶל מוֹעֵד וְאֵילָךְ! סְמִי מִכָּאן רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל.
> **Translation:** **As it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili says: The burnt-**offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert did not require skinning and cutting** into pieces, **because** the requirement of **skinning and cutting** offerings applied **only from** the time God commanded this mitzva in **the Tent of Meeting and onward.** The Gemara concludes: There is no clear evidence as to what Rabbi Yishmael actually maintains in this regard, and therefore one should **delete Rabbi Yishmael from** this list **here,** i.e., the list of those who hold that the burnt-offering sacrificed in the desert was a burnt-offering of appearance.

The *gemara* challenges this inference. Rabbi Yose HaGlili — who explicitly holds that the *olah* brought in the wilderness was the *olat tamid* — also holds, in a separate *baraita*, that the *olah* they brought in the wilderness did not require *hefshet v'nituach*: *lefi she'ein hefshet v'nituach ela me'Ohel Moed v'eilach* — those procedural requirements only became operative from the *Ohel Moed* onward. Here is explicit evidence that one can consistently hold both that the *olah* was an *olat tamid* and that it was brought without the later-specified procedural requirements. The manner in which the *tamid* was brought evolved over time as its details were revealed. This is entirely compatible with Rabbi Yishmael's theory of Torah transmission.

### Gemara's Conclusion

Once the *gemara* establishes this precedent from Rabbi Yose HaGlili's *baraita*, it concludes that the concern which seemed to force Rabbi Yishmael into the *Beit Shammai* camp is no longer compelling. Rabbi Yishmael's theory — that details are given as they appear — permits the *olat tamid* to have been brought differently before those details were given. He can therefore hold, like *Beit Hillel*, that the *olah* at Sinai was an *olat tamid*, not a personal *olat re'iyah*. The *gemara* removes Rabbi Yishmael from the *Beit Shammai* column and reassigns him to *Beit Hillel*.

The broader debate between Rabbi Yishmael and Rabbi Akiva about how the Torah was transmitted — whether details were implicit at Sinai or only given explicitly later — emerges here as something more than an abstract theory. It has direct bearing on how we understand the history of the *korbanot* and on the legal weight we give to pre-Sinaitic precedents. Viewed against the backdrop of the *Beit Shammai*–*Beit Hillel* dispute, there is a certain power to *Beit Shammai*'s reading: identifying the *olah* at the foot of Sinai as an *olat re'iyah* brings a vivid, experiential imagery to what *re'iyah* means — appearing before God in a moment of direct encounter, as captured in the verse *vayichezu et ha'Elohim*. According to *Beit Hillel*, by contrast, what was enacted at Sinai was the *olat tamid* — the steady, daily service — which suggests a different kind of religious weight: not the intense singular encounter but the continuity and regularity of daily devotion. In this sense, holding the two positions up against each other is itself illuminating: *Beit Shammai* highlights the once-in-a-season metaphysical high of direct encounter with God, while *Beit Hillel* locates the paradigmatic act of worship in that quieter, undramatic gesture of bringing an *olah* every morning and every afternoon, day after day — and it is that model, *Beit Hillel* seems to suggest, that was already present at the foot of Sinai.

## Parsing the Verse



### Textual Punctuation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בָּעֵי רַב חִסְדָּא: הַאי קְרָא הֵיכִי כְּתִיב? ״וַיִּשְׁלַח אֶת נַעֲרֵי בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל וַיַּעֲלוּ עוֹלוֹת״ — כְּבָשִׂים, ״וַיִּזְבְּחוּ זְבָחִים שְׁלָמִים לַה׳״ — ״פָּרִים״. אוֹ דִּלְמָא: אִידֵּי וְאִידֵּי פָּרִים הֲווֹ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Ḥisda raises a dilemma: This verse, how is it written,** i.e., how should it be understood? Should the following verse be read as two separate halves, with the first part consisting of: **“And he sent the young men of the children of Israel, and they sacrificed burnt-**offerings**”** (Exodus 24:5), which were **sheep;** and the second part consisting of the rest of the verse: **“And they sacrificed peace-**offerings **of bulls to the Lord,”** i.e., these peace-offerings alone were bulls? **Or perhaps both of these** were **bulls,** as the term: “Bulls,” refers both to the burnt-offerings and the peace-offerings.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְמַאי נָפְקָא מִינַּהּ? מָר זוּטְרָא אָמַר: לְפִיסּוּק טְעָמִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is the practical difference** between the two readings? **Mar Zutra said:** The practical difference is with regard **to the punctuation of** the cantillation **notes,** whether there should be a break in the verse after: “And they sacrificed burnt-offerings,” indicating that these offerings consisted of sheep; or whether it should read: “And they sacrificed burnt-offerings and sacrificed peace-offerings of bulls,” as one clause.

Rav Chisda raises a question about how to read the verse at the end of *Mishpatim* that describes the sacrifices at the foot of Sinai: *vayishlach et na'arei b'nei Yisrael vaya'alu olot vayizbechu zvachim shlamim l'Hashem parim*. The question is whether there is a syntactic break — effectively a period — after the word *olot*, such that the word *parim* (bulls) applies only to the *shlamim* that follow, with the *olah* remaining unspecified as to species. Alternatively, perhaps *parim* applies to the entire verse, and both the *olot* and the *shlamim* were bulls.

### Tropp Analysis

Mar Zutra answers that the resolution depends on the *trop* — the cantillation marks. Whether an *etnachtah* or a lesser disjunctive accent appears after *olot* determines whether the word *parim* falls back over the entire verse or only on the latter clause.

### Vow Formula Test

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אַחָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּרָבָא אָמַר: לָאוֹמֵר ״הֲרֵי עָלַי עוֹלָה כָּעוֹלָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר״, מַאי? פָּרִים הֲווֹ, אוֹ כְּבָשִׂים הֲווֹ?! תֵּיקוּ.
> **Translation:** **Rav Aḥa, son of Rava, said** that the difference between these two readings of the verse is **for one who says** in the form of a vow: It is incumbent **upon me** to bring **a burnt**-offering **like the burnt**-offering **that the Jewish people sacrificed in the desert** at Mount Sinai. **What** is he required to bring? **Were they bulls** or **were they sheep?** The Gemara does not provide an answer and states that the question **shall stand** unresolved.

Rav Acha the son of Rava proposes a practical *nafka mina*: if someone declares a vow to bring an *olah* "like the *olah* brought in the wilderness," would that vow be fulfilled with a bull (*par*) or a sheep (*keves*)? The answer depends on what species the wilderness *olah* actually was. If *parim* covers the *olot* as well, the vow requires a bull; if the *olah* is left unspecified, it might be a sheep.

### Unresolved Answer

The *gemara* concludes with *teiku* — the question remains unresolved. Even granting the reading that the *olah* and *shlamim* are syntactically separated, the species of the *olah* cannot be determined from the verse alone.

## Re'iyah: No Min/Max



### Mishnaic Teaching

The *gemara* now turns to a new dimension of the *korban re'iyah* — the question of whether it has a *shiur*, a prescribed limit. The *mishnah* in the first chapter of *Peah*, recited daily in *davening*, lists certain *mitzvot* that have no *shiur*: *ha'peah, ha'bikkurim, v'hara'ayon, u'gemilut chasadim* — *peah*, *bikkurim*, *ra'ayon* (i.e., *re'iyah*), and acts of loving-kindness. The unusual term *ra'ayon* rather than *re'iyah* will be addressed shortly.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תְּנַן הָתָם, אֵלּוּ דְּבָרִים שֶׁאֵין לָהֶם שִׁיעוּר:
> **Translation:** **We learned** in a mishna **there** (*Pe’a* 1:1): **These are** the mitzvot **that have no measure:**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַפֵּאָה, וְהַבִּכּוּרִים, וְהָרֵאָיוֹן, וּגְמִילוּת חֲסָדִים, וְתַלְמוּד תּוֹרָה.
> **Translation:** **Produce in the corner of the field** that must be left unharvested, **which is given to the poor** [***pe’a***]; **and the first fruits,** which are brought to the Temple; **and the appearance** in the Temple on the pilgrim Festivals; **and acts of loving-kindness; and Torah study.**

### Initial Interpretation


Rabbi Yochanan's initial reading of this *mishnah* is that *ein lo shiur* means there is no upper limit — one may spend as much as one wishes on these *mitzvot* — but there is a lower limit, a minimum below which one cannot go. Applied to *re'iyah*, one can bring as lavish a *korban* as one desires, but there is a floor.

Yet this initial framing already raises a deeper question worth pausing on: what exactly does it mean for these *mitzvot* to have no *shiur*? The issue turns out to be genuinely ambiguous. Consider *peah*: it clearly has no lower limit — one may leave even the smallest corner of one's field. But does it have an upper limit? One might have assumed the answer is obviously yes — you cannot make the entire field *peah* — but in fact the *Yerushalmi* entertains the surprising possibility that after one cuts even a single stalk, fulfilling the verse *bekutzrechem*, the obligation is triggered, and virtually the entire remainder could in principle be designated as *peah*. There are other opinions holding that *peah* cannot exceed half the field, so there are views that affirm an upper limit; but the point is that it is contested. The same question applies to *bikkurim*: is one permitted, in principle, to designate one's entire tree or field as first fruits? There is actually an opinion, cited by *Tosafot*, attributed to Rabbi Yochanan himself, *oseh adam kol sadehu bikkurim* — that a person may make his entire field *bikkurim* — which is striking, since one might have assumed "first fruits" implies fruits designated relative to others not so designated. Similarly, for *mitzvot* like *gemilut chasadim* and *Talmud Torah*, the natural reading of "no *shiur*" may gesture at something more expansive than merely the absence of a lower minimum. All of this is to say that when the *mishnah* declares these *mitzvot* to have no *shiur*, the precise direction of that limitlessness — whether it addresses the floor, the ceiling, or both — is far from self-evident, and the same ambiguity will carry directly into the *gemara*'s discussion of *re'iyah*, as Rabbi Oshaia's teaching will make clear.

### Torah vs. Rabbinic Limits

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: כִּסְבוּרִין אָנוּ לוֹמַר, הָרֵאָיוֹן אֵין לוֹ שִׁיעוּר — לְמַעְלָה, אֲבָל יֵשׁ לוֹ שִׁיעוּר — לְמַטָּה, עַד שֶׁבָּא רַבִּי אוֹשַׁעְיָא בְּרַבִּי, וְלִימֵּד: הָרֵאָיוֹן אֵין לוֹ שִׁיעוּר לֹא לְמַעְלָה וְלֹא לְמַטָּה. אֲבָל חֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: הָרְאִיָּיה מָעָה כֶּסֶף, וְהַחֲגִיגָה שְׁתֵּי כֶסֶף.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yoḥanan said: We thought to say that the** mitzva of **appearance** in the Temple **does not have a maximum measure, but it does have a minimum measure; until Rabbi Oshaya the Distinguished came and taught: The** mitzva of **appearance has neither a maximum nor a minimum measure. However, the Rabbis say:** The burnt-offering of **appearance** must be worth one **silver *ma’a* and the Festival peace**-offering **two silver** coins.

Rabbi Oshaia then teaches a more expansive position: *hara'ayon ein lo shiur lo lema'alah v'lo lemata* — neither an upper limit nor a lower limit. At the biblical level, one may spend as little or as much as one wishes on the *re'iyah*. The apparent lower limits familiar from the *mishnah* — the *ma'ah kesef* for *re'iyah* and the *shtei kesef* for *chagigah* — are not of biblical origin. Rather, *aval Chachamim omrim*, the Sages established a minimum. Rabbi Oshaia's teaching thus reframes the *mishnah*: what it denies is any biblical *shiur*; the rabbinic minimum is a separate layer. This reading presumably applies in parallel fashion to *peah* and *bikkurim* as well — the biblical obligation has no minimum, while the Sages prescribed a floor.

## Defining Re'iyah



### Re'ayon Meaning

The *mishnah*'s unusual term *ra'ayon* prompts a dispute. What precisely does the obligation of *re'iyah* require?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי הָרֵאָיוֹן? רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: רְאִיַּית פָּנִים בַּעֲזָרָה, וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ אָמַר: רְאִיַּית פָּנִים בְּקׇרְבָּן.
> **Translation:** § **What is the** mitzva of **appearance** to which this mishna is referring? **Rabbi Yoḥanan said:** It is the mitzva of **the appearance of** one’s **face in** the Temple **courtyard.** One may appear in the Temple as often as he likes over the course of a Festival. **And Reish Lakish said:** It is **the appearance of** one’s **face** in the Temple **with an offering.** There is no measure in this regard, as every time one appears in the Temple during a Festival he must bring an offering.

### Rabbi Yochanan's View


Rabbi Yochanan explains: *re'iyat panim ba'azarah* — the *mitzvah* is fulfilled by appearing in the *azarah*, the Temple courtyard. On this reading, the appearance itself is the essence of the *mitzvah*, and bringing a *korban* is not required each and every time one enters the *azarah*.

### Reish Lakish's View

Reish Lakish disagrees: *re'iyat panim be'korban* — appearing before God means appearing with a *korban*. The verse *velo yeira'u panai reikam* — do not appear before Me empty-handed — generates a requirement that every act of appearing be accompanied by an offering. On Reish Lakish's reading, appearing without a *korban* is itself a violation of *lo yeira'u panai reikam*.

## Dispute: Yom Tov vs Other



### First Day Consensus

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בָּעִיקָּר הָרֶגֶל, כּוּלֵּי עָלְמָא לָא פְּלִיגִי דִּרְאִיַּית פָּנִים בְּקׇרְבָּן. כִּי פְּלִיגִי בִּשְׁאָר יְמוֹת הָרֶגֶל: כׇּל הֵיכָא דַּאֲתָא וְאַיְיתִי — כּוּלֵּי עָלְמָא לָא פְּלִיגִי דִּמְקַבְּלִינַן מִינֵּיהּ, כִּי פְּלִיגִי דַּאֲתָא וְלָא אַיְיתִי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: **On the main** i.e., on the first, day of **the pilgrimage Festival, everyone agrees that the appearance of** one’s **face** must be accompanied **by an offering. When they disagree,** it is with regard **to the rest of the days of the pilgrimage Festival,** i.e., whether or not one who appears must sacrifice an offering. Furthermore, **anywhere,** i.e., in any case **that one comes and brings** an offering, **everyone agrees that we accept it from him. When they disagree,** it is **when he comes and does not bring** an offering. Is this man obligated to bring an offering or not?

The *gemara* now clarifies the precise scope of this dispute. On the first day of *yom tov* — the *ikar ha'regel* — everyone agrees that *re'iyat panim be'korban* applies: whenever one appears in the *azarah*, one must bring a *korban*. On that day, multiple appearances each require their own offering.

### Chol HaMoed Question

The actual dispute concerns the other days of *yom tov* — *chol ha'mo'ed*. Is it permissible on those days to enter the *azarah* without bringing a *korban*?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן סָבַר: רְאִיַּית פָּנִים בָּעֲזָרָה, דְּכׇל אֵימַת דְּאָתֵי לָא צָרִיךְ לְאֵתוֹיֵי, רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ אָמַר: רְאִיַּית פָּנִים בְּקׇרְבָּן, דְּכׇל אֵימַת דְּאָתֵי צָרִיךְ לְאֵתוֹיֵי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains **that Rabbi Yoḥanan holds** that the mitzva is merely **the appearance of** one’s **face in the** Temple **courtyard.** Therefore, one **doesn’t need to bring** an offering **whenever he comes** to the Temple. **Reish Lakish said** that the mitzva is **the appearance of** one’s **face with an offering,** which means that **whenever one comes he must bring** an offering with him.

### Yochanan on Other Days

Rabbi Yochanan holds that on the other days of *yom tov* there is no obligation to bring a *korban* each time one appears. One may enter the *azarah* freely, without a gift, and this does not violate *lo yeira'u panai reikam*.

### Lakish on Other Days


Reish Lakish holds that *every* appearance, on any day of *yom tov*, requires a *korban*. The obligation of *lo yeira'u panai reikam* is not confined to the first day; it attaches to every act of appearing before God throughout the festival.

This dispute is significant for the broader question of how *re'iyah* is conceptualized. The *mitzvah* has long been understood as consisting of two intertwined components: appearing in the *mikdash* and bringing a *korban*. The discussion here probes what happens when the two are separated — specifically, whether appearance without a *korban* is legally meaningful or constitutes a deficient fulfillment. Conversely, one might ask whether sending a *korban* without personally appearing would constitute anything at all. These two components are deeply bound together, and the dispute between Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish maps onto the question of which component is primary.

## Reish Lakish's Proofs



### Cannot Come Empty-Handed

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵיתִיבֵיהּ רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ לְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: ״וְלֹא יֵרָאוּ פָנַי רֵיקָם״! אֲמַר לֵיהּ: בְּעִיקַּר הָרֶגֶל.
> **Translation:** **Reish Lakish raised an objection to** the opinion of **Rabbi Yoḥanan.** The verse states: “Three times you shall keep a feast for Me in the year…**and none shall appear before Me empty-handed”** (Exodus 23:14–15). This verse indicates that one must bring an offering whenever he appears in the Temple. Rabbi Yoḥanan **said to him:** That obligation applies only **on the main** day of **the pilgrimage Festival,** but not on the rest of the Festival days.

Reish Lakish challenges Rabbi Yochanan directly: *velo yeira'u panai reikam* — how can you permit someone to appear before God on *chol ha'mo'ed* without bringing a *korban*? The plain meaning of the verse prohibits precisely this.

### Parallel Derivation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵיתִיבֵיהּ: ״וְלֹא יֵרָאוּ פָנַי רֵיקָם״ — בִּזְבָחִים. אַתָּה אוֹמֵר בִּזְבָחִים, אוֹ אֵינוֹ אֶלָּא בְּעוֹפוֹת וּמְנָחוֹת? וְדִין הוּא: נֶאֶמְרָה חֲגִיגָה לַהֶדְיוֹט, וְנֶאֶמְרָה רְאִיָּיה לַגָּבוֹהַּ. מָה חֲגִיגָה הָאֲמוּרָה לַהֶדְיוֹט — זְבָחִים, אַף רְאִיָּיה הָאֲמוּרָה לַגָּבוֹהַּ — זְבָחִים.
> **Translation:** Reish Lakish **raised an objection to** the opinion of Rabbi Yoḥanan from a *baraita*: **“And none shall appear before Me empty-handed.”** This teaches that Festival pilgrims must appear **with animal offerings.** Do **you say** that they must appear **with animal offerings, or** perhaps **it is only with bird**-offerings **or meal**-offerings? **And it is** possible to determine the answer **by logical derivation:** It **is stated** that one must bring **a Festival peace**-offering **for a commoner** to eat, **and** it **is stated** that one must bring a burnt-offering of **appearance,** which is **for God. Just as the Festival peace**-offering **that is stated for a commoner** is referring to **animal offerings, so too,** the burnt-offering of **appearance that is stated** to be sacrificed wholly **for God** is referring to **animal offerings.**


Reish Lakish then constructs a logical derivation — a *dinah v'omeir* — from the parallel structure of the festival obligations. The *chagigah* is brought for the person; the *re'iyah* is brought before God. The verse references *zevachim* in relation to both. Just as the *chagigah* offering must be a *zevach*, so too must the *re'iyah* offering be a *zevach* — a proper animal sacrifice, not merely a bird or grain offering.

### Mammal Requirement

How do we know that the *chagigah* must be a mammal? From the verse *velo yalin chelev chagig ad boker* — the fat of the *chagigah* offering shall not remain until morning. Since the *chagigah* has fat, it cannot be a bird; it must be a sheep or a cow. By the same derivation, the *re'iyah* offering must also be a proper mammal.

### Chagigah vs. Olah Type

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמָה הֵן זְבָחִים — עוֹלוֹת. אַתָּה אוֹמֵר עוֹלוֹת, אוֹ אֵינוֹ אֶלָּא שְׁלָמִים? וְדִין הוּא: נֶאֶמְרָה חֲגִיגָה לַהֶדְיוֹט, וְנֶאֶמְרָה רְאִיָּיה לַגָּבוֹהַּ. מָה חֲגִיגָה הָאֲמוּרָה לַהֶדְיוֹט — בְּרָאוּי לוֹ, אַף רְאִיָּיה הָאֲמוּרָה לַגָּבוֹהַּ — בְּרָאוּי לוֹ.
> **Translation:** **And what are** these **animal offerings** that the Festival pilgrims sacrifice to God? **Burnt**-offerings. Do **you say** that they are **burnt**-offerings, **or** perhaps **it is only** that they are **peace**-offerings? **And it is** possible to determine the answer **by logical derivation:** It **is stated** that one must bring a **Festival peace**-offering **for a commoner, and** it **is stated** that one must bring a burnt-offering of **appearance,** which is solely **for God. Just as the Festival peace**-offering **that is stated for a commoner** is referring to an offering that is **fitting for him** to eat, i.e., a peace-offering, **so too,** the burnt-offering of **appearance that is stated** to be **for God** is referring to an offering that is **fitting for Him,** i.e., a burnt-offering, which is entirely consumed on the altar.

The derivation continues: the *chagigah* is a *shlamim* — the one who brings it eats from it and benefits. The *re'iyah* is offered before God. By parallel logic — *ma chagigah ha'amurah l'hedyot ra'uy lo, af re'iyah ha'amurah la'gavoah ra'uy lo* — the *re'iyah* must be something entirely for God, which means it must be an *olah*, not a *shlamim*.

### Anthropomorphic Reasoning

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכֵן בְּדִין: שֶׁלֹּא יְהֵא שׁוּלְחָנְךָ מָלֵא, וְשׁוּלְחַן רַבְּךָ רֵיקָם. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: בְּעִיקַּר הָרֶגֶל.
> **Translation:** **And similarly,** one can learn **by logical derivation that your table should not be full** of meat **while your Master’s table,** i.e., the altar, **is empty.** This indicates that one must sacrifice a burnt-offering whenever one appears in the Temple, which contradicts the opinion of Rabbi Yoḥanan. Rabbi Yoḥanan again **said to him:** This obligation applies only **on the main** day of **the pilgrimage Festival.**

The *gemara* invokes an explicitly anthropomorphic justification: *shelo yehei shulchancha malei v'shulchan rabacha reikan* — your table should not be full while the table of your Master is empty. You consume the *chagigah*; God should receive the *olah* in its entirety. Whatever its figurative character, the reasoning is clear: the *re'iyah* offering must be an *olah*, and it must accompany every appearance.

## Reish Lakish Limited



### Chol HaMoed Challenge

Rabbi Yochanan presses back: if Reish Lakish holds that *velo yeira'u panai reikam* applies to every appearance, then even on *chol ha'mo'ed* one could never enter the *azarah* without a *korban*. Is that really his position?

### First Day Limitation

Reish Lakish responds that the prohibition of *lo yeira'u panai reikam* applies specifically on the *ikar ha'regel* — the first day of *yom tov*. That is the primary moment of obligatory appearing; it is then that the prohibition against appearing empty-handed is in full force. On the subsequent days of the festival the obligation does not extend in the same way. The debate between Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish thus resolves into a narrower question than it first appeared: both agree that on the first day every appearance requires a *korban*, and both agree that one may *voluntarily* bring additional *korbanot* on subsequent days. The live question is whether appearing on *chol ha'mo'ed* without a *korban* constitutes a violation — and here Reish Lakish, having anchored the prohibition to the *ikar ha'regel*, concedes that it does not.
