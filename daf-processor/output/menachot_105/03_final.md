# Menachot 105 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Mincha Vows: Quantity

### One Isaron

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ "הֲרֵי עָלַי עִשָּׂרוֹן" – יָבִיא אֶחָד, "עֶשְׂרוֹנוֹת" – יָבִיא שְׁנַיִם, "פֵּירַשְׁתִּי וְאֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ מָה פֵּירַשְׁתִּי" – יָבִיא שִׁשִּׁים עִשָּׂרוֹן.
>
> **Translation:** MISHNA: One who says: **It** is incumbent **upon me** to bring **a tenth** of an ephah for a meal offering, **must bring** a meal offering of **one**-tenth. If he says: It is incumbent upon me to bring **tenths** of an ephah, **he must bring two**-tenths, as that is the minimum plural amount. If he says: **I specified** several tenths of an ephah **but I do not know which** number **I specified, he must bring sixty-tenths,** as that is the maximum amount of a meal offering. The flour beyond the amount that he actually specified is rendered a voluntary meal offering.

The new *perek* — the final *perek* of Masechet Menachot — opens with a series of cases governing vows of *minchot*, beginning with the most basic. The *mishnah* states: *harei alai isaron* — if someone takes upon himself a vow to bring an *isaron*, the standard measure of flour for a *mincha*, he brings one *isaron*. This is, as the *Gemara* will note, entirely self-evident.

### Two Isaronot

If someone says *isaronot* — the plural form — implicitly vowing a *mincha* of *isaronot*, he brings two, since two is the minimum the plural can denote. One cannot obligate the person in more than the minimum implied by his words.

### Uncertain Quantity

The third case is where the genuine *chiddush* lies. If someone says *perashti ve-eini yodea ma perashti* — "I specified a quantity but I do not remember what I specified" — *yavi shishim isaron*: he brings a *mincha* of sixty *isaron*. We have already established in earlier portions of the tractate that sixty is the maximum; no *mincha* may exceed that amount. He therefore brings sixty and declares: however many *isronot* I vowed, those are for my vow, and anything beyond that — sixty minus the amount I actually vowed — is a free-will *mincha*, a *nedavah*. The *mishnah* leaves open, for the moment, the question of whether this is brought as a single large *mincha* in one vessel or in separate vessels, a matter the *Gemara* will address.

## Type Specification

### Five Mincha Types

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** "הֲרֵי עָלַי מִנְחָה" – יָבִיא אֵיזוֹ שֶׁיִּרְצֶה. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: יָבִיא מִנְחַת הַסּוֹלֶת, שֶׁהִיא מְיוּחֶדֶת שֶׁבַּמְּנָחוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** If one says: **It** is incumbent **upon me** to bring **a meal offering, he may bring whichever** meal offering **that he wishes,** i.e., the fine-flour meal offering, the shallow-pan or deep-pan meal offering, or the meal offering baked in an oven in the form of loaves or wafers. **Rabbi Yehuda says: He must bring the fine-flour meal offering, as it is the** most **notable of the meal offerings.**

The *mishnah* continues: *harei alai mincha* — if one simply vows to bring a *mincha* without specifying which type, *yavi eizo she-yirtze*, he may bring whichever of the five varieties enumerated at the beginning of *Vayikra* he wishes. These five are: *solet* (fine flour with oil), *machavat* (a flat pan), *marcheshet* (a deep pan), and the two forms of *ma'afeh tanur* (oven-baked offerings) — *challot* (loaves) and *rekikin* (wafers). Since he did not specify, he retains his choice.

### Rabbi Yehuda: Solet Default

*Rabbi Yehuda* disagrees. He holds that one who vows an unspecified *mincha* must bring *minchat solet*, because it is *meyuchedet she-be-menachot* — it is, in some sense, the distinctive or designated *mincha* among the *menachot*. The *Gemara* will explore the precise meaning of this characterization.

## Mincha Vows: Quantity (Continued)

### Rabbi's Separate Vessels

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פֵּירַשְׁתִּי מִנְחָה שֶׁל עֶשְׂרוֹנִים, וְאֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ כַּמָּה פֵּירַשְׁתִּי – יָבִיא שִׁשִּׁים עִשָּׂרוֹן. רַבִּי אוֹמֵר: יָבִיא מְנָחוֹת שֶׁל עֶשְׂרוֹנוֹת, מֵאֶחָד וְעַד שִׁשִּׁים.
>
> **Translation:** If one says: **I specified a meal offering of tenths** of an ephah **but I do not know how many** tenths **I specified, he must bring** a meal offering of **sixty-tenths** of an ephah. **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **says:** His obligation is satisfied only when **he brings meal offerings of** all sizes, in increments of **tenths** of an ephah, ranging **from one**-tenth of an ephah **to sixty**-tenths, for a total of sixty meal offerings with a total volume of 1,830 tenths of an ephah, or 183 ephahs. He fulfills his vow with one of the meal offerings, and the rest are rendered voluntary meal offerings.

Before turning to *Rabbi Yehuda*'s position, the *Gemara* returns to the quantity cases. The *mishnah*'s last case reads: *perashti mincha shel isaronot ve-eini yodea kama perashti* — "I vowed a *mincha* of *isaronot* but do not remember how many." This closely resembles the earlier case of *perashti ve-eini yodea ma perashti*, where one simply said *isaronot* without the word *mincha*. The *Tanna Kamma* holds the same rule applies: bring sixty *isaron*, with the vowed portion designated for the *neder* and any excess as *nedavah*.

*Rebbi*, however, states: *yavi menachot shel isronot me-echad ve-ad shishim* — one must bring sixty separate *menachot*, one of one *isaron*, one of two, one of three, and so on up to sixty. The *Gemara* naturally asks: why does *Rebbi* not dispute the earlier case in the *mishnah* as well, where one said only *isaronot* without specifying *mincha*?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא רַבִּי, בְּאוֹמֵר "פֵּירַשְׁתִּי עֶשְׂרוֹנוֹת אֲבָל לֹא קְבַעְתִּים בִּכְלִי", דְּמַיְיתֵי שִׁיתִּין עֶשְׂרוֹנוֹת בְּשִׁיתִּין מָאנֵי.
>
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Yoḥanan** disagreed and **said: You** may **even say** that this statement is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi, because the case at the end of the mishna is a case where one specified that he would bring the tenths of an ephah in a single vessel. In such a case Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi holds that one must bring sixty different amounts in sixty different vessels. By contrast, the former case in the mishna is one **where** the person **says: I specified** several **tenths** of an ephah **but I did not establish** that **they** must be brought **in** one **vessel.** In such a case all agree **that he must bring sixty-tenths** of an ephah **in sixty vessels,** a tenth in each vessel.

*Chizkiyah* resolves this by stating that the *Tanna Kamma*'s ruling in the first case — where one simply said *isaronot* and forgot the quantity — is not like *Rebbi* at all; *Rebbi* would dispute it.

*Rabbi Yochanan*, however, maintains that *Rebbi* could even agree with the first case. The distinction is: *be-omer perashti isronot aval lo kafiti bi-kli* — when one said only *isaronot* and did not say *mincha* of *isaronot*, he made no specification that they all constitute a single unit in one vessel. By contrast, when one says "a *mincha* of *isaronot*," the word *mincha* implies a single offering in a single vessel, and therefore the amount must be exact — it can be neither more nor less. In that case, *Rebbi* holds that one must bring every possibility from one to sixty, each in its own vessel, so that whichever was the actual amount vowed is fulfilled precisely. But when one merely said *isaronot* — not a *mincha* of them — perhaps that implies multiple *menachot* or multiple vessels, and therefore sixty individual *isronot* in sixty separate vessels can serve as *nedavah* for whatever the amount beyond the vow actually was.

## Solet Default: Rationale

### First Mentioned Challenge

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנָא: הוֹאִיל וּפָתַח בּוֹ הַכָּתוּב תְּחִלָּה.
>
> **Translation:** **It is taught** in a *baraita*: Rabbi Yehuda says that he must bring a fine-flour meal offering, **since** in the passage of the Torah that discusses the meal offering (Leviticus, chapter 2) **the verse opens with** the fine-flour meal offering **first,** mentioning it before the other meal offerings.

The *Gemara* now examines *Rabbi Yehuda*'s position. A *baraita* explains his reasoning: *ho'il u-patach bo ha-katuv techilah* — because *solet* is the type the Torah introduces first. Indeed, *Vayikra* opens the laws of *mincha* with *venefesh ki takriv mincha korban mincha la-Hashem solet yihiye korbano* — if one brings a *mincha*, it shall be *solet* — and only afterward introduces the variants of *ma'afeh tanur*, *machavat*, and *marcheshet*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מֵעַתָּה, הָאוֹמֵר "הֲרֵי עָלַי עוֹלָה" – יָבִיא בֶּן בָּקָר, הוֹאִיל וּפָתַח בּוֹ הַכָּתוּב תְּחִלָּה. "מִן הַצֹּאן" – יָבִיא כֶּבֶשׂ, הוֹאִיל וּפָתַח בּוֹ הַכָּתוּב תְּחִלָּה. "מִן הָעוֹף" – יָבִיא תּוֹרִים, הוֹאִיל וּפָתַח בּוֹ הַכָּתוּב תְּחִלָּה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **If that is so, one who says: It** is incumbent **upon me** to bring **a burnt offering,** without specifying which type of animal, **must bring a young bull** as his burnt offering, **since the verse opens with it first,** in the passage discussing burnt offerings. Similarly, one who says: It is incumbent upon me to bring a burnt offering **from the flock, must bring a lamb** as his burnt offering, **since the verse opens with it first** in the passage discussing burnt offerings of the flock. Similarly, one who says: It is incumbent upon me to bring a burnt offering **from** a type of **bird, must bring doves** as his burnt offering, **since the verse opens with it first** in the passage discussing burnt offerings of birds.

The *Gemara*, however, challenges this reasoning with a sharp rhetorical counter. *Ela me-ata* — if the logic is simply "the Torah mentions it first," then: *ha-omer harei alai olah yavi ben bakar ho'il u-fatach bo ha-katuv techilah* — one who vows an *olah* without specifying should have to bring from the cattle, since the Torah in *Vayikra* opens the laws of *olah* with *im olah korbano min ha-bakar*. Similarly, *min ha-tzon yavi keves*, one who vows an *olah* from the flock should have to bring a sheep, since sheep are listed before goats. And *min ha-of yavi torim*, from birds one should have to bring turtle doves, since they appear before the young pigeons. Yet the following *mishnah* rules otherwise — *harei alai olah yavi keves*, one who vows an *olah* simply brings a lamb (the minimum obligation), and *Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah* says he may bring from birds. Being first-mentioned is plainly not the operative criterion there.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַלְּמָה תְּנַן: הֲרֵי עָלַי עוֹלָה – יָבִיא כֶּבֶשׂ. רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר בֶּן עֲזַרְיָה אוֹמֵר: תּוֹר אוֹ בֶּן יוֹנָה, וְלָא פְּלִיג רַבִּי יְהוּדָה.
>
> **Translation:** If so, **why did we learn** in a mishna (107a): One who says: **It** is incumbent **upon me** to bring **a burnt offering, must bring a lamb,** which is the least expensive land animal sacrificed as an offering. **Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya says:** He may bring either **a dove or pigeon** as a bird burnt offering. **And Rabbi Yehuda does not disagree.**

Moreover, *Rabbi Yehuda* does not dispute the ruling regarding *olah* — he does not argue there that one must bring from the cattle. So why does he invoke the "first mentioned" logic for *mincha* if he does not apply it to *olah*?

### No Descriptor Name

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא מַאי מְיוּחֶדֶת שֶׁבַּמְּנָחוֹת? דְּלֵית לֵיהּ שֵׁם לְוַוי.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara provides a new interpretation: **Rather, what** does Rabbi Yehuda mean when he says that the fine-flour meal offering is the most **notable of the meal offerings?** He means **that it has no modifier.** Only a fine-flour offering is referred to simply as a meal offering, with no other qualification.

The *Gemara* therefore steps back: *ella mai meyuchedet she-be-menachot delait leh shem levai* — what does it truly mean that *solet* is the distinctive *mincha*? It means that *solet* is the only *mincha* that carries no qualifying descriptor name. Every other type requires an appended designation: *minchat machavat*, *minchat marcheshet*, *korban mincha ma'afeh tanur*. But *solet* is introduced simply as *korban mincha* — a *mincha* — with *solet* functioning merely as a description of the substance (flour), not as a categorizing title. It is, in other words, the default: it requires no further qualification to specify what it is.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא תָּנָא הוֹאִיל וּפָתַח בּוֹ הַכָּתוּב תְּחִלָּה קָאָמַר? הָכִי קָאָמַר: אֵיזֶהוּ מִנְחָה מְיוּחֶדֶת שֶׁבַּמְּנָחוֹת, דְּלֵית לֵיהּ שֵׁם לְוַוי? זוֹ שֶׁפָּתַח בּוֹ הַכָּתוּב תְּחִלָּה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: **But isn't it taught** in the *baraita* that Rabbi Yehuda **says** that one must bring a fine-flour meal offering **since the verse opens with it first?** The Gemara answers: **This** is what the *baraita* **is saying: Which meal offering** is the most **notable of the meal offerings, as it has no modifier?** It is **this,** i.e., the fine-flour meal offering, **with which the verse opens first.** The reason that Rabbi Yehuda holds that one must bring a fine-flour meal offering is not because the verse opens with it, but because it has no modifier.

The *Gemara* then asks: but the *baraita* itself says *patach bo ha-katuv techilah* — the reason given is that the Torah opens with it. How then can we say the real reason is the absence of a descriptor name? The *Gemara* answers: *hachi ka-amar* — this is what the *tanna* means. Which *mincha* is the one without a qualifying descriptor name? It is the one the Torah lists first. The fact of being listed first is not itself the reason; it is the identifying marker — a *siman be-alma* — to help you locate which *mincha* bears no adjective. The actual logic is that an unspecified vow of *mincha* defaults to the one called simply *mincha*, not *minchat X*. The first listing in the Torah is merely a pointer to that type.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פְּשִׁיטָא, מִנְחַת הַסּוֹלֶת קָאָמַר, סִימָנָא בְּעָלְמָא.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges: If so, the explanation of the *baraita* is unnecessary; isn't it **obvious** that Rabbi Yehuda is referring to **the fine-flour meal offering,** as **he says** so explicitly? The Gemara answers: The *baraita* explains that the reference to the meal offering with which the passage opens **merely** serves as **a mnemonic,** so one should not forget which type of meal offering Rabbi Yehuda is referring to.

### Olah vs Mincha

The distinction from *olah* is, the lecturer notes, actually quite elegant and deserves appreciation independent of the *Gemara*'s resolution. The Torah's presentation of *mincha* is fundamentally different from its presentation of *olah*. For *olah*, the Torah says *adam ki yakriv mikem korban la-Hashem min ha-behema min ha-bakar umin ha-tzon takrivu* — the offering can come from any of several categories, and then *im olah korbano min ha-bakar* presents the cattle option as one branch among equals. The structure is genuinely open-ended. By contrast, the *mincha* passage says *venefesh ki takriv mincha korban mincha la-Hashem solet yihiye korbano* — "if you bring a *mincha*, it shall be *solet*" — presenting *solet* not as one option but as the statement of what a *mincha* is, with the other types introduced as variations. The Torah's framing itself signals that *solet* is the default.

## Minei Mincha Debate

### Rav Papi's Question

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״מִנְחָה״, ״מִין הַמִּנְחָה״ [וְכוּ׳]. בָּעֵי רַב פָּפָּא: ״מִינֵי מִנְחָה״ מַהוּ?
>
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that if one says: It is incumbent upon me to bring **a meal offering,** or: It is incumbent upon me to bring **a type of meal offering,** he must bring one meal offering. This is because he stated his intent in the singular. But if he says in the plural: It is incumbent upon me to bring meal offerings, or: Meal offerings of a certain type, he must bring two meal offerings. **Rav Pappa raises a dilemma:** If one said: It is incumbent upon me to bring **types of a meal offering,** using a combination of singular and plural forms, **what is** the *halakha*?

The *mishnah* teaches further: *mincha o min ha-mincha yavi achat* — if one vows "a *mincha*" or "from a type of *mincha*," he brings one. *Menachot o min ha-menachot yavi shtayim* — if he uses the plural, "types of *menachot*," he brings two. *Rav Papi* then poses a question: *minei mincha mahu?* — what is the law if one says *minei mincha*, pluralizing the word for "type" (*minei*) but leaving *mincha* in the singular? On one reading, *keivan de-amar minei tarti ka-amar* — since he said "types" in the plural, he means two. And the singular *mincha* simply denotes the category, as indeed the Torah uses the singular when referring to the entire category of *menachot* — *vezot torat ha-mincha* — meaning not a single *mincha* but the genus. But on another reading, *keivan de-amar mincha chada mincha ka-amar* — since he said *mincha* in the singular, he means only one. And *minei mincha* then means: from among the types of *mincha*, I obligate myself in one — *miminei mincha chada mincha alai*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֵּיוָן דְּאָמַר "מִינֵי", תַּרְתֵּי קָאָמַר, וּמַאי "מִנְחָה"? (דְּכוּלַּהּ) מְנָחוֹת נָמֵי "מִנְחָה" מִיקַּרְיָין, דִּכְתִיב "וְזֹאת תּוֹרַת הַמִּנְחָה".
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the dilemma: Perhaps it should be reasoned that **since he said: Types,** in the plural, apparently **he was saying** that he intends to bring **two** meal offerings. **And** if so, **what** is the reason he used the singular word: **Meal offering?** He used it **because the entire** category of **meal offerings is also referred to as: Meal offering,** in the singular, **as it is written** in the verse: **"And this is the law of the meal offering:** The sons of Aaron shall offer it before the Lord, in front of the altar" (Leviticus 6:7).

### Baraita Evidence

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אוֹ דִלְמָא, כֵּיוָן דְּאָמַר "מִנְחָה", חֲדָא מִנְחָה קָאָמַר, וּמַאי "מִינֵי מִנְחָה"? הָכִי קָאָמַר: "מִמִּינֵי מִנְחָה חֲדָא מִנְחָה עֲלַי".
>
> **Translation:** **Or perhaps** it should be reasoned that **since he said: Meal offering,** in the singular, apparently **he was saying** that he intends to bring only **one meal offering. And** if so, **what** did he mean by using the plural phrase: **Types of a meal offering? This** is what **he was saying: Of** the various **types of a meal offering,** it is incumbent **upon me** to bring **one.**

The *Gemara* attempts to resolve this from the *mishnah* itself. The cases of "one" all involve every word in the singular — *mincha*, *min mincha* — suggesting that any use of a plural would produce an obligation of two. So *haminei mincha shtayim* — if "types" is plural, it should mean two. But the *Gemara* counters: *eima seifa* — look at the second half of the *mishnah*. The cases that produce an obligation of two all feature *menachot* in the plural. *Haminei mincha chada* — the fact that "types" was used without pluralizing *menachot* might mean only one. Neither reading can be proven from the *mishnah* alone, which is precisely why the question was being asked in the first place.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: ״מִנְחָה״ ״מִין מִנְחָה״ – יָבִיא אַחַת, הָא ״מִינֵי מִנְחָה״ – שְׁתַּיִם.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Come** and **hear** a proof that his intent is to bring two meal offerings, from that which is stated in the mishna: If one says: It is incumbent upon me to bring **a meal offering,** or: It is incumbent upon me to bring **a type of meal offering, he must bring one. This** indicates that if he said: **Types of a meal offering,** he must bring **two.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵימָא סֵיפָא: ״מְנָחוֹת״, ״מִין מְנָחוֹת״ – יָבִיא שְׁתַּיִם, הָא ״מִינֵי מִנְחָה״ – חֲדָא. אֶלָּא, מֵהָא לֵיכָּא לְמִשְׁמַע מִינַּהּ.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this proof. **Say the latter clause:** If one says: It is incumbent upon me to bring **meal offerings,** or: It is incumbent upon me to bring **meal offerings of** a certain **type, he must bring two. This** indicates that if he says: **Types of a meal offering,** he must bring only **one. Rather, no** inference is **to be learned from this** mishna, as the potential inferences are contradictory.

The *Gemara* then brings a *baraita*: *min menachot alai yavi shtei menachot m'min echad* — "a type of *menachot* on me" requires bringing two *menachot* of the same type. This resolves a matter that was only implicit in the *mishnah* — namely, that when one says "a type" in the singular but pluralizes *menachot*, the two must be of the same type. Further, the *baraita* continues: *haminei mincha* — if one had said *minei mincha* (types in the plural, *mincha* in the singular), the implication seems to be only one *mincha*. The *Gemara* initially considers this a proof.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּא שְׁמַע: ״מִין מְנָחוֹת עָלַי״ – יָבִיא שְׁתֵּי מְנָחוֹת מִמִּין אֶחָד, הָא ״מִמִּינֵי מִנְחָה״ – חֲדָא.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests another resolution to Rav Pappa's dilemma: **Come** and **hear** that which is taught in a *baraita*: If one says: It is incumbent **upon me** to bring **meal offerings of** a certain **type, he must bring two meal offerings of one type. This** indicates that if one says: It is incumbent upon me to bring **of** the various **types of a meal offering,** he is required to bring only **one.**

But the *Gemara* deflects: *dilma lo* — perhaps *minei mincha* does not mean one, but rather two of two *different* types, as opposed to *min menachot* which is two of the *same* type. And yet, *ve-halo tanya hachi* — the *baraita* continues explicitly: *minei menachot alai yavi shtei menachot mishnei minim* — "types of *menachot* on me" produces two *menachot* of two different types. If *minei mincha* already conveyed two *menachot* of two different types, it would have been unnecessary for the *baraita* to introduce *minei menachot* for that purpose. The very fact that the *baraita* reserves *minei menachot* for the case of two of two different types seems to imply that *minei mincha* means only one.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּלְמָא: הָא ״מִינֵי מִנְחָה״ – מֵבִיא שְׁתֵּי מְנָחוֹת מִשְּׁנֵי מִינִין.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this inference: **Perhaps** the correct inference from the *baraita* is that **this** indicates that if one says: It is incumbent upon me to bring **types of a meal offering, he must bring two meal offerings, of two** different **types.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא לָא תָּנֵי הָכִי, ״מִין מְנָחוֹת עָלַי״ – מֵבִיא שְׁתֵּי מְנָחוֹת מִמִּין אֶחָד, ״מִינֵי מְנָחוֹת עָלַי״ – מֵבִיא שְׁתֵּי מְנָחוֹת מִשְּׁנֵי מִינִין, הָא ״מִינֵי מִנְחָה״ – חֲדָא.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges the rejection. **But this is not taught** in the *baraita*, as the full *baraita* reads as follows: If one says: It is incumbent **upon me** to bring **meal offerings of** a certain **type, he must bring two meal offerings of one type.** If he says: It is incumbent **upon me** to bring **types of meal offerings, he must bring two meal offerings, of two** different **types. This** indicates that if he says: It is incumbent upon me to bring **types of a meal offering,** he brings only **one.**

### Minei Mincha Unresolved

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּלְמָא הָא מַנִּי, רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן הִיא, דְּאָמַר: מֶחֱצָה חַלּוֹת וּמֶחֱצָה רְקִיקִין יָבִיא, וּמַאי ״מִינֵי מִנְחָה״? מִנְחָה דְּאִית בַּהּ תְּרֵי מִינֵי.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara rejects this proof: **Perhaps** the *baraita* is not in accordance with all opinions; rather, in accordance with **whose** opinion **is this** *baraita*? **It is** in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon, who said** with regard to one who vows to bring a meal offering baked in an oven that if he wants to bring **half** of the meal offering as **loaves and half** of it as **wafers, he may bring** it in that manner. **And** accordingly, **what** is the meaning of the phrase: **Types of a meal offering?** It means **a meal offering that has two types** of baked dough. Therefore, bringing one such meal offering is sufficient.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲבָל רַבָּנַן דְּאָמְרִי, מֶחֱצָה חַלּוֹת וּמֶחֱצָה רְקִיקִין – לֹא יָבִיא, מֵבִיא שְׁתֵּי מְנָחוֹת מִשְּׁנֵי מִינִין.
>
> **Translation:** **But** according to the opinion of **the Rabbis, who say** that if one wishes to bring **half** of the meal offering as **loaves and half** of it as **wafers, he may not bring** it in this manner, as they hold that all of the baked items in a meal offering must be of the same type, **he** consequently **must bring two meal offerings of two** different **types.**

The *Gemara* responds: *dilma ha mani Rabbi Shimon hi* — perhaps this *baraita* follows *Rabbi Shimon*, who holds that a *ma'afeh tanur* can be brought as a combination of *challot* and *rekikin*. According to *Rabbi Shimon*, a single *mincha* can contain two types — *mincha de-it bah terei minei*. Therefore *minei mincha* according to *Rabbi Shimon* means two types within one *mincha* — namely, the *ma'afeh tanur* that is part *challot* and part *rekikin*. That is why the *baraita* can reserve *minei menachot* for two separate *menachot* of two types. But according to the Rabbis, who hold that *ma'afeh tanur* must be either entirely *challot* or entirely *rekikin* and cannot be divided, there is no single *mincha* with two types. In that case, *minei mincha* might indeed mean two *menachot* of two types. The question of *Rav Pappa* therefore remains unresolved.

## Uncertain Type: Five Options

### Five Types Standard

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״פֵּירַשְׁתִּי וְאֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ אֵיזֶה מֵהֶן פֵּירַשְׁתִּי״ – יָבִיא חֲמִשְׁתָּן.
>
> **Translation:** If one says: **I specified** a meal offering **but I do not know what** meal offering **I specified, he must bring all five** types of meal offerings.

The *mishnah* then rules: *perashti ve-eini yodea eizeh mehem perashti yavi chameshtan* — if one vowed a *mincha* of a specified type but does not remember which of the five types he vowed, he must bring all five, playing it safe. One of them fulfills his vow and the others are free-will offerings.

### Non-Rabbi Shimon

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי יִרְמְיָה: דְּלָא כְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן, דְּאִי רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן – כֵּיוָן דְּאָמַר מֶחֱצָה חַלּוֹת וּמֶחֱצָה רְקִיקִין יָבִיא.
>
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yirmeya said:** This *halakha* **is not in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon. As, if** it were in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon, since he said** that if one wants to bring **half** of his baked meal offering as **loaves and half** of it as **wafers he may bring** it in that manner, why does one have to bring only five meal offerings to cover all possible vows that he may have made? He should be required to bring several meal offerings baked in an oven to cover all the possible combinations of loaves and wafers.

*Rabbi Yirmiyah* observes: *de-lo ke-Rabbi Shimon* — this ruling follows a position other than that of *Rabbi Shimon*. For *Rabbi Shimon* holds that a *ma'afeh tanur* can be half *challot* and half *rekikin*, or indeed any distribution of the ten units between the two types. Therefore, one who does not remember what type of *mincha* he vowed would, according to *Rabbi Shimon*, need to consider not five but potentially fourteen possibilities: the three base types (*solet*, *machavat*, *marcheshet*) plus eleven possible distributions of *challot* and *rekikin* within the *ma'afeh tanur* (all ten as *challot*, nine *challot* and one *rekikin*, eight and two, and so on through all ten as *rekikin*). Working with *Rabbi Yehuda*'s position of ten units, that is eleven internal permutations, yielding fourteen total. (Under *Rabbi Meir*, who holds twelve units, it would be sixteen.)

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי נָמֵי סָבַר לַהּ כְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה, דְּאָמַר כׇּל הַמְּנָחוֹת בָּאוֹת עֶשֶׂר, אִיכָּא לְסַפּוֹקַהּ בְּאַרְבַּע עֶשְׂרֵה מְנָחוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** Therefore, **even if** the *tanna* of the mishna **holds in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabbi Yehuda, who said** that **all meal offerings are brought** as **ten** items, as opposed to the opinion of Rabbi Meir that all meal offerings are brought as twelve items, **there is** cause **for uncertainty,** which renders **fourteen** different **meal offerings** necessary. In addition to the shallow-pan meal offering, the deep-pan meal offering, and the fine-flour meal offering, there are another eleven combinations of baked meal offering that he may have intended.

## Rabbi Shimon: Permutations

### Half-and-Half Mixing

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַבָּיֵי אָמַר: אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן, שָׁמְעִינַן לֵיהּ לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן דְּאָמַר: מַיְיתֵי וּמַתְנֵי.
>
> **Translation:** **Abaye** rejected Rabbi Yirmeya's explanation and **said: You** may **even say** that the mishna is in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon. We have heard that Rabbi Shimon said:** One who is uncertain whether he is obligated to bring a certain offering **may bring** the offering **and stipulate** that if he is obligated to bring an offering, this is his offering, and if he is not obligated, it is a voluntary offering. Therefore, in the case of the mishna, one can bring the five types of meal offerings, with his baked meal offering including ten loaves and ten wafers, and stipulate that whichever items were included in his vow serve as fulfillment of his obligation, and all the others are voluntary offerings.

*Abaye*, however, says: *afilu teima Rabbi Shimon* — even according to *Rabbi Shimon*, one need only bring the five types, not fourteen. This is because *Rabbi Shimon* allows one to bring an offering and attach a stipulation. *Rabbi Shimon*'s allowance to stipulate means that one can bring the standard five — including all ten *challot* and all ten *rekikin* — and declare that whichever combination of *challot* and *rekikin* constitutes one's vow is the *neder*, and the remainder is *nedavah*.

### Fourteen Possibilities

The logic bears careful articulation. If one does not remember whether he vowed *challot*, *rekikin*, or some mixture, he need not bring eleven separate *menachot* of *ma'afeh tanur* to cover every permutation. Instead, he brings the *challot* and *rekikin* together and stipulates.

### Abaye's Stipulation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּתַנְיָא: רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר, לַמׇּחֳרָת מֵבִיא אֲשָׁמוֹ וְלוּגּוֹ עִמּוֹ, וְאוֹמֵר: אִם מְצוֹרָע הוּא – זֶהוּ אֲשָׁמוֹ וְזֶה לוּגּוֹ, וְאִם לָאו – אָשָׁם זֶה יְהֵא שַׁלְמֵי נְדָבָה.
>
> **Translation:** **As it is taught** in a *baraita* (*Tosefta*, *Nazir* 6:1): How should one whose status as a leper is uncertain bring his guilt offering and *log* of oil on the eighth day of his purification? **Rabbi Shimon says: On the following day,** after his seven days of purification, **he brings his guilt offering and his *log*** of oil **with it and says** the following stipulation: **If this** offering is one **of a leper,** i.e., if I am a leper, **this is his guilt offering and that is his *log*** of oil. **And if** I am **not** a leper, **this** animal that I brought for **a guilt offering** shall be **a gift peace offering,** because their sacrificial rites are equivalent.

This works because *Rabbi Shimon* is known to permit stipulation-based hedging in cases of doubt, as *Abaye* demonstrates from an explicit *baraita*. We know *Rabbi Shimon* says: *meisi u-masni* — one brings and stipulates. The case establishing this principle follows immediately.

## Metzora Stipulation Case

### Asham or Shelamim

The *baraita* cited is the case of a person whose status is uncertain — he may be a *metzora* who requires specific *korbanot* for his *tahara*. *Rabbi Shimon omer le-machar mevi ashamo ve-logo imo* — *Rabbi Shimon* says: the following day he brings his *asham* and his *log* of oil together and declares the following stipulation: *im metzora hu zehu ashamo vezehu logo* — if he is indeed a *metzora*, this is his *asham* and this is his *log* and the *tahara* procedure is to be performed accordingly. *Ve-im lav, asham ze yehe shalmei nedava* — and if he is not a *metzora*, this *asham* should become a *shelamim* of free will.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאוֹתוֹ אָשָׁם טָעוּן שְׁחִיטָה בַּצָּפוֹן, וּמַתַּן בְּהוֹנוֹת, וּסְמִיכָה, וּנְסָכִים, וּתְנוּפַת חָזֶה וָשׁוֹק, וְנֶאֱכָל לְזִכְרֵי כְהוּנָּה לְיוֹם וָלַיְלָה.
>
> **Translation:** **And that** uncertain **guilt offering requires slaughter in the north** of the Temple courtyard, as does a guilt offering; **and placement** of the blood on the right **thumb, and** right **big toe,** and right ear of the leper, as described in Leviticus 14:14; **and** it requires **placing hands** on the head of the animal, **and** the accompanying wine **libations, and waving of the breast and thigh,** as does a peace offering. **And it is eaten by males of the priesthood for** one **day and** the following **night** like a guilt offering, and not for two days and the intervening night like a peace offering.

The practical execution is then spelled out. *Ve-oto asham ta'un shechita ba-tzafon* — the animal must be slaughtered in the north, since it might be an *asham*, which is *kodshei kodashim* and requires *shechita* in the north, even though a *shelamim* may be slaughtered anywhere. *U-matan behonot* — the blood is applied to the thumb, toe, and ear as required for the *tahara* of a *metzora*. *U-smicha* — he performs *smicha* on the animal, as required for a *shelamim*. *Tosafot* notes that *smicha* applies to an *asham* as well, though whether it is *de-oraita* for an *asham* is debated; in any case, *smicha* is unambiguously required for a *shelamim*. *U-nesachim* — *nesachim* are brought, as is done for a *shelamim*. The *asham metzora* itself actually carries *nesachim* as well, as studied earlier in the tractate, but other *ashamot* do not; the emphasis here is that this offering will have *nesachim*. *U-tnufat chazeh va-shok* — the *chazeh* and *shok* are waved, as is done for a *shelamim* but not an *asham*. All the actions appropriate to one or the other possibility are performed.

*Ve-ne'echal li-zichrei kehuna le-yom ve-layla* — it is eaten only by male *kohanim*, and only for a single day and night. If it turns out to be a *shelamim*, this is a restriction, since *shelamim* may normally be eaten by anyone in *Yerushalayim* over two days. This means one risks the possibility that the *shelamim* will have to be burned before it otherwise would need to be.

### Eating Limitations

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵימַר דְּאָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן: מַיְיתֵי וּמַתְנֵי לְכַתְּחִילָּה – לְתַקּוֹנֵי גַּבְרָא, אֲבָל בְּעָלְמָא – דִּיעֲבַד אִין, לְכַתְּחִילָּה לָא.
>
> **Translation:** The resolution given there was: **Say that Rabbi Shimon said** that in a case of uncertainty **one may bring** an offering **and stipulate** with regard to its type ***ab initio*** only **for the remedy of a man,** e.g., in order to purify a person from his uncertain status as a leper, as there is no other way for him to purify himself. **But in general, after the fact,** after uncertainty arose with regard to the status of a certain offering it is **indeed** permitted to sacrifice the offering in a manner that may reduce the amount of time allotted for eating it, but one may **not** consecrate such an offering ***ab initio*.**

The *Gemara* — self-referentially noting a discussion in the *Kodashim* tractates — asks: when does *Rabbi Shimon* allow one to bring an offering and stipulate in this fashion *lechatchila*? The answer: *le-takkunei gavra* — to restore this person to normal life. A *metzora* cannot remain in that state indefinitely; he must be returned to a normal state.

### Metzora Exception

The implication is that in general, *Rabbi Shimon* would not ordinarily permit stipulation *lechatchila*; ordinarily, *be-di'avad in, lechatchila lo* — after the fact it may be acceptable, but one would not set out from the start to hedge with stipulations in this way. The special leniency for the *metzora* is precisely because there is no other way to restore him to normal life.

### Minchot: No Destruction

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָנֵי מִילֵּי גַּבֵּי שְׁלָמִים, דְּקָא מְמַעֵט בַּאֲכִילָתָן, דְּהָווּ לְהוּ קָדָשִׁים לְבֵית הַפְּסוּל, אֲבָל מְנָחוֹת – אֲפִילּוּ לְכַתְּחִילָּה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains why here it would nevertheless be permitted to make the stipulation *ab initio*: Nevertheless, **this statement** that one may stipulate with regard to an offering only after the fact applies only **to a peace offering, as** sacrificing it as a guilt offering **reduces its** allotted time for **eating, which** may bring **sacrificial** meat **to the status of disqualification. But** stipulation with regard to **meal offerings** when one does not remember which type he vowed to bring is permitted **even *ab initio*,** as this does not reduce its allotted time for eating. Therefore, the mishna could be in accordance with the opinion of Rabbi Shimon.

The *Gemara* then clarifies why the *metzora* case requires a special justification while the *mincha* case does not. The particular problem in the *metzora* case is *hanei milei gabei shelamim de-kamam'it ba-achilatan de-havu lehu kodashim le-veit ha-pasul* — one risks treating what might be a *shelamim* as though it were an *asham*, limiting its eating to one day and to *kohanim* only, thereby potentially causing it to be burned before it needs to be. That destructive consequence requires special license to override, and the license is the *metzora*'s need to return to normalcy.

By contrast, *aval menachot afilu lechatchila* — with *menachot*, nothing is being limited or destroyed by the stipulation. One is simply designating one portion as *neder* and another as *nedavah*, neither of which involves any degradation or premature burning of the *mincha*. Therefore *Rabbi Shimon* would permit this stipulation *lechatchila* with no special justification needed.

## Double Portions Strategy

### Two Isaronot, Two Log

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב פָּפָּא לְאַבָּיֵי: רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן דְּאָמַר מֶחֱצָה חַלּוֹת וּמֶחֱצָה רְקִיקִין יָבִיא, הָא קָא מַיְיתֵי עִשָּׂרוֹן אֶחָד מִשְּׁנֵי עִשָּׂרוֹן, וְלוֹג אֶחָד מִשְּׁנֵי לוּגִּין!
>
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa said to Abaye:** How can you explain that the mishna could be in accordance with the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon, who said** that if one wants to bring **half** of his baked meal offering as **loaves and half** of it as **wafers he may bring** it in that manner, as one can bring a meal offering of ten loaves and ten wafers and stipulate that some of them are brought in order to fulfill his obligation and the rest are a voluntary offering? This cannot be, as the twenty loaves and wafers constitute a total of two-tenths of an ephah in volume, and must therefore be sanctified in two separate service vessels. This causes a situation where **one brings one-tenth** of an ephah, which constitutes one meal offering as fulfillment of the individual's obligation, **from two** separate **tenths** of an ephah. **And** similarly, the two meal offerings require two *log* of oil, each of which is sanctified in a separate vessel, and it turns out that each meal offering includes **one *log*** of oil **from two** separate ***log*.**

*Rav Pappa* then poses a question to *Abaye*: according to *Rabbi Shimon*, who says that a *ma'afeh tanur* can be half *challot* and half *rekikin*, if one plays this out in practice, one would be bringing one *isaron* out of two *isronot* and one *log* out of two *logim*. That is, to cover all the permutations within *ma'afeh tanur*, one would bring two *isronot* — one that will become the *challot* and one that will become the *rekikin* — together with two *logim* of oil.

### Neder/Nedavah Split

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁמְעִינַן לֵיהּ לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן, דְּאָמַר: אִם הֵבִיא עִשָּׂרוֹן אֶחָד מִשְּׁנֵי עֶשְׂרוֹנוֹת, וְלוֹג אֶחָד מִשְּׁנֵי לוּגִּין – יָצָא.
>
> **Translation:** Abaye answered: This is not difficult, as **we have heard that Rabbi Shimon said** that **if one brought one-tenth** of an ephah as a meal offering **from two** separate **tenths, and** similarly, if one brought **one *log*** of oil **from two** separate ***log*, he has fulfilled** his obligation.

One then stipulates: of this, the amount needed for my *neder* is my *neder*, and the remainder is my *nedavah*. Whichever combination of *challot* and *rekikin* I vowed, those units are my *neder*, and the balance — the ten minus x from the *challot* and the ten minus y from the *rekikin* — is a free-will *mincha*.

### Rav Pappa Clarification

*Rav Pappa* confirms this works, noting that we know *Rabbi Shimon* holds that if someone brought two *isronot* and two *logim* and said the part needed for his *mincha* should count, *yatza* — it is valid. The stipulation of taking one *isaron* from two *isronot* and one *log* from two *logim* is precisely what *Rabbi Shimon* endorses in principle.

## Kemitza Oil Problems

### Kemitza Uncertainty

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִיקְמָץ הֵיכִי קָמַץ? דְּמַתְנֵי וְאָמַר: אִי חַלּוֹת לְחוֹדַיְיהוּ, וּרְקִיקִין לְחוֹדַיְיהוּ אֲמַרִי – דְּקָא קָמֵיצְנָא מֵחַלּוֹת לֶיהֱוֵי אַחַלּוֹת, דְּקָא קָמֵיצְנָא מֵרְקִיקִין לֶיהֱוֵי אַרְקִיקִין. אִי מֶחֱצָה רְקִיקִין וּמֶחֱצָה חַלּוֹת אֲמַרִי – דְּקָא קָמֵיצְנָא מֵחַלּוֹת לֶיהֱוֵי אַמֶּחֱצָה חַלּוֹת וּמֶחֱצָה רְקִיקִין, וּדְקָא קָמֵיצְנָא מֵרְקִיקִין לִיהְווֹ אַמֶּחֱצָה רְקִיקִין וּמֶחֱצָה חַלּוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But how does one remove a handful** from this meal offering, which consists of both loaves and wafers? The Gemara answers **that he stipulates and says: If** I specified in my vow that I would bring **loaves only, or** similarly if **I said** that I would bring **wafers only, let the handful that I remove from the loaves be for the loaves,** and **let the handful that I remove from the wafers be for the wafers. If I said** in my vow that I would bring a meal offering that is **half wafers and half loaves, let the handful that I remove from the loaves be for half the loaves and half the wafers, and let the handful that I remove from the wafers be for half the wafers and half the loaves.**

The discussion then pivots to the *kmitza* problem. How does one perform *kmitza* when the type of *mincha* is uncertain? This is not a trivial procedural question. If the *mincha* is all *challot*, the *kmitza* must be taken entirely from *challot*. If it is all *rekikin*, entirely from *rekikin*. If it is a mixture of *challot* and *rekikin*, presumably the *kmitza* must reflect that mixture — it must contain the appropriate proportion of both types, or at least some of each.

### Two Kemitzot

The proposed solution is to take two separate *kmatzim* — one entirely from the *challot* and one entirely from the *rekikin*. One then stipulates: *ei chalot lachudaihu ve-rekikin lachudaihu* — if I vowed all *challot*, the *kmitza* from the *challot* is for my *neder* and the *kmitza* from the *rekikin* is for the *nedavah*. If I vowed all *rekikin*, the reverse. But what if I vowed some mixture — *mechtza rekikin u-mechtza challot*, or any other proportional split?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא בָּעֵי מִיקְמָץ חַד קוֹמֶץ מֵחַלּוֹת וּמֵרְקִיקִין, וְקָא קָמֵיץ מֵחַלּוֹת אַרְקִיקִין וּמֵרְקִיקִין אַחַלּוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges this suggestion: **But** if the vow was to bring a meal offering that is half loaves and half wafers, **it requires removing one handful from the loaves and** wafers together; **and** according to this suggestion, **he removes a handful from the loaves for the wafers and** a handful **from the wafers for the loaves.**

The stipulation is: *deka kamatzna me-chalot lihavei amar mechtza challot u-mechtza rekikin* — the *kmitza* taken from the *challot*, let it stand for the *challot* portion of my *neder*; and the *kmitza* from the *rekikin*, let it stand for the *rekikin* portion of my *neder*. The remainder of each — *ten minus x* from the *challot* and *ten minus y* from the *rekikin* — is the *nedavah*. In this way, each *kmitza* is said to "work for" the relevant proportion of the vowed and free-will portions simultaneously. This looks like it resolves the question, and yet the *Gemara* raises a difficulty: by taking one *kmitza* from all *challot* and one from all *rekikin*, you are effectively applying the *challot* *kmitza* to the *rekikin* portion of the *neder* as well, and the *rekikin* *kmitza* to the *challot* portion of the *neder*. That is, *ve-ka kamitz me-chalot a-rekikin u-me-rekikin achalot* — you are taking a *kmitza* of *challot* and directing it over the *rekikin* and a *kmitza* of *rekikin* and directing it over the *challot*.

### One Kemitza Works

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁמְעִינַן לֵיהּ לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן דְּאָמַר: אִם קָמַץ וְעָלָה בְּיָדוֹ מֵאֶחָד עַל שְׁנֵיהֶם – יָצָא.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **We have heard that Rabbi Shimon said: If** one brought a meal offering that is part loaves and part wafers, and the priest **removed a handful, and** that which **came up in his hand** was only **from one of the two** types, either only loaves or only wafers, **he has fulfilled** his obligation of removing a handful.

At this point, the *Gemara* reveals that what appeared to be a series of devastating objections are in fact all resolvable by appeal to known positions of *Rabbi Shimon*. *Shmia lei le-Rabbi Shimon de-amar im kamatz be-yado me-chad al sheneihem yatza* — we know *Rabbi Shimon* says that if one took a *kmitza* from one of the two types in a mixed *mincha* of *challot* and *rekikin*, it is valid for both types. Therefore taking one *kmitza* from the *challot* and one from the *rekikin*, and then stipulating that each works for the correct proportional share of the *neder* and the *nedavah*, is fully consistent with *Rabbi Shimon*'s position.

The dialectical arc, then, is this: it initially appeared that *Rabbi Shimon*'s position would require fourteen separate offerings to cover all permutations. *Abaye* argued that one need only bring the standard five and stipulate. *Rav Pappa* elaborated the mechanics — two *isronot* and two *logim* with a *neder*/*nedavah* split — and this was confirmed to be *Rabbi Shimon*'s view. The *kmitza* difficulty was raised and shown to be resolved by *Rabbi Shimon*'s own ruling that a *kmitza* from one type in a mixed *mincha* is valid for both.

### Remainder Oil

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא אִיכָּא מוֹתַר שֶׁמֶן, דְּאִי מֶחֱצָה חַלּוֹת וּמֶחֱצָה רְקִיקִין אָמַר – מוֹתַר הַשֶּׁמֶן מַחְזִירוֹ לַחַלּוֹת, אִי כּוּלְּהוּ רְקִיקִין אָמַר – מוֹתַר הַשֶּׁמֶן נֶאֱכָל לַכֹּהֲנִים.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But isn't there** a problem with **the surplus oil?** The Gemara (75a) states that if a meal offering is brought half as loaves and half as wafers, the oil is divided equally between them; half is mixed with the loaves and half is applied to the wafers. The surplus oil from the wafers may also be mixed in with the loaves. But if the meal offering is baked entirely as wafers, the oil that remains is given to the priests. Therefore, Abaye's explanation of the mishna is problematic, **because if** the person **said** in his vow that he would bring **half loaves and half wafers, one brings back the surplus oil** and mixes it in **with the loaves.** But **if he said** that **the entire** offering shall be **wafers, the surplus oil** should be **eaten by the priests.**

However, one more problem remains: *ve-ha ikka motzar shemen* — what about the remainder of the oil? The issue is this. For a *mincha* that is all *challot*, the oil is mixed directly into the loaves — *challot* require a heavy, integral application of oil. For a *mincha* that is all *rekikin*, the *rekikin* receive only a smear of oil, and the remainder of the oil stays separate and is eaten by the *kohanim*. But if the *mincha* is half *challot* and half *rekikin*, *motzar shemen machziru la-challot* — the leftover oil from the *rekikin* portion is poured back into the *challot*. This creates a problem for the stipulation strategy: if the *mincha* turns out to be all *challot* or all *rekikin*, the remainder oil for the *rekikin* stays separate; if it is a mixture, that remainder must be poured back. One cannot both pour and not pour the oil at the same time.

### Oil Chi Pattern

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן יְהוּדָה, דְּתַנְיָא: רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן: מוֹשְׁחָן כְּמִין כִּי, וּמוֹתַר הַשֶּׁמֶן נֶאֱכָל לַכֹּהֲנִים.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara resolves this problem **in accordance with** the statement of **Rabbi Shimon ben Yehuda. As it is taught** in a *baraita* that **Rabbi Shimon ben Yehuda says in the name of Rabbi Shimon** that the oil of a meal offering baked half as loaves and half as wafers is applied as follows: **One anoints** the wafers **in the shape of** the Greek letter **chi,** Χ, **and the surplus oil is eaten by the priests.** Consequently, the surplus oil of a meal offering baked half as loaves and half as wafers is used the same way as that of a meal offering baked entirely as wafers.

The *Gemara* resolves this by invoking *Rabbi Shimon ben Yehuda*. *D'tanya Rabbi Shimon ben Yehuda omer mishum Rabbi Shimon* — *Rabbi Shimon ben Yehuda* said in the name of *Rabbi Shimon*: even when a *mincha* is half *challot* and half *rekikin*, one does not pour the remainder oil back into the *challot*. Instead, *moshcham ke-minchu* — one applies the oil in the pattern of a chi (the Greek letter), making an X-shaped smear across the *rekikin*, and *motzar shemen ne'echal le-kohanim* — any remaining oil is eaten by the *kohanim*. According to this version of *Rabbi Shimon*'s opinion, the treatment of the oil is identical whether the *mincha* is all *challot*, all *rekikin*, or any combination: the *challot* receive their oil mixed in, the *rekikin* receive a smear in the chi pattern, and any leftover oil goes to the *kohanim*. There is no scenario in which the oil must be poured back. Therefore the stipulation strategy works perfectly even with regard to the oil — nothing changes in the treatment of the oil across all the permutations.

## Minchat Nesachim Excluded

### Different Category

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב כָּהֲנָא לְרַב אָשֵׁי: וְהָא אִיכָּא לְסַפּוֹקַהּ בְּמִנְחַת נְסָכִים, דְּאָמַר רָבָא: מִתְנַדֵּב אָדָם מִנְחַת נְסָכִים בְּכׇל יוֹם.
>
> **Translation:** § **Rav Kahana said to Rav Ashi:** If one specifies in his vow which meal offering he will bring, and subsequently forgets, why does he bring only five types of meal offerings? **Isn't there** room **to be uncertain** with regard to another type of meal offering, namely, the **meal offering** brought with the **libations** that accompany various burnt offerings? This meal offering can also be brought independently as a gift, **as Rava said: A person may volunteer a meal offering** brought with the **libations every day,** if he so wishes.

Having established that even according to *Rabbi Shimon* the answer is five types with stipulation, the *Gemara* then addresses a challenge raised by *Rav Kahana* to *Rav Ashi*: *ve-ika le-ispukei be-minchat nesachim* — but there is a sixth possibility one should have to account for. *Rava* has stated that *misnadiv adam minchat nesachim be-chol yom* — a person may voluntarily bring a *minchat nesachim* every day, independently of an animal sacrifice. This *minchat nesachim*, unlike the five *menachot* enumerated at the beginning of *Vayikra*, has a different ratio of oil to flour. Should one not have to bring it as well when uncertain which type of *mincha* was vowed?

### Five Distinctions

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי קָא מִסְתַּפְּקָא לֵיהּ בְּאוֹמֵר ״הֲרֵי עָלַי מִנְחָה״ – בִּגְלַל יָחִיד, בִּגְלַל צִיבּוּר – לָא מִסְתַּפְּקָא לֵיהּ. בָּאָה בִּגְלַל עַצְמָהּ, בָּאָה בִּגְלַל זֶבַח – לָא מִסְתַּפְּקָא לֵיהּ. טְעוּנָה לְבוֹנָה, שֶׁאֵינָהּ טְעוּנָה לְבוֹנָה – לָא מִסְתַּפְּקָא לֵיהּ. בָּאָה בְּלוֹג, בָּאָה בִּשְׁלֹשָׁה לוּגִּין – לָא מִסְתַּפְּקָא לֵיהּ. טְעוּנָה קְמִיצָה, שֶׁאֵינָהּ טְעוּנָה קְמִיצָה – לָא קָא מִסְתַּפְּקָא לֵיהּ.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers that there is no uncertainty with regard to the possibility that he said that he would bring a meal offering brought with the libations, because it is completely different than the other types of meal offerings. **When one is uncertain** as to which meal offering he vowed to bring, he is uncertain only with regard to meals offerings **brought** exclusively **due to** the obligation of **an individual. But** one is not uncertain **with regard to** a meal offering **brought due to** the obligation of **the public.** Furthermore, **one is uncertain** only with regard to a meal offering that **is brought due to its own** obligation. **But** one is not uncertain **with regard to** a meal offering **brought** with libations **due to** the requirements of **an offering.** Additionally, **one is uncertain** only with regard to a meal offering that **requires** that **frankincense** be brought with it. **But** one is not uncertain **with regard to** a meal offering brought with libations, **which does not require frankincense.** Moreover, **one is uncertain** only with regard to a type of meal offering that **is brought with** one ***log*** of oil. **But** one is not uncertain **if** it **is brought with three *log*** of oil or more. Finally, **one is uncertain** only with regard to a meal offering that **requires removal of a handful. But** one is not uncertain **with regard to** a meal offering **that does not require removal of a handful.**

The *Gemara* responds with a series of categorical distinctions. *Ki ka mistafqa lei, be-ba'ah be-yachid, be-ba'ah be-tzibur lo mistafqa lei* — when someone is uncertain what type of *mincha* he vowed, he would only consider a *mincha* brought by private individuals. A *minchat nesachim* can be brought by the *tzibur* as well, making it categorically distinct from the five standard types. *Ki ka mistafqa lei be-ba'ah be-fnei atzma, be-ba'ah im ha-zevach lo mistafqa lei* — one would only be uncertain about a *mincha* that normally stands alone, whereas a *minchat nesachim*, even if it can be brought independently, standardly accompanies an animal sacrifice. *Ki ka mistafqa lei te'una levona, she'eina te'una levona lo mistafqa lei* — standard *menachot* require *levona*; a *minchat nesachim* does not. *Ki ka mistafqa lei ba'ah be-log, ba'ah be-shlosha login lo mistafqa lei* — standard *menachot* come with one *log* of oil per *isaron*, whereas a *minchat nesachim* uses a substantially greater proportion of oil — two or three *logim* per *isaron* — making it sufficiently anomalous that one would not think of it as a default vow. *Ki ka mistafqa lei te'una kmitza, she'eina te'una kmitza lo mistafqa lei* — standard *menachot* require *kmitza*, the removal of a handful; a *minchat nesachim* is entirely burned and has no *kmitza*.

### Not Default Option

These five distinctions establish that a *minchat nesachim* is so categorically different from the standard five *menachot* of private individuals — in terms of its bringing by individuals versus the public, its independence versus its accompaniment to an animal, its *levona*, its oil ratio, and its *kmitza* — that when someone vows an unspecified *mincha* and then cannot remember which type, a *minchat nesachim* simply would not have been what he had in mind. Therefore there is no need to add it as a sixth option, and the ruling to bring five types — or, according to *Rabbi Shimon*, five types with appropriate stipulation — stands.
