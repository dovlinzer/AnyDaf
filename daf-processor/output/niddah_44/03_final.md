# Niddah 44 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Tumah to Development

### Daf Overview

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי' תִּנוֹקֶת בַּת יוֹם אֶחָד — מִטַּמְּאָה בְּנִדָּה. בַּת עֲשָׂרָה יָמִים — מִטַּמְּאָה בְּזִיבָה. תִּנוֹק בֶּן יוֹם אֶחָד — מִטַּמֵּא בְּזִיבָה, וּמִטַּמֵּא בִּנְגָעִים, וּמִטַּמֵּא בִּטְמֵא מֵת, וְזוֹקֵק לְיִבּוּם, וּפוֹטֵר מִן הַיִּבּוּם, וּמַאֲכִיל בִּתְרוּמָה, וּפוֹסֵל (אֶת) [מִן] הַתְּרוּמָה, וְנוֹחֵל וּמַנְחִיל, וְהַהוֹרְגוֹ חַיָּיב, וַהֲרֵי הוּא לְאָבִיו וּלְאִמּוֹ וּלְכׇל קְרוֹבָיו כְּחָתָן שָׁלֵם.
>
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **A baby girl,** even one who is **one day old,** who experiences an emission of blood, **becomes impure with** the impurity of **a menstruating woman.** A baby girl who is **ten days old** who experiences an emission of blood for three consecutive days after the conclusion of the seven days fit for menstruation **becomes impure with** the impurity of ***ziva*,** and is therefore obligated to observe seven clean days before immersion. **A baby boy,** even one who is **one day old, becomes impure with** the impurity of ***ziva*; and becomes impure with** the impurity of **leprous marks; and becomes impure with impurity** imparted by **a corpse; and he creates** a levirate bond requiring the widow of his childless brother **to** enter into **levirate marriage** with him; **and he exempts** his widowed mother **from the** obligation of **levirate marriage,** freeing her to marry anyone she chooses; **and he enables** his mother, an Israelite woman who is no longer married to his father, a priest, to continue **to partake of *teruma*; and he disqualifies** his mother, the daughter of a priest who is no longer married to his father, an Israelite man, **from** continuing to partake of ***teruma*,** because the child is unfit to partake of *teruma*; **and he inherits** the estate of his mother if she died on the day of his birth; **and** if he dies, **he bequeaths** that inheritance to his paternal brothers; **and one who kills him is liable** for his murder, as it is written: "And he that smites any man mortally shall be put to death" (Leviticus 24:17), i.e., any man, including a child who is one day old; **and** if he dies, **his** status in relation **to his father and to his mother and to all his relatives,** in terms of the *halakhot* of mourning, is **like** that of **a full-fledged groom [*keḥatan shalem*],** whose death is deeply mourned.

Daf 44 opens with the *mishna* found at the bottom of 43b. Until this point, the *masechet* has been concerned with the details of *tumah* and *tahara*: precisely when *tumah* takes effect, where it originates in the body, and the distinctions between touching and carrying. With the *mishna* before us, the *masechet* pivots to an entirely new subject — the ages of development of boys and girls and the different *halakhot* that apply at different stages of their maturity.

### Niddah Blood

By way of brief clarification before proceeding: concerning the blood of a *niddah*, it is worth noting that *mishkav* of a *niddah* and similar matters convey *tumah* not only through touching but even through carrying. This distinction will not occupy us further here, but it is important to have it correct.

## Infants: Niddah/Zavah

### Female Infant Tumah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ' מְנָהָנֵי מִילֵּי? דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״אִשָּׁה״ — אֵין לִי אֶלָּא אִשָּׁה, בַּת יוֹם אֶחָד לְנִדָּה מִנַּיִן? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וְאִשָּׁה״.
>
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara asks: **From where is this matter,** that the *halakhot* of menstruation apply even to a one-day-old baby girl, derived? **As the Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states: "And if a woman has an issue, and her issue in her flesh is blood, she shall be in her impurity seven days" (Leviticus 15:19). When the verse states **"a woman," I have** derived **only** that the *halakhot* of menstruation apply to an adult **woman. From where** do I derive that the impurity **of a menstruating woman** also applies to **a one-day-old** baby? **The verse states: "And a woman,"** to include even a baby girl.

The *mishna* states: *tinoket bat yom echad metame'ah be-niddah* — a baby girl even one day old, if she produces uterine bleeding, is considered a *niddah*. We have encountered this *halakha* before, but it bears emphasis here because it goes to the heart of a fundamental question: to what degree is *niddah* a formal legal category, and to what degree is it organically connected to the physiological phenomenon of menstruation? The answer is clearly that it is a formal definition, since a one-day-old infant can acquire the status.

### Zavah from Vav

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בַּת עֲשָׂרָה יָמִים לְזִיבָה — מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי? דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״אִשָּׁה״ — אֵין לִי אֶלָּא אִשָּׁה, בַּת עֲשָׂרָה יָמִים לְזִיבָה מִנַּיִן? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״וְאִשָּׁה״.
>
> **Translation:** The mishna further teaches that a baby girl who is **ten days old** who experiences an emission of blood for three consecutive days after the conclusion of the seven days fit for menstruation, becomes impure with the impurity **of *ziva*.** Again, the Gemara asks: **From where are these matters** derived? **As the Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states: "And if a woman has an issue of her blood many days not in the time of her menstruation…she shall be as in the days of her menstruation: She is impure" (Leviticus 15:25). When the verse states **"a woman," I have** derived **only** that the *halakhot* of a *zava* apply to an adult **woman. From where** do I derive that the impurity **of *ziva*** also applies to **a ten-day-old** baby? **The verse states: "And a woman,"** to include even a baby girl.

The *mishna* continues: *bat asarah yamim metame'ah be-zivah* — at ten days old she can be a *zavah*. This is because the period of *zivah* begins only on the eighth day after the first bleeding. For a girl to become a *zavah gedolah* she must bleed on three consecutive days — days eight, nine, and ten — meaning the earliest she can attain that status is when she is ten days old.

### Zav from Ish Ish

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תִּינוֹק בֶּן יוֹם אֶחָד כּוּ׳. מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי? דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״אִישׁ אִישׁ״ — מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״אִישׁ אִישׁ״? לְרַבּוֹת בֶּן יוֹם אֶחָד שֶׁמְּטַמֵּא בְּזִיבָה, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה.
>
> **Translation:** The mishna further teaches that **a baby boy,** even one who is **one day old,** can become impure with the impurity of *ziva*. Once again, the Gemara asks: **From where are these matters** derived? **As the Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The verse states with regard to a *zav*: "When **any man** has an issue out of his flesh, his issue is impure" (Leviticus 15:2). It would have been enough for the verse to state "a man." **Why** must **the verse state "any man"?** It is in order **to include** even **a one-day-old** baby boy who has such a discharge, to teach **that he becomes impure with** the impurity of ***ziva*.** This is **the statement of Rabbi Yehuda.**

Regarding infant boys: *tinok ben yom echad metameh be-zivah* — a one-day-old male infant who produces a penile discharge of the type associated with *zivah* can become a *zav*. However, he cannot be a *ba'al keri*, because the *tumah* of *shikhvat zera* applies only when the emission occurs in the context of what is halakhically recognized as an act of intercourse, which requires the boy to be at least nine years old and a day. Even if what emerges from him is biologically semen, it does not carry the *tumah* of *shikhvat zera*. That is why *shikhvat zera* is not listed among the *tumot* applicable to infants.

### Yishmael: Male/Female

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל בְּנוֹ שֶׁל רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בֶּן בְּרוֹקָא אוֹמֵר: אֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ, הֲרֵי הוּא אוֹמֵר: ״וְהַזָּב אֶת זוֹבוֹ לַזָּכָר וְלַנְּקֵבָה״, ״לַזָּכָר״ — כֹּל שֶׁהוּא, בֵּין גָּדוֹל בֵּין קָטָן, ״לַנְּקֵבָה״ — כֹּל שֶׁהִיא, בֵּין גְּדוֹלָה בֵּין קְטַנָּה. אִם כֵּן מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״אִישׁ אִישׁ״? דִּבְּרָה תוֹרָה כִלְשׁוֹן בְּנֵי אָדָם.
>
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Yishmael, son of Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Beroka, says** that this derivation **is not necessary, as** the verse **states: "And of those who have an issue, whether it be a male or a female"** (Leviticus 15:33). The phrase **"whether it be a male"** includes **anyone** who is a male, **whether** he is **an adult** or **whether** he is **a minor; "or a female"** includes **anyone** who is a female, **whether** she is **an adult** or **whether** she is **a minor. If so, why** must **the** earlier **verse state "any man"? The Torah spoke in the language of people,** i.e., this emphasis is not unusual and therefore one should not derive a *halakha* from the superfluous word.

### Negaim & Met Tumah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִטַּמֵּא בִּנְגָעִים. דִּכְתִיב: ״אָדָם כִּי יִהְיֶה בְעוֹר בְּשָׂרוֹ״ — ״אָדָם״, כֹּל שֶׁהוּ. וּמְטַמֵּא בִּטְמֵא מֵת. דִּכְתִיב: ״וְעַל הַנְּפָשׁוֹת אֲשֶׁר הָיוּ שָׁם״ — נֶפֶשׁ כֹּל דְּהוּ.
>
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches: **And** a one-day-old baby **becomes impure with** the impurity of **leprous marks.** The Gemara explains that this is derived from that **which is written** with regard to leprous marks: **"When a person shall have in the skin of his flesh"** (Leviticus 13:2). This serves to include **anyone who is a person,** irrespective of age. The mishna further teaches: **And** a one-day-old baby **becomes impure with impurity** imparted by **a corpse.** The Gemara explains that this is derived from that **which is written** in the context of purification from impurity imparted by a corpse: "And a pure person shall take hyssop, and dip it in the water, and sprinkle it upon the tent, and upon all the vessels, **and upon the people that were there"** (Numbers 19:18). This apparently superfluous mention of "people" serves to include **anyone who is a person,** irrespective of age.

The *mishna* adds that even a one-day-old child can become *tamei* through *tzara'at* and can become *tamei met* — *tamei* through contact with a corpse. These two are listed explicitly, and *Tosafot* explains why.

*Tosafot* notes that by *nega'im* the Torah writes *ish o ishah* — man or woman — which might imply that only adults are subject to *tzara'at*. Similarly, by *tumah* from a corpse the verse writes *ha-ish asher yitma* — the man who becomes *tamei* — which again suggests limitation to adults. The *mishna* therefore specifies that even a one-day-old child is subject to both, and the *Gemara* will derive this from the *pesukim* through alternative textual readings. These two *tumot* are singled out precisely because the language of the verses might have led us to restrict them to adults.

## Birth Threshold

### Yibum & Birth

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְזוֹקֵק לְיִבּוּם. דִּכְתִיב: ״כִּי יֵשְׁבוּ אַחִים יַחְדָּו״ — אַחִים שֶׁהָיָה לָהֶם יְשִׁיבָה אַחַת בָּעוֹלָם. וּפוֹטֵר מִן הַיִּבּוּם. ״וּבֵן אֵין לוֹ״ אָמַר רַחֲמָנָא, וְהָא אִית לֵיהּ.
>
> **Translation:** The mishna also teaches: **And** a one-day-old baby **creates** a levirate bond requiring the widow of his childless brother **to** enter into **levirate marriage** with him. The Gemara explains that this is derived from that **which is written: "If brothers dwell together** and one of them dies, and he has no child, the wife of the dead man shall not be married outside of the family to one not of his kin. Her brother-in-law will have intercourse with her and take her to him to be his wife, and consummate the levirate marriage" (Deuteronomy 25:5). This verse is referring to **brothers who had one dwelling in the world,** i.e., who were alive at the same time, which includes a baby who was born the day his brother died. The mishna teaches: **And** a one-day-old baby **exempts** his widowed mother **from the** obligation of **levirate marriage.** The Gemara explains the derivation: **The Merciful One states:** "And one of them dies, **and he has no child"** (Deuteronomy 25:5), **and this** late husband **has** a child, albeit one who is one day old.

The *mishna* now turns to a category that works in the opposite direction: here, the novelty is not that an infant is included, but that a fetus is excluded. The *mishna* states that a born child can create the obligation of *yibum* — *zokeket le-yibum* — and can exempt a widow from *yibum* — *poteret min ha-yibum*.

To explain: if a man dies and his brother was already born at the time of death, that surviving brother creates the *mitzva* of *yibum* with the deceased's wife. If, however, the brother was still a fetus at the time of the brother's death, there is no *mitzva* of *yibum*. He must have been born — an actual living person — at the moment the obligation would take effect. This is the reverse of the *chiddush* above: whereas with *tumah* the surprise was that even an infant is included, here the surprise is that a fetus does not yet suffice. Only birth confers the necessary legal standing.

Similarly, regarding *poteret min ha-yibum*: if a surviving wife is pregnant with her dead husband's child, she is not yet exempt from *yibum*. She must wait to see whether the child will be born as a viable living infant. Even if she is pregnant, this does not yet constitute having a child in the halakhic sense that would dissolve the *yibum* obligation. Only once the child is actually born does she become exempt. In the meantime, she cannot remarry, for the child may yet be born and retroactively establish that *yibum* was never required.

One might ask: does the child need to survive for a particular period after birth? The answer is no — even a child who lives only one day suffices, provided there is no indication that it was never viable. Once born, even briefly, it exempts the mother from *yibum*.

### Terumah Via Child

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַאֲכִיל בִּתְרוּמָה. דִּכְתִיב: ״וִילִיד בֵּיתוֹ הֵם יֹאכְלוּ בְלַחְמוֹ״, קְרִי בֵיהּ: ״יַאֲכִילוּ בְּלַחְמוֹ״.
>
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches: **And** a one-day-old baby **enables** his mother, an Israelite woman who is no longer married to his father, a priest, to continue **to partake of *teruma*.** The Gemara explains that this is **as it is written,** with regard to those who are entitled to partake of *teruma* on account of a priest: **"And those who are born in his house, they may eat [*yokhelu*] of his bread"** (Leviticus 22:11). **Read into** the verse: Those who are born in his house **enable others to eat [*ya'akhilu*] of his bread,** i.e., on account of her son the priest, the Israelite mother may continue to partake of *teruma* even after the death of his father.

The *mishna* states *uma'akhil ba-terumah* — a born child can enable his mother to eat *terumah*. This refers to the following case: a non-*kohen* woman married to a *kohen* may eat *terumah* by virtue of her marriage. If her husband dies, she may continue to eat *terumah* by virtue of her surviving children. However, if she is merely pregnant and has not yet given birth, she cannot yet eat *terumah* on that basis. Only once the child is born can his existence grant her the right. It is worth noting the halakhic logic here: her right to *terumah* after her husband's death rests not on her own blood connection to *kehuna* — she has none — but on being the mother of children who are *kohanim* or *benot kohen*. Since that status requires an actual born person, the child must be born before she can claim the benefit.

### Pregnant Bas Kohen

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּפוֹסֵל מִן הַתְּרוּמָה. ״וְזֶרַע אֵין לָהּ״ אָמַר רַחֲמָנָא — וְהָא אִית לַהּ. מַאי אִירְיָא זֶרַע? אֲפִילּוּ עוּבָּר נָמֵי! דִּכְתִיב ״כִּנְעוּרֶיהָ״ — פְּרָט לִמְעוּבֶּרֶת. וּצְרִיכִי, דְּאִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״וְזֶרַע אֵין לָהּ״, מִשּׁוּם דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא חַד גּוּפָא וְהַשְׁתָּא תְּרֵי גּוּפֵי, אֲבָל הָכָא — דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא חַד גּוּפָא וְהַשְׁתָּא חַד גּוּפָא — אֵימָא תֵּיכוֹל, כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״כִּנְעוּרֶיהָ״. וְאִי כְּתַב רַחֲמָנָא ״כִּנְעוּרֶיהָ״, מִשּׁוּם דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא גּוּפָא סְרִיקָא וְהַשְׁתָּא גּוּפָא מַלְיָא, אֲבָל הָכָא — דְּמֵעִיקָּרָא גּוּפָא סְרִיקָא וְהַשְׁתָּא גּוּפָא סְרִיקָא, אֵימָא תֵּיכוֹל — צְרִיכָא.
>
> **Translation:** The mishna also teaches: **And** a one-day-old baby **disqualifies** his mother, the daughter of a priest who is no longer married to his father, an Israelite man, **from** continuing to partake of ***teruma*.** The Gemara explains that the reason is that **the Merciful One states:** "But if a priest's daughter becomes a widow, or divorced, **and has no child,** and is returned to her father's house, as in her youth, she may eat of her father's bread" (Leviticus 22:13), **and this** daughter of a priest **has** a child. The Gemara asks: **Why** state **specifically** that she has **a child? Even** if she has **a fetus** in her womb from an Israelite man, the same *halakha* applies, **as it is written: "As in her youth,"** which **excludes a pregnant woman,** since her pregnancy has changed her physical state from that of her youth. The Gemara answers that both derivations **are necessary. As, if the Merciful One had written** only: **"And has no child,"** I would say that the reason the daughter of a priest who has a child from an Israelite man may no longer partake of *teruma* is **due to** the fact **that at the outset,** before she married, she was **one body, and now** she has developed into **two bodies,** herself and her child. **But here,** in a case when she is merely pregnant, **when at the outset** she was **one body and now** she remains **one body,** one might **say** that **she should** be permitted to **partake** of *teruma*. Therefore, **the Merciful One writes: "As in her youth."** **And** by contrast, **if the Merciful One had written** only: **"As in her youth,"** I would say that the reason that the daughter of a priest who is pregnant from an Israelite man may no longer partake of *teruma* is **due to** the fact **that at the outset** she had **an empty body and now** she has **a full body,** and consequently she is not returning to her father's household in her initial state. **But here,** after she has given birth, **where at the outset** she had **an empty body and now** she still has **an empty body,** one might **say** that **she should** be permitted to **partake** of *teruma*. Therefore, the Merciful One writes: "And has no child." Therefore, both derivations are **necessary.**

The *mishna* then states *uposseil min ha-terumah* — a born child can disqualify his mother from eating *terumah*. This is the reverse case: a *bat kohen* who married a *Yisrael* loses her right to eat *terumah* by virtue of her marriage into a non-*kohen* household. If her *Yisrael* husband dies and she has no children and is not pregnant, she returns to her father's house and resumes eating *terumah*. But if she has a child from her *Yisrael* husband, she remains connected to that household and cannot eat *terumah*.

The *mishna*'s language implies that it is only the *born* child that prevents her return — suggesting that if she were merely pregnant, she could go back. The *Gemara*, however, challenges this reading directly, since the *halakha* is that pregnancy alone also prevents her return. This challenge will be addressed at length below.

### Rav Sheshet Resolution

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** קְרָאֵי אִתָּרוּץ, אֶלָּא מַתְנִיתִין מַאי אִרְיָא ״בֶּן יוֹם אֶחָד״? אֲפִילּוּ עוּבָּר נָמֵי! אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: הָכָא בְמַאי עָסְקִינַן? בְּכֹהֵן שֶׁיֵּשׁ לוֹ שְׁתֵּי נָשִׁים, אַחַת גְּרוּשָׁה וְאַחַת שֶׁאֵינָהּ גְּרוּשָׁה, וְיֵשׁ לוֹ בָּנִים מִשֶׁאֵינָהּ גְּרוּשָׁה, וְיֵשׁ לוֹ בֶּן יוֹם אֶחָד מִן הַגְּרוּשָׁה. דְּפוֹסֵל בְּעַבְדֵי אָבִיו מִלֶּאֱכוֹל בִּתְרוּמָה, וּלְאַפּוֹקֵי מִדְּרַבִּי יוֹסֵי, דְּאָמַר: עוּבָּר נָמֵי פּוֹסֵל, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן: בֶּן יוֹם אֶחָד — אִין, עוּבָּר — לָא.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: The need for both **verses** has been **resolved, but the mishna** remains difficult: **What** is the reason the mishna is referring **specifically** to a baby who is **one day old,** when, as stated above, the same *halakha* applies **even** to **a fetus? Rav Sheshet said: Here we are dealing with a priest who has two wives: One** who is **a divorcée,** as she was previously divorced from another man, and who was therefore married to this priest in violation of *halakha*, **and one who is not a divorcée. And he has sons from** the wife **who is not a divorcée, and he has** a baby boy who is **one day old from the** wife who is **a divorcée.** This son is disqualified from the priesthood and may not partake of *teruma*. Rav Sheshet continues: The mishna is teaching **that** this baby **disqualifies his father's** Canaanite **slaves from partaking of *teruma*** again. Since this child is entitled to a portion of his father's inheritance, which includes his slaves, they may no longer partake of *teruma* due to his presence in the world. **And** the mishna teaches this **to exclude** the opinion **of Rabbi Yosei, who said** that **a fetus also disqualifies** his father's slaves from partaking of *teruma*. For this reason, the *tanna* of the mishna **teaches us** that with regard to **a one-day-old** baby boy, **yes,** he disqualifies his father's slaves from partaking of *teruma*, but **a fetus** does **not.**

The *Gemara* now raises a strong challenge against the *mishna*'s formulation of *posseil min ha-terumah*. The verse states *ve-zera ein lah* — when she has no offspring, the *bat kohen* who was widowed from her *Yisrael* husband can return and eat *terumah*. *Ve-ha issa* — but she does have seed, namely the fetus in her womb. Even a fetus should prevent her return.

The *Gemara* presses further: the verse also states *ki-neuracha* — she returns like her youth, as if she could erase her past and go back to the state she was in when she was a young woman in her father's house. *Prat le-muberet* — this excludes a pregnant woman outright. If she is pregnant, she is plainly not returning as she was in her youth. So why does the *mishna* require the child to be born? Pregnancy alone should already prevent her return.

The *Gemara* answers with *tzarichi* — both formulations are necessary. Had the Torah stated only *ve-zera ein lah*, I might have thought that a child — who is a separate body — creates a profound enough change to prevent her return, since what began as one body has become two. But perhaps pregnancy, where she is still one person carrying something internal, is not a sufficient change. *Eima techal* — maybe she can still go back when pregnant. Therefore the Torah writes *ki-neuracha* — not like her youth — to tell us that even pregnancy prevents her return.

Conversely, had the Torah stated only *ki-neuracha*, I might have thought that pregnancy — which visibly transforms the person, *gufa malya*, a full body as opposed to an empty one — is sufficient to prevent return, but that a child who now lives independently somewhere poses no such obstacle. One might say *gufa srika*, the body is the same as it was, empty, and perhaps the distant child is not enough of a connection. Therefore the Torah states *ve-zera ein lah* — even a born child, even when the mother's body looks as it did before, remains enough of a link to her prior marriage to prevent return.

Thus each formulation teaches what the other cannot, and both are necessary.

### Nachal & Hanchil

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נוֹחֵל וּמַנְחִיל. נוֹחֵל, מִמַּאן? מֵאָבִיו. וּמַנְחִיל, לְמַאן? לְאָחִיו מֵאָבִיו. אִי בָּעֵי מֵאֲבוּהּ (לֵירְתֵי) [לֵירְתוּ], וְאִי בָּעֵי מִינֵּיהּ (לֵירְתֵי) [לֵירְתוּ]!
>
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that this baby **inherits and he bequeaths.** The Gemara asks: **From whom does he inherit?** It must be **from his father. And to whom does he bequeath?** Presumably, he bequeaths **to his paternal brother,** in a case where the baby inherited his father's property and then died on the same day, as maternal half-brothers do not inherit from each other. The Gemara raises a difficulty with regard to this interpretation: What is the novelty of the *halakha* that the brother of this one-day-old baby inherits from him? After all, **if** the surviving brother **wants, let him inherit from his father, and if he wants, let him inherit from** the one-day-old baby. Either way, he receives his late father's property.

The *mishna* states that the child must be born to inherit — *nochal* — and to bequeath — *manchil*. If his father dies while he is still in the womb, he does not yet inherit. Likewise, he cannot bequeath until he is born. The significance of this distinction will be unpacked by the *Gemara*.

### Mother's Property Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: נוֹחֵל בְּנִכְסֵי הָאֵם לְהַנְחִיל לְאֶחָיו מִן הָאָב. וְדַוְקָא בֶּן יוֹם אֶחָד, אֲבָל עוּבָּר — לָא. מַאי טַעְמָא? דְּהוּא מָיֵית בְּרֵישָׁא, וְאֵין הַבֵּן יוֹרֵשׁ אֶת אִמּוֹ בַּקֶּבֶר לְהַנְחִיל לְאֶחָיו מִן הָאָב.
>
> **Translation:** **Rav Sheshet said:** The mishna is teaching that a one-day-old baby **inherits his mother's property** if she died on the day he was born, so that he is able **to bequeath** it, even if he dies after a day, **to** his heirs who are not the mother's heirs, e.g., **a paternal half-brother. And** in such a case it is **specifically** when he is at least **one day old** that he inherits from his mother and bequeaths the property to his paternal half-brothers, **but a fetus,** whose mother died before he emerged, does **not** inherit from his mother. **What is the reason** for this? The reason is **that** presumably the fetus **died first,** before its mother died, **and** there is a *halakha* that **a son does not inherit from his mother** while **in the grave,** i.e., after death, **in order to bequeath to his paternal half-brother.**

The *Gemara* asks a probing question: why does it even matter whether the infant inherited and bequeathed? If we take the case of three brothers — *Reuven*, *Shimon*, and *Levi* — and *Levi* is born one day old, inherits from the father, and then dies, his estate passes to *Reuven* and *Shimon*. But had *Levi* never been born, *Reuven* and *Shimon* would have inherited from the father directly. The end result appears identical. In what case does it actually matter whether *Levi* inherited and bequeathed?

The *Gemara* also establishes the case of inheriting the mother's property to pass it on to paternal brothers. Here is where the distinction is starkest. Say a mother — the paradigmatic case is *Rachel* dying in childbirth — dies as her son is born. The newborn son inherits her estate. When he then dies, his paternal brothers inherit from him. As a result, the mother's property passes to her husband's other children, who are her son's paternal brothers. Had the son not been born at all, the mother's property would have reverted to her own blood relatives — her father's family. It is precisely because he was born, inherited, and bequeathed that the maternal property flows to the paternal line. *Nochal be-nichsei ha-em le-hanhil le-achiv min ha-av* — he inherits from his mother in order to pass on to his father's brothers.

## Fetal Viability

### Mother Dies First

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִינִי? וְהָא הֲוָה עוֹבָדָא, וּפַרְכֵּס עַד תְּלָת פִּרְכּוּסֵי! אָמַר מָר בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב אָשֵׁי: מִידֵּי דְּהָוֵה אַזְּנַב הַלְּטָאָה דִּמְפַרְכֶּסֶת.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Is that so,** that it is presumed that the fetus died before its mother? **But wasn't there an incident** in which the mother died **and** the fetus **made up to three spasmodic motions** afterward? **Mar, son of Rav Ashi, said:** That is **just as it is with the tail of the lizard, which twitches** after being severed from the lizard, but it is merely a spasmodic motion which does not indicate that it is still alive.

Interestingly, the *Gemara* assumes something that goes beyond the simple legal principle. Rather than saying straightforwardly that a fetus lacks legal personhood and therefore cannot inherit, the *Gemara* leaves open the possibility that a fetus *could* inherit in utero. It asks why the infant must be born, and answers: *de-hu mayyit bereisha* — because when a mother dies, the assumption is that the fetus dies first. The mother is not yet dead when the fetus expires. Therefore the fetus cannot inherit from the mother, because by the time the mother's death takes effect, the fetus is already gone. *Ve-ein ha-ben yoresh et imo ba-kever* — a son cannot inherit from his mother once he is already dead.

This is a strikingly unusual reading of the *mishna*. The simple understanding would be that a fetus is simply not yet a legal person and cannot inherit regardless of the sequence of deaths. Our *Gemara*, however, appears to assume that perhaps a fetus *can* inherit — and the reason it does not in this case is purely the biological assumption about the order of death.

The *Gemara* challenges this assumption: *va-ha havi uvda u-firkes ad tlata pirkusei* — was there not an actual incident where a fetus thrashed three times after the mother's death, demonstrating that the fetus survived her? *Mar the son of Rav Ashi* responds: that proves nothing. *Midi de-hava aznav ha-leta'ah* — it is like the tail of a lizard. If you cut off a lizard's tail, the tail continues to thrash. That thrashing does not indicate ongoing life; it is a residual muscular reflex, not a sign of vitality.

### Tosafot: In a Box

*Tosafot* adds a further dimension. Even if we accept the *Gemara*'s position that the fetus typically dies before the mother, *Tosafot* notes the implication: if the mother dies first, the fetus might actually be considered as though already born. *Tosafot* uses the metaphor of a box — *de-ke-munach be-kupsa dami*: once the mother is dead, the fetus is no longer inside a living person but is, as it were, sitting in a container. He has independent viability. In that case, even if we otherwise hold that a fetus cannot inherit, the *Gemara*'s insistence that the fetus dies first may be necessary precisely because if the mother died first, the fetus would be deemed already born and capable of inheriting.

### Tosafot: Abortion Status

This leads *Tosafot* into a discussion that is of major significance for the laws of abortion. *Tosafot* raises the question of whether one who kills a fetus in its mother's womb is guilty of a capital offense. Given the uncertainty about the fetus's legal status — is it a person or not — the question arises on both sides of the ledger. And here *Tosafot* uses language that is striking: *muttar le-horgo be-veten* — it is permissible to kill him in utero.

The word *muttar* is stark and has attracted significant attention in the abortion literature. Most authorities understand abortion to be prohibited in some degree — the question is how severely — and *Tosafot*'s use of *muttar* is far more permissive than the consensus. Some later authorities argue that *Tosafot* did not intend this language literally, but the phrase is there and has played a role in the halakhic debate.

### Shabbat Paradox

*Tosafot* himself recognizes the resulting paradox and raises it explicitly. If one could say it is *muttar* to abort a fetus, why then can one violate *Shabbat* to save a fetus? *Amai mechallelin alav et ha-Shabbat?* The basis for *chilul Shabbat* is *pikuach nefesh* — saving a life. If the fetus is not a life, there should be no *pikuach nefesh* to justify *chilul Shabbat*. Yet halakhic practice clearly permits — indeed, requires — violating *Shabbat* to save a fetus in danger.

*Tosafot* answers: *de-mikkol makom mishum pikuach nefesh mechallelin alav et ha-Shabbat, af al gav de-muttar le-horgo* — nevertheless, because of *pikuach nefesh*, one violates *Shabbat* for the fetus even though abortion might be permissible. The two rulings coexist: a choice to end the pregnancy may be permitted, but if the pregnancy is being preserved, one violates *Shabbat* to protect it.

This paradox is not unique to the beginning of life. An analogous tension appears at the end of life, where in certain circumstances a person may have the right to refuse intervention and allow their own death, while at the same time, if they choose to live, every halakhic resource including *chilul Shabbat* is deployed to preserve them. *Tosafot* recognizes this as a genuine and unresolved tension, and the observation remains directly relevant to contemporary halakhic discussions.

## Inheritance Details

### Bechor Reduction

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָר בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יוֹסֵף מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא אָמַר: לוֹמַר שֶׁמְּמַעֵט בְּחֵלֶק בְּכוֹרָה, וְאָמַר מָר בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יוֹסֵף מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא: בֵּן שֶׁנּוֹלַד אַחַר מִיתַת הָאָב אֵינוֹ מְמַעֵט בְּחֵלֶק בְּכוֹרָה, מַאי טַעְמָא? ״וְיָלְדוּ לוֹ״ בָּעֵינַן.
>
> **Translation:** **Mar, son of Rav Yosef, said** a different explanation of the mishna's ruling **in the name of Rava:** The mishna teaches that a one-day-old baby inherits in order **to say that** such a child **reduces the portion of the firstborn.** A firstborn is entitled to a double portion of the inheritance, which is calculated by taking into account the portion due to his dead brother. **And Mar, son of Rav Yosef,** further **said in the name of Rava: A son who was born after his father's death does not reduce the portion of the firstborn.** Therefore, the *halakha* in the mishna does not apply to a fetus. **What is the reason** for this? **We require** fulfillment of the verse: "If a man has two wives, the one beloved, and the other hated, **and they bore him children"** (Deuteronomy 21:15), and this does not apply to a fetus not yet born at the time of the father's death.

*Mar the son of Rav Yosef* in the name of *Rava* gives the answer: *lomar she-mema'it be-chelek bechora* — the born infant reduces the *bechor*'s double portion. If there are only *Reuven* and *Shimon*, *Reuven* the *bechor* receives a double share — say, four units out of six. With *Levi* present, the estate is divided into four shares, with *Reuven* receiving two and the others one and a half each. When *Levi* dies, his one and a half is divided equally among the remaining brothers, including *Reuven*. *Reuven* ends up with less than the four units he would have received had *Levi* never existed. Thus the born-and-deceased infant's existence materially diminishes the *bechor*'s portion, and the distinction between inheritance by a born infant versus a fetus carries real halakhic consequence.

### Born After Death

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּסוּרָא מַתְנוּ הָכִי, בְּפוּמְבְּדִיתָא מַתְנוּ הָכִי: אָמַר מָר בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יוֹסֵף מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא — בְּכוֹר שֶׁנּוֹלַד לְאַחַר מִיתַת אָבִיו אֵינוֹ נוֹטֵל פִּי שְׁנַיִם. מַאי טַעְמָא? ״יַכִּיר״ בָּעֵינַן, וְהָא לֵיכָּא. וְהִלְכְתָא: כְּכֹל הָנֵי לִישָּׁנֵי דְּמָר בְּרֵיהּ דְּרַב יוֹסֵף מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes: **In Sura they taught** Mar's statement **that** way, whereas **in Pumbedita they taught** it **this** way: **Mar, son of Rav Yosef, said in the name of Rava: A firstborn who was born after his father's death does not receive a double** portion. **What is the reason** for this? **We require** fulfillment of the verse: "But **he shall acknowledge** the firstborn…by giving him a double portion" (Deuteronomy 21:17), **and** in this case the father **is not** there to acknowledge him. The Gemara concludes: **And the *halakha*** is **in accordance with all these versions of** the statement of **Mar, son of Rav Yosef, in the name of Rava,** i.e., a one-day-old baby reduces the portion of the firstborn, a son born after his father's death does not reduce the portion of the firstborn, and a firstborn born after his father's death does not receive a double portion.

The *Gemara* returns to the laws of *bechor* and inheritance. *Mar the son of Rav Yosef* in the name of *Rava* teaches: if *Levi* was born after his father's death, his existence does not reduce *Reuven*'s double portion. In such a case, the size of the double portion is calculated based on the children who were alive at the time the father died. *Reuven*'s entitlement is set aside first, calculated as if *Levi* never existed, and then *Levi* inherits from the remainder together with the others.

Conversely, if the *bechor* himself was born after the father's death, he does not receive a double portion at all. *Yakir ba'inan* — the verse requires the father to recognize his firstborn son, meaning the father must have been alive when the son was born. If the *bechor* was born posthumously, the condition of recognition is not met, and the double portion does not apply.

## Horgo Chayav & Aveilus

### Murder Threshold

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַהוֹרְגוֹ חַיָּיב. דִּכְתִיב ״וְאִישׁ כִּי יַכֶּה כׇּל נֶפֶשׁ״ — מִכׇּל מָקוֹם.
>
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And one who kills** a one-day-old baby is **liable** for his murder. The Gemara explains that the reason for this is **as it is written: "And he who smites any man mortally** shall be put to death" (Leviticus 24:17), where the phrase "any man" indicates that this verse applies **in any case,** even in the case of a one-day-old baby.

The *mishna* states *ve-hargo chayav* — only once the child is born does killing him constitute murder. A *pasuk* confirms this: *ve-ish ki yakkeh kol nefesh* — if a man strikes any soul. The phrase *kol nefesh* — any soul — teaches that even an infant is included; the *chiddush* of the *mishna* is not the birth requirement per se but that even a newborn counts as a full life for the purposes of murder. The birth requirement emerges as the other side of the same coin.

### Rav Moshe: Murder

It should be noted that *Rav Moshe Feinstein* took the position that abortion constitutes an act equivalent to murder, even if the perpetrator is exempt from the death penalty. This view is grounded in an ambiguous *Rambam* which *Rav Moshe* reads in support of that position. Prior to *Rav Moshe*, authorities debated the severity of abortion across a broad spectrum — from the relatively lenient to the very severe — but *Rav Moshe* appears to be the first to articulate explicitly that abortion is structurally equivalent to murder, with only the capital sanction removed.

### Aveilus Day One

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַהֲרֵי הוּא לְאָבִיו וּלְאִמּוֹ וּלְכׇל קְרוֹבָיו כְּחָתָן שָׁלֵם. לְמַאי הִלְכְתָא? אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: לְעִנְיַן אֲבֵלוּת. כְּמַאן? דְּלָא כְּרַבָּן שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל, דְּאָמַר: כֹּל שֶׁשָּׁהָה שְׁלֹשִׁים יוֹם בָּאָדָם אֵינוֹ נֵפֶל. הָא לֹא שָׁהָה — סָפֵק הָוֵי! הָכָא בְמַאי עָסְקִינַן? דְּקִים לֵיהּ שֶׁכָּלוּ לוֹ חֳדָשָׁיו.
>
> **Translation:** The mishna further teaches: **And** if a one-day-old baby dies, **his** status in relation **to his father and to his mother and to all his relatives** is **like** that of **a full-fledged groom.** The Gemara asks: **With regard to what *halakha*** is this stated? **Rav Pappa said: With regard to mourning.** The Gemara comments: **In accordance with whose** opinion is this stated? It is **not in accordance with** the opinion of **Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who said: With regard to humans, any** child **that remained** alive **thirty days** after birth **is not** considered **a non-viable newborn.** It can be inferred from this statement that if **he did not remain** alive for thirty days after birth, **he is** of **uncertain** status. The Gemara refutes this proof: **Here we are dealing with** a case **where one is certain that its months** of gestation **were completed,** and therefore it is certainly a viable newborn.

For the halakhic significance of the phrase *ke-chatan shalem* — like a full *chatan* — the *Gemara* states *amar Rav Pappa le-inyan aveilus*: full laws of mourning apply even upon the death of a one-day-old infant. *Shiva* is observed, and all the obligations of *aveilus* apply.

This ruling, however, is not in accordance with *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel*, who holds that only after a child has survived thirty days can we establish that it was viable. If the child dies within thirty days, it is presumed to have been a *nefel* — a non-viable birth — and no *aveilus* is observed. According to *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel*, a child who dies before thirty days is a *safek*, and the presumption against *aveilus* prevails.

The *Gemara* reconciles the ruling of our *mishna* with the position of *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* as follows: *hacha be-mai askinan de-khillu lo chodashav* — the *mishna* is speaking of a case where the full term of gestation was completed and observable. In such circumstances, the physical features and robustness of the infant may allow us to determine viability before thirty days. Even *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* would agree that when full gestation is evident, we do not need to wait the full thirty days before confirming viability and therefore before *aveilus* applies.

*Tosafot* adds a further clarification: the dispute between *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* and the *chakhamim* applies specifically when the infant dies from what appears to be natural weakness. But if the infant died from an external cause — say, a fall — and there was no indication of inherent frailty, the *chakhamim* would require *aveilus* even within thirty days, while *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* would still demand the thirty-day threshold.

## 3 Years 1 Day

### 3-Year Threshold

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי' בַּת שָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנִים וְיוֹם אֶחָד — מִתְקַדֶּשֶׁת בְּבִיאָה, וְאִם בָּא עָלֶיהָ יָבָם — קְנָאָהּ, וְחַיָּיבִין עָלֶיהָ מִשּׁוּם אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ, וּמְטַמְּאָה אֶת בּוֹעֲלָהּ לְטַמֵּא מִשְׁכָּב תַּחְתּוֹן כָּעֶלְיוֹן. נִשֵּׂאת לַכֹּהֵן — תֹּאכַל בִּתְרוּמָה; בָּא עָלֶיהָ אֶחָד מִן הַפְּסוּלִין — פְּסָלָהּ מִן הַכְּהוּנָּה; בָּא עָלֶיהָ אֶחָד מִכׇּל הָעֲרָיוֹת הָאֲמוּרוֹת בַּתּוֹרָה — מוּמָתִין עָלֶיהָ, וְהִיא פְּטוּרָה. פָּחוֹת מִכֵּן — כְּנוֹתֵן אֶצְבַּע בָּעַיִן.
>
> **Translation:** MISHNA: **A girl** who is **three years and one day old,** whose father arranged her betrothal, **is betrothed through intercourse,** as the halakhic status of intercourse with her is that of intercourse in all halakhic senses. **And** in a case where the childless husband of a girl three years and one day old dies, **if** his brother the ***yavam* engages in intercourse with her, he acquires her** as his wife; **and** if she is married, a man other than her husband **is liable for** engaging in intercourse with **her due to** violation of the prohibition against intercourse with **a married woman.** **And** if she is impure due to menstruation, **she imparts impurity to one who engages in intercourse with her** who then **renders impure** all the layers of **bedding beneath** him, rendering them impure **like the upper** bedding covering a *zav*, in the sense that it assumes first-degree ritual impurity and does not become a primary source of ritual impurity, and it renders impure food and drink, but it does not render impure people and vessels. If **she marries a priest, she may partake of *teruma*,** like any other wife of a priest; if she is unmarried and **one** of the men **who are unfit** for the priesthood, e.g., a *mamzer* or *ḥalal*, **engaged in intercourse with her, he disqualifies her from** marrying into **the priesthood,** and if she is the daughter of a priest, she is disqualified from partaking of *teruma*. Finally, if **one of all those with whom relations are forbidden,** as **stated in the Torah,** e.g., her father or her husband's father, **engaged in intercourse with her, they are executed** by the court **for** engaging in intercourse with **her, and she is exempt,** because she is a minor. If the girl is **less than that** age, younger than three years and one day, the status of intercourse with her is not that of intercourse in all halakhic senses; rather, it is **like placing a finger into the eye.** Just as in that case, the eye constricts, sheds tears, and then returns to its original state, so too, in a girl younger than three years and one day old, the hymen returns to its original state.

The *mishna* now moves up the developmental ladder: *bat shalosh shanim ve-yom echad* — a girl three years and a day old. This is the threshold at which an act of sexual intercourse with her is halakhically recognized as an act of *bi'ah*. Below this age, the act is not considered *bi'ah* in the halakhic sense.

Before proceeding to the *mishna*'s list of consequences, it must be said plainly: the entire discussion presupposes the sexual violation of very young children. The *Gemara* treats it with complete legal detachment, noting thresholds and consequences without any remark on the horror of the underlying acts. When this material is studied — whether in *Daf Yomi* or in *yeshivot* — to engage with it without acknowledgment of that horror is itself a serious problem. The legal analysis is what it is; the human dimension cannot be bracketed.

### Kiddushin vs. Bia

A father can give his daughter in *kiddushin* through *kesef* or *shtar* even when she is one day old. But *kiddushin* effected through *bi'ah* — through intercourse — requires that she be three years and a day old, since only then is the act considered a valid act of *bi'ah*. So too, *nisuin* — the consummation of marriage in the full halakhic sense — takes effect only at three years and a day.

### Yibum & Adultery

If she was given in marriage by her father and her husband died without children, the *mitzva* of *yibum* falls on the surviving brother. *Yibum* is effected through intercourse, and therefore *yabam kanah* — the brother-in-law acquires her through the act — only when she is three years and a day old. Similarly, if another man has intercourse with a girl who has been betrothed or married, this constitutes adultery — *eshes ish* — only when she is three years and a day old. Below that threshold, the act does not rise to the level of a recognized sexual act, and the liability of *eshes ish* does not apply.

### Niddah Intercourse

If the girl is a *niddah* and a man has intercourse with her when she is three years and a day old, the man becomes *tamei* in the manner of one who has relations with a *niddah*. The *mishna* specifies *mishkav tachton ke-elyon* — his own *mishkav* takes on a secondary level of *tumah*, equivalent to what is carried above a *zav* rather than the primary *tumah* of the *zav* himself. This specific consequence flows from the act being considered a recognized *bi'ah*, which only applies at three years and a day.

### Wife to Kohen

If she is the wife of a *kohen* through *nesuin* — and again, *nesuin* is only effective once she is three years and a day — she eats *terumah* by virtue of that marriage. *Kiddushin* alone, prior to *nesuin*, does not confer the right to eat *terumah*.

### Pasul Kohen

If a man who is *pasul* to marry a *kohen* — a *chalal*, a *mamzer*, or another *pasul* — has intercourse with a girl who is three years and a day old, she becomes *pesulah min ha-kehunah* — invalid to marry a *kohen*. This disqualification flows from the act being a recognized *bi'ah*. Below three years and a day, no such *bi'ah* is recognized, and therefore no disqualification results.

### Arayos Intercourse

If a man commits one of the forbidden *arayos* with her — a case of incest, for instance — when she is three years and a day old, both parties are subject to the relevant penalties, including death in those cases where the *arayos* carries a capital punishment. The perpetrator is of course fully liable; the girl, being below the age of adulthood, is exempt. The point is that the severity of the *isur* and its consequences apply only once the act is recognized as *bi'ah*, i.e., at three years and a day.

### Under 3 Years

The *mishna* concludes with the formulation for intercourse with a girl below three years and a day: *ke-noten etzba ba-ayin* — it is like putting a finger in an eye. The metaphor apparently points to the following biological assumption underlying the *mishna*: below three years, the hymen is not ruptured permanently, or if ruptured, it grows back. The act therefore leaves no lasting physical change, and on that basis it is not considered a recognized act of *bi'ah*. This seems to be the *mishna*'s understanding of why three years functions as the dividing line — though the analogy is obscure, and the *Gemara*'s discussion of this will continue in the next session.

## Gemara: Textual Derivations

### Examining the Metaphor

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ' תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: בַּת שָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנִים מִתְקַדֶּשֶׁת בְּבִיאָה, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי מֵאִיר. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: בַּת שָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנִים וְיוֹם אֶחָד.
>
> **Translation:** GEMARA: **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: **A girl** who is **three years** old **is betrothed through intercourse;** this is **the statement of Rabbi Meir. And the Rabbis say:** She must be **three years and one day old.**
