# Niddah 44 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Tumah to Development

### Daf Overview

Daf 44 opens with the *mishna* found at the bottom of 43b. Until this point, the *masechet* has been concerned with the details of *tumah* and *tahara*: precisely when *tumah* takes effect, where it originates in the body, and the distinctions between touching and carrying. With the *mishna* before us, the *masechet* pivots to an entirely new subject — the ages of development of boys and girls and the different *halakhot* that apply at different stages of their maturity.

### Niddah Blood

By way of brief clarification before proceeding: concerning the blood of a *niddah*, it is worth noting that *mishkav* of a *niddah* and similar matters convey *tumah* not only through touching but even through carrying. This distinction will not occupy us further here, but it is important to have it correct.

## Infants: Niddah/Zavah

### Female Infant Tumah

The *mishna* states: *tinoket bat yom echad metame'ah be-niddah* — a baby girl even one day old, if she produces uterine bleeding, is considered a *niddah*. We have encountered this *halakha* before, but it bears emphasis here because it goes to the heart of a fundamental question: to what degree is *niddah* a formal legal category, and to what degree is it organically connected to the physiological phenomenon of menstruation? The answer is clearly that it is a formal definition, since a one-day-old infant can acquire the status.

The *mishna* continues: *bat asarah yamim metame'ah be-zivah* — at ten days old she can be a *zavah*. This is because the period of *zivah* begins only on the eighth day after the first bleeding. For a girl to become a *zavah gedolah* she must bleed on three consecutive days — days eight, nine, and ten — meaning the earliest she can attain that status is when she is ten days old.

### Male Infant Zav

Regarding infant boys: *tinok ben yom echad metameh be-zivah* — a one-day-old male infant who produces a penile discharge of the type associated with *zivah* can become a *zav*. However, he cannot be a *ba'al keri*, because the *tumah* of *shikhvat zera* applies only when the emission occurs in the context of what is halakhically recognized as an act of intercourse, which requires the boy to be at least nine years old and a day. Even if what emerges from him is biologically semen, it does not carry the *tumah* of *shikhvat zera*. That is why *shikhvat zera* is not listed among the *tumot* applicable to infants.

### Negaim & Met Tumah

The *mishna* adds that even a one-day-old child can become *tamei* through *tzara'at* and can become *tamei met* — *tamei* through contact with a corpse. These two are listed explicitly, and *Tosafot* explains why.

### Tosafot on Psukim

*Tosafot* notes that by *nega'im* the Torah writes *ish o ishah* — man or woman — which might imply that only adults are subject to *tzara'at*. Similarly, by *tumah* from a corpse the verse writes *ha-ish asher yitma* — the man who becomes *tamei* — which again suggests limitation to adults. The *mishna* therefore specifies that even a one-day-old child is subject to both, and the *Gemara* will derive this from the *pesukim* through alternative textual readings. These two *tumot* are singled out precisely because the language of the verses might have led us to restrict them to adults.

## Birth Threshold

### Yibum & Birth

The *mishna* now turns to a category that works in the opposite direction: here, the novelty is not that an infant is included, but that a fetus is excluded. The *mishna* states that a born child can create the obligation of *yibum* — *zokeket le-yibum* — and can exempt a widow from *yibum* — *poteret min ha-yibum*.

To explain: if a man dies and his brother was already born at the time of death, that surviving brother creates the *mitzva* of *yibum* with the deceased's wife. If, however, the brother was still a fetus at the time of the brother's death, there is no *mitzva* of *yibum*. He must have been born — an actual living person — at the moment the obligation would take effect. This is the reverse of the *chiddush* above: whereas with *tumah* the surprise was that even an infant is included, here the surprise is that a fetus does not yet suffice. Only birth confers the necessary legal standing.

Similarly, regarding *poteret min ha-yibum*: if a surviving wife is pregnant with her dead husband's child, she is not yet exempt from *yibum*. She must wait to see whether the child will be born as a viable living infant. Even if she is pregnant, this does not yet constitute having a child in the halakhic sense that would dissolve the *yibum* obligation. Only once the child is actually born does she become exempt. In the meantime, she cannot remarry, for the child may yet be born and retroactively establish that *yibum* was never required.

One might ask: does the child need to survive for a particular period after birth? The answer is no — even a child who lives only one day suffices, provided there is no indication that it was never viable. Once born, even briefly, it exempts the mother from *yibum*.

### Terumah Via Child

The *mishna* states *uma'akhil ba-terumah* — a born child can enable his mother to eat *terumah*. This refers to the following case: a non-*kohen* woman married to a *kohen* may eat *terumah* by virtue of her marriage. If her husband dies, she may continue to eat *terumah* by virtue of her surviving children. However, if she is merely pregnant and has not yet given birth, she cannot yet eat *terumah* on that basis. Only once the child is born can his existence grant her the right. It is worth noting the halakhic logic here: her right to *terumah* after her husband's death rests not on her own blood connection to *kehuna* — she has none — but on being the mother of children who are *kohanim* or *benot kohen*. Since that status requires an actual born person, the child must be born before she can claim the benefit.

### Pregnant Bas Kohen

The *mishna* then states *uposseil min ha-terumah* — a born child can disqualify his mother from eating *terumah*. This is the reverse case: a *bat kohen* who married a *Yisrael* loses her right to eat *terumah* by virtue of her marriage into a non-*kohen* household. If her *Yisrael* husband dies and she has no children and is not pregnant, she returns to her father's house and resumes eating *terumah*. But if she has a child from her *Yisrael* husband, she remains connected to that household and cannot eat *terumah*.

The *mishna*'s language implies that it is only the *born* child that prevents her return — suggesting that if she were merely pregnant, she could go back. The *Gemara*, however, challenges this reading directly, since the *halakha* is that pregnancy alone also prevents her return. This challenge will be addressed at length below.

### Nachal & Hanchil

The *mishna* states that the child must be born to inherit — *nochal* — and to bequeath — *manchil*. If his father dies while he is still in the womb, he does not yet inherit. Likewise, he cannot bequeath until he is born. The significance of this distinction will be unpacked by the *Gemara*.

### Horgo Chayav

The *mishna* states *ve-hargo chayav* — only once the child is born is killing him considered murder. Prior to birth, the taking of a fetal life is not *categorized* as murder in the sense that would carry the death penalty. This of course opens onto the broader question of abortion, which the *Gemara* and later authorities will discuss.

### Aveilus & Choson

Finally, the *mishna* states: *va-harei hu le-aviv ule-imo ule-khol kerovav ke-chatan shalem* — once he is born, he is to his father, his mother, and all his relatives like a complete *chatan*. The *Gemara* will explain that the halakhic significance of this phrase is with respect to *aveilus*: if the infant dies, the full laws of mourning apply even for a one-day-old child.

## Gemara: Textual Derivati…

### Niddah from Vav

The *Gemara* asks: *mena hanei milei* — from where do we derive that an infant girl can be a *niddah*? A *beraita* is cited: the verse writes *isha* — a woman. I might have thought only an adult woman. How do I know even a one-day-old girl? *Talmud lomar ve-isha* — the *vav* prefixed to the word *isha* extends the category to include any female, even an infant.

### Zavah from Vav

The same derivation applies by *zavah*. There too the verse writes *isha*, and one might think only an adult. The *vav* in *ve-isha* teaches us that any female — even a ten-day-old infant — can become a *zavah*. The two *derashot* are parallel in form but are derived independently: one *ve-isha* by the laws of *niddah*, and one *ve-isha* by the laws of *zavah*.

### Zav from Ish Ish

For a male infant as *zav*, the derivation is different. The verse concerning *zav* uses the doubled formulation *ish ish* — any man. *Mah talmud lomar ish ish?* What does the doubling teach? *Le-rabot ben yom echad* — to include even a one-day-old infant, who can become a *zav*. This is the position of *Rabbi Yehuda*.

### Yishmael: Male/Female

*Rabbi Yishmael the son of Rabbi Yochanan ben Broka* disagrees and says one need not derive the inclusion of infants from the doubled *ish ish* at all. Another verse states *ve-ha-zav et zovo la-zachar ve-la-nekevah* — the one who has the flow, whether male or female. *La-zachar kol she-hu* — any type of male being, whether adult or minor; *la-nekevah kol she-hi* — any female, whether adult or minor. This verse explicitly covers any male or female regardless of age, rendering the *drasha* from *ish ish* unnecessary. According to this view, *ish ish* is a case of *dibra Torah ki-leshon benei adam* — the Torah sometimes doubles its language in the manner of ordinary human speech, without intending any specific inclusion.

It should be noted parenthetically that the principle of *dibra Torah ki-leshon benei adam* is characteristically associated with *stam* Rabbi Yishmael. Whether the *Rabbi Yishmael ben Rabbi Yochanan ben Broka* mentioned here is the same authority deserves further investigation.

### Negaim from Adam

How do we know that *tzara'at* applies even to infants, given that the relevant verse says *ish o ishah*? The *Gemara* derives it from a different verse: *adam ki yihye be-or besaro* — a person, when there is in the skin of his flesh... The word *adam* — person — carries no restriction as to age. *Adam kol she-hu* — any type of person, male or female, adult or infant.

### Met Tumah from Nefesh

Similarly, the *tumah* from a corpse is derived from the verse *ve-al ha-nefashot asher hayu sham* — that the ashes of the *parah adumah* are sprinkled upon the souls that were there. *Nefesh kol she-hu* — any soul, with no restriction to adult or male. Thus even a one-day-old infant becomes *tamei* through contact with a corpse.

## Birth Requirement: Yibum

### Achim Yachdav

The *Gemara* now turns to the derivations for the opposite direction — that birth is required. Regarding *zikuk le-yibum*, the verse states *ki yeshvu achim yachdav* — when brothers dwell together and one dies. The phrase *yeshvu yachdav* — dwelling together — means they were both simultaneously alive in the world. *Achim she-haya lahem yeshiva achat ba-olam* — brothers who shared a period of co-existence. If the younger brother was born only after his elder brother had already died, they never co-existed, and therefore there is no *mitzva* of *yibum*.

### Ven Ein Lo

Regarding *poter min ha-yibum* — exemption from *yibum* — the verse states *u-ven ein lo*, that the *yibum* obligation applies only when the deceased has no son. As long as the child has not yet been born, *leit lei* — there is no son. A fetus is a potential son, not yet a son in the halakhic sense. Only once he is born is the widow exempt.

### Pregnant Bas Kohen

The *Gemara* now raises a strong challenge against the *mishna*'s formulation of *posseil min ha-terumah*. The verse states *ve-zera ein lah* — when she has no offspring, the *bat kohen* who was widowed from her *Yisrael* husband can return and eat *terumah*. *Ve-ha issa* — but she does have seed, namely the fetus in her womb. Even a fetus should prevent her return.

### Knuracha Pshat

The *Gemara* presses further: the verse also states *ki-neuracha* — she returns like her youth, as if she could erase her past and go back to the state she was in when she was a young woman in her father's house. *Prat le-muberet* — this excludes a pregnant woman outright. If she is pregnant, she is plainly not returning as she was in her youth. So why does the *mishna* require the child to be born? Pregnancy alone should already prevent her return.

### Why Both Stated?

The *Gemara* answers with *tzarichi* — both formulations are necessary. Had the Torah stated only *ve-zera ein lah*, I might have thought that a child — who is a separate body — creates a profound enough change to prevent her return, since what began as one body has become two. But perhaps pregnancy, where she is still one person carrying something internal, is not a sufficient change. *Eima techal* — maybe she can still go back when pregnant. Therefore the Torah writes *ki-neuracha* — not like her youth — to tell us that even pregnancy prevents her return.

Conversely, had the Torah stated only *ki-neuracha*, I might have thought that pregnancy — which visibly transforms the person, *gufa malya*, a full body as opposed to an empty one — is sufficient to prevent return, but that a child who now lives independently somewhere poses no such obstacle. One might say *gufa srika*, the body is the same as it was, empty, and perhaps the distant child is not enough of a connection. Therefore the Torah states *ve-zera ein lah* — even a born child, even when the mother's body looks as it did before, remains enough of a link to her prior marriage to prevent return.

Thus each formulation teaches what the other cannot, and both are necessary.

### Rav Sheshet Resolution

The *Gemara* then asks: fine, the *pesukim* have been explained. But our *mishna* — why does it specify a *one-day-old* child for *posseil min ha-terumah*? Given the Torah's teaching that even pregnancy prevents return, the *mishna* should have said pregnancy alone prevents her from going back. *Amar Rav Sheshet*: the *mishna* is not speaking about the *bat kohen*'s own status at all. It is addressing an entirely different case.

### Two Wives Case

*Hacha be-mai askinan* — what is the *mishna* actually addressing? A *kohen* who had two wives: one a divorcée (*grusha*) and one a non-divorcée. Children born from the divorcée are *chalalim* and cannot eat *terumah*; children born from the non-divorcée are legitimate *kohanim* or *benot kohen* who can eat *terumah*. Now the *kohen* dies, leaving behind sons from both wives as well as slaves. Those slaves are co-owned by all heirs. A slave who is partly owned by a non-*kohen* cannot eat *terumah*. Since the son of the divorcée is a *chalal*, his ownership of a portion of the slaves disqualifies them from eating *terumah*.

The question is: when does the *chalal* son's ownership begin? Only once he is born — *ben yom echad* — can he inherit. If he was still a fetus when his father died, he could not yet inherit, and therefore his ownership share would not yet exist, and the slaves could eat *terumah*. But once he is born and inherits, the slaves become partly owned by a non-*kohen* and are disqualified.

This reading is explicitly directed against the position of *Rabbi Yosi*, who holds that a fetus can inherit and therefore could disqualify the slaves even before birth. The *mishna* comes to teach us that a fetus does not inherit — *ben yom echad in, ubar lo* — and that inheritance, with all its halakhic consequences, begins only at birth.

## Inheritance Details

### Nochal Umanchil

The *Gemara* returns to the direct language of the *mishna*: *nochal u-manchil* — only once born does the infant inherit from his father and bequeath to others upon his own death.

### Why Inheritance Matters

The *Gemara* asks a probing question: why does it even matter whether the infant inherited and bequeathed? If we take the case of three brothers — *Reuven*, *Shimon*, and *Levi* — and *Levi* is born one day old, inherits from the father, and then dies, his estate passes to *Reuven* and *Shimon*. But had *Levi* never been born, *Reuven* and *Shimon* would have inherited from the father directly. The end result appears identical. In what case does it actually matter whether *Levi* inherited and bequeathed?

### Bechor Reduction

*Mar the son of Rav Yosef* in the name of *Rava* gives the answer: *lomar she-mema'it be-chelek bechora* — the born infant reduces the *bechor*'s double portion. If there are only *Reuven* and *Shimon*, *Reuven* the *bechor* receives a double share — say, four units out of six. With *Levi* present, the estate is divided into four shares, with *Reuven* receiving two and the others one and a half each. When *Levi* dies, his one and a half is divided equally among the remaining brothers, including *Reuven*. *Reuven* ends up with less than the four units he would have received had *Levi* never existed. Thus the born-and-deceased infant's existence materially diminishes the *bechor*'s portion, and the distinction between inheritance by a born infant versus a fetus carries real halakhic consequence.

### Mother's Property Case

The *Gemara* also establishes the case of inheriting the mother's property to pass it on to paternal brothers. Here is where the distinction is starkest. Say a mother — the paradigmatic case is *Rachel* dying in childbirth — dies as her son is born. The newborn son inherits her estate. When he then dies, his paternal brothers inherit from him. As a result, the mother's property passes to her husband's other children, who are her son's paternal brothers. Had the son not been born at all, the mother's property would have reverted to her own blood relatives — her father's family. It is precisely because he was born, inherited, and bequeathed that the maternal property flows to the paternal line. *Nochal be-nichsei ha-em le-hanhil le-achiv min ha-av* — he inherits from his mother in order to pass on to his father's brothers.

## Fetal Viability

### Mother Dies First

Interestingly, the *Gemara* assumes something that goes beyond the simple legal principle. Rather than saying straightforwardly that a fetus lacks legal personhood and therefore cannot inherit, the *Gemara* leaves open the possibility that a fetus *could* inherit in utero. It asks why the infant must be born, and answers: *de-hu mayyit bereisha* — because when a mother dies, the assumption is that the fetus dies first. The mother is not yet dead when the fetus expires. Therefore the fetus cannot inherit from the mother, because by the time the mother's death takes effect, the fetus is already gone. *Ve-ein ha-ben yoresh et imo ba-kever* — a son cannot inherit from his mother once he is already dead.

This is a strikingly unusual reading of the *mishna*. The simple understanding would be that a fetus is simply not yet a legal person and cannot inherit regardless of the sequence of deaths. Our *Gemara*, however, appears to assume that perhaps a fetus *can* inherit — and the reason it does not in this case is purely the biological assumption about the order of death.

### Thrashing & Lizard

The *Gemara* challenges this assumption: *va-ha havi uvda u-firkes ad tlata pirkusei* — was there not an actual incident where a fetus thrashed three times after the mother's death, demonstrating that the fetus survived her? *Mar the son of Rav Ashi* responds: that proves nothing. *Midi de-hava aznav ha-leta'ah* — it is like the tail of a lizard. If you cut off a lizard's tail, the tail continues to thrash. That thrashing does not indicate ongoing life; it is a residual muscular reflex, not a sign of vitality.

### Tosafot: In a Box

*Tosafot* adds a further dimension. Even if we accept the *Gemara*'s position that the fetus typically dies before the mother, *Tosafot* notes the implication: if the mother dies first, the fetus might actually be considered as though already born. *Tosafot* uses the metaphor of a box — *de-ke-munach be-kupsa dami*: once the mother is dead, the fetus is no longer inside a living person but is, as it were, sitting in a container. He has independent viability. In that case, even if we otherwise hold that a fetus cannot inherit, the *Gemara*'s insistence that the fetus dies first may be necessary precisely because if the mother died first, the fetus would be deemed already born and capable of inheriting.

### Tosafot: Abortion Status

This leads *Tosafot* into a discussion that is of major significance for the laws of abortion. *Tosafot* raises the question of whether one who kills a fetus in its mother's womb is guilty of a capital offense. Given the uncertainty about the fetus's legal status — is it a person or not — the question arises on both sides of the ledger. And here *Tosafot* uses language that is striking: *muttar le-horgo be-veten* — it is permissible to kill him in utero.

### Mutar Language

The word *muttar* is stark and has attracted significant attention in the abortion literature. Most authorities understand abortion to be prohibited in some degree — the question is how severely — and *Tosafot*'s use of *muttar* is far more permissive than the consensus. Some later authorities argue that *Tosafot* did not intend this language literally, but the phrase is there and has played a role in the halakhic debate.

### Shabbat Paradox

*Tosafot* himself recognizes the resulting paradox and raises it explicitly. If one could say it is *muttar* to abort a fetus, why then can one violate *Shabbat* to save a fetus? *Amai mechallelin alav et ha-Shabbat?* The basis for *chilul Shabbat* is *pikuach nefesh* — saving a life. If the fetus is not a life, there should be no *pikuach nefesh* to justify *chilul Shabbat*. Yet halakhic practice clearly permits — indeed, requires — violating *Shabbat* to save a fetus in danger.

*Tosafot* answers: *de-mikkol makom mishum pikuach nefesh mechallelin alav et ha-Shabbat, af al gav de-muttar le-horgo* — nevertheless, because of *pikuach nefesh*, one violates *Shabbat* for the fetus even though abortion might be permissible. The two rulings coexist: a choice to end the pregnancy may be permitted, but if the pregnancy is being preserved, one violates *Shabbat* to protect it.

This paradox is not unique to the beginning of life. An analogous tension appears at the end of life, where in certain circumstances a person may have the right to refuse intervention and allow their own death, while at the same time, if they choose to live, every halakhic resource including *chilul Shabbat* is deployed to preserve them. *Tosafot* recognizes this as a genuine and unresolved tension, and the observation remains directly relevant to contemporary halakhic discussions.

## Inheritance Details…

### Born After Death

The *Gemara* returns to the laws of *bechor* and inheritance. *Mar the son of Rav Yosef* in the name of *Rava* teaches: if *Levi* was born after his father's death, his existence does not reduce *Reuven*'s double portion. In such a case, the size of the double portion is calculated based on the children who were alive at the time the father died. *Reuven*'s entitlement is set aside first, calculated as if *Levi* never existed, and then *Levi* inherits from the remainder together with the others.

Conversely, if the *bechor* himself was born after the father's death, he does not receive a double portion at all. *Yakir ba'inan* — the verse requires the father to recognize his firstborn son, meaning the father must have been alive when the son was born. If the *bechor* was born posthumously, the condition of recognition is not met, and the double portion does not apply.

### Halacha: Rav Yosef

The *Gemara* concludes: *ve-hilkheta ke-khol hanei lishnei be-Mar bar Rav Yosef mishemei de-Rava* — the *halakha* follows both rulings transmitted by *Mar the son of Rav Yosef* in the name of *Rava*: a posthumously born *bechor* does not receive a double portion, and a posthumously born ordinary brother does not reduce the *bechor*'s share.

## Horgo Chayav & Aveilus

### Murder Threshold

The *mishna* states *ve-hargo chayav* — only once the child is born does killing him constitute murder. A *pasuk* confirms this: *ve-ish ki yakkeh kol nefesh* — if a man strikes any soul. The phrase *kol nefesh* — any soul — teaches that even an infant is included; the *chiddush* of the *mishna* is not the birth requirement per se but that even a newborn counts as a full life for the purposes of murder. The birth requirement emerges as the other side of the same coin.

## Fetal Viability…

### Rav Moshe: Murder

It should be noted that *Rav Moshe Feinstein* took the position that abortion constitutes an act equivalent to murder, even if the perpetrator is exempt from the death penalty. This view is grounded in an ambiguous *Rambam* which *Rav Moshe* reads in support of that position. Prior to *Rav Moshe*, authorities debated the severity of abortion across a broad spectrum — from the relatively lenient to the very severe — but *Rav Moshe* appears to be the first to articulate explicitly that abortion is structurally equivalent to murder, with only the capital sanction removed.

## Horgo Chayav & Aveilus…

### Kol Nefesh

The verse *ve-ish ki yakkeh kol nefesh* — *kol nefesh* — applies to any soul, confirming that a newborn infant is fully protected under the laws of murder. The *chiddush* is inclusive rather than exclusive: it is telling us not that a fetus is excluded, but that even the youngest born child is included.

### Aveilus Day One

For the halakhic significance of the phrase *ke-chatan shalem* — like a full *chatan* — the *Gemara* states *amar Rav Pappa le-inyan aveilus*: full laws of mourning apply even upon the death of a one-day-old infant. *Shiva* is observed, and all the obligations of *aveilus* apply.

### 30-Day Viability

This ruling, however, is not in accordance with *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel*, who holds that only after a child has survived thirty days can we establish that it was viable. If the child dies within thirty days, it is presumed to have been a *nefel* — a non-viable birth — and no *aveilus* is observed. According to *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel*, a child who dies before thirty days is a *safek*, and the presumption against *aveilus* prevails.

### Early Viability Signs

The *Gemara* reconciles the ruling of our *mishna* with the position of *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* as follows: *hacha be-mai askinan de-khillu lo chodashav* — the *mishna* is speaking of a case where the full term of gestation was completed and observable. In such circumstances, the physical features and robustness of the infant may allow us to determine viability before thirty days. Even *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* would agree that when full gestation is evident, we do not need to wait the full thirty days before confirming viability and therefore before *aveilus* applies.

*Tosafot* adds a further clarification: the dispute between *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* and the *chakhamim* applies specifically when the infant dies from what appears to be natural weakness. But if the infant died from an external cause — say, a fall — and there was no indication of inherent frailty, the *chakhamim* would require *aveilus* even within thirty days, while *Rabban Shimon Ben Gamliel* would still demand the thirty-day threshold.

## 3 Years 1 Day

### 3-Year Threshold

The *mishna* now moves up the developmental ladder: *bat shalosh shanim ve-yom echad* — a girl three years and a day old. This is the threshold at which an act of sexual intercourse with her is halakhically recognized as an act of *bi'ah*. Below this age, the act is not considered *bi'ah* in the halakhic sense.

Before proceeding to the *mishna*'s list of consequences, it must be said plainly: the entire discussion presupposes the sexual violation of very young children. The *Gemara* treats it with complete legal detachment, noting thresholds and consequences without any remark on the horror of the underlying acts. When this material is studied — whether in *Daf Yomi* or in *yeshivot* — to engage with it without acknowledgment of that horror is itself a serious problem. The legal analysis is what it is; the human dimension cannot be bracketed.

### Kiddushin vs. Bia

A father can give his daughter in *kiddushin* through *kesef* or *shtar* even when she is one day old. But *kiddushin* effected through *bi'ah* — through intercourse — requires that she be three years and a day old, since only then is the act considered a valid act of *bi'ah*. So too, *nisuin* — the consummation of marriage in the full halakhic sense — takes effect only at three years and a day.

### Yibum & Adultery

If she was given in marriage by her father and her husband died without children, the *mitzva* of *yibum* falls on the surviving brother. *Yibum* is effected through intercourse, and therefore *yabam kanah* — the brother-in-law acquires her through the act — only when she is three years and a day old. Similarly, if another man has intercourse with a girl who has been betrothed or married, this constitutes adultery — *eshes ish* — only when she is three years and a day old. Below that threshold, the act does not rise to the level of a recognized sexual act, and the liability of *eshes ish* does not apply.

### Niddah Intercourse

If the girl is a *niddah* and a man has intercourse with her when she is three years and a day old, the man becomes *tamei* in the manner of one who has relations with a *niddah*. The *mishna* specifies *mishkav tachton ke-elyon* — his own *mishkav* takes on a secondary level of *tumah*, equivalent to what is carried above a *zav* rather than the primary *tumah* of the *zav* himself. This specific consequence flows from the act being considered a recognized *bi'ah*, which only applies at three years and a day.

### Wife to Kohen

If she is the wife of a *kohen* through *nesuin* — and again, *nesuin* is only effective once she is three years and a day — she eats *terumah* by virtue of that marriage. *Kiddushin* alone, prior to *nesuin*, does not confer the right to eat *terumah*.

### Pasul Kohen

If a man who is *pasul* to marry a *kohen* — a *chalal*, a *mamzer*, or another *pasul* — has intercourse with a girl who is three years and a day old, she becomes *pesulah min ha-kehunah* — invalid to marry a *kohen*. This disqualification flows from the act being a recognized *bi'ah*. Below three years and a day, no such *bi'ah* is recognized, and therefore no disqualification results.

### Arayos Intercourse

If a man commits one of the forbidden *arayos* with her — a case of incest, for instance — when she is three years and a day old, both parties are subject to the relevant penalties, including death in those cases where the *arayos* carries a capital punishment. The perpetrator is of course fully liable; the girl, being below the age of adulthood, is exempt. The point is that the severity of the *isur* and its consequences apply only once the act is recognized as *bi'ah*, i.e., at three years and a day.

### Under 3 Years

The *mishna* concludes with the formulation for intercourse with a girl below three years and a day: *ke-noten etzba ba-ayin* — it is like putting a finger in an eye. The metaphor apparently points to the following biological assumption underlying the *mishna*: below three years, the hymen is not ruptured permanently, or if ruptured, it grows back. The act therefore leaves no lasting physical change, and on that basis it is not considered a recognized act of *bi'ah*. This seems to be the *mishna*'s understanding of why three years functions as the dividing line — though the analogy is obscure, and the *Gemara*'s discussion of this will continue in the next session.
