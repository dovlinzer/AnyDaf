## Intro: Nashim & Yibum



### Nashim Definition

We begin *Seder Nashim*. The word *nashim* is generally translated as "women," but a very reasonable suggestion has been made that the more proper translation is actually "wives." The word *isha* in the plural yields *nashim*, and when we say, for instance, that *Shlomo* had many *nashim*, it does not mean women in general — it means wives. Indeed, so much of the tractates in this *seder* concern issues relating to wives: *Yevamot*, *Gittin*, *Kiddushin*, and even *Nedarim*, where, though the *Torah* also addresses young daughters and the rights of fathers to annul vows, the primary subject is husbands and fathers exercising those powers. It is worth pausing to reflect on what it means that women are treated largely as subject matter rather than as subjects in their own right.

### Masechet Structure

*Yevamot* is a very challenging *masechta* with which to open the *seder*. For *Daf Yomi* purposes, the ordering — largest *masechtos* first, as judged by number of chapters — is not easy on the learner. One begins with *Shabbat* and *Eruvin*, enormous tractates, and now with *Yevamot*, which will occupy us for many months. It would have been more pedagogically gentle to build up gradually. Nevertheless, that is the order, and we proceed.

### Yevamot Scope

While a good part of this *masechta* deals with the actual *mitzvah* of *yibum*, an equally important section addresses general issues of personal status. Questions of *mamzer*, of who may marry whom, of *patzu daka* and *krus shepcha* (those with certain physical disqualifications from the congregation), questions about individuals of uncertain Jewish status or uncertain paternity — all of these appear here. So too do matters concerning the women whom *kohanim* are forbidden to marry, extensive discussion of conversion (*gerus*), and even questions of *agunah*, which one might expect to find in *Gittin*. The question of whether a husband has died and whether a single witness suffices to establish that fact is treated at length in *Yevamot*. It is therefore more accurate to approach this tractate as the comprehensive treatment of personal status law, with the *mitzvah* of *yibum* as its central organizing theme rather than its only concern.

### Yabam Terminology

The literal meaning of *yabam* and *yevama* deserves mention: these terms mean brother-in-law and sister-in-law respectively. The word *yibum* is rendered in English as "levirate marriage" — and it turns out that "levirate" derives from the Latin *levir*, meaning a husband's brother. The Hebrew terms, however, carry a broader meaning beyond the technical *mitzvah* context; *yevama* simply means sister-in-law and *yabam* simply means brother-in-law, though both obviously also carry their specific technical legal senses.

### Yibum History

The practice of *yibum* is attested before *matan Torah*: we find it in the story of *Yehuda* and *Tamar*, and there are echoes of something like it in the book of *Rut*, though that case does not involve brothers. The *Ramban* draws on the *Rut* story to argue that *yibum* existed as an ancient cultural practice predating *matan Torah*, and that it originally applied not only to brothers but to any male relative. The *Torah* then came and, because of the prohibitions of *arayot*, restricted this practice generally — but preserved it in the one specific case of brothers.

### Lehakim Shem

This raises a profound question: what does it truly mean *lehakim shem achiv hamet* — to establish the name of the dead brother? It certainly cannot mean merely giving the child the dead brother's literal name, since we find no such practice in the *Torah* and none in the story of *Yehuda* and *Tamar* — indeed, *Yehuda* himself named the children. So why does the act of *yibum* specifically accomplish this purpose?

The *Ramban* hints at the idea of *gilgul neshamot* — some form of reincarnation of the dead brother's soul. The suggestion is that when conception occurs through a family member, it may allow for some continuity of the dead brother's soul in the child born from that union, even if one does not adopt a full doctrine of *gilgul neshamot*. There clearly seems to be something deeper operating on a metaphysical level: when this happens through a family member, the child represents a more genuine continuity of the dead father than would otherwise be possible.

### Yibum Halakha

For *halacha* purposes, the implications of *yibum* touch heavily on questions of inheritance — who inherits the dead brother's property — though we will see that this is not a matter of complete consensus. The entire complex of laws surrounding *yibum* is animated by this underlying tension between perpetuating the dead brother's name and line, and the network of legal relationships the act creates.

### Asei/Lo Taase

The central legal challenge of *yibum* is striking: the *mitzvah* of *yibum* requires a man to marry his dead brother's wife — yet the prohibition of *eshet ach* (a brother's wife) is among the most serious *arayot*, carrying the penalty of *kares*. How is this resolved? The non-simple answer is that this may be one of those cases where the *Torah* states two things that are essentially contradictory *be-dibbur echad* — as a single unified declaration. We find a parallel in *shamor ve-zachor*, though those two are not contradictory, merely two dimensions of *Shabbat*. More pertinently, there are cases where the *Torah* states two things that genuinely contradict one another and yet both are true — and *yevama yavo aleha* alongside the prohibition of *eshet ach* is one of the most powerful examples of this. Whether *yibum* fits the standard pattern of *asei doche lo ta'asei* — a positive commandment overriding a prohibition — or represents a more radical *chiddush* where the *Torah* itself holds the contradiction in simultaneous validity, is one of the foundational conceptual questions this *masechta* will address.

### Karaite Views

Non-rabbinic groups found their own solutions to this apparent contradiction. The *Karaites* offered a fascinating approach: they argued that the *Torah* only permits *yibum* precisely in the case where the man is *not* the actual brother — that when the *Torah* uses the word *achim*, it uses it loosely, as in *Avraham*'s words to *Lot*, *halo anashim achim anachnu*, where it does not mean literal brothers. On their reading, the *mitzvah* of *yibum* would apply only in a case like *Rut* — where the parties are related but not in a relationship of *issur erva*. That is how they resolve the apparent collision with the prohibition of a brother's wife. The *Essenes* (or another such group) proposed yet another solution — that *yibum* applied only to *eirusin* (betrothal) rather than *nissu'in* (full marriage) — another way of defusing the conflict with the *issur erva* of *eshet ach*. For *Chazal*, of course, that collision is precisely the point and the *chiddush*.

## Mishna Order



### Opening Sugya

At the beginning of this *masechta* we encounter what we find at the opening of many *masechtot*: an initial *stam* passage that is technically oriented and hyper-attentive to questions of language and grammatical precision in the *mishna*, rather than engaging with conceptual substance. Just as *Kiddushin* opens by asking why the *mishna* uses the formulation *ha-isha nikneit* rather than *ha-ish koneh*, and why the language of *kinyan* is employed, so too here. This type of opening is generally understood to represent one of the latest layers of the *Gemara*, perhaps added even after the main *stam* editorial work. *Rav Sherira Gaon*, in his famous letter, notes that these opening passages frequently serve as an opportunity to quote material that will appear later or to introduce ideas relevant to future discussions — they do not develop new ideas themselves. That is precisely what we will find here, as this *sugya* opens a window onto some of the complex material we will encounter later in the tractate.

The question the *Gemara* takes up is: what is the organizing principle behind the *mishna*'s list of the fifteen women whose status as *arayot* exempts them — and their *tzarot* (co-wives) — from *yibum* and *chalitza*? The *mishna* begins with *bito* (his daughter) and proceeds through granddaughter, then to wife's daughter and her descendants, then mother-in-law and grandmother-in-law, then sisters, and finally the various categories of wives. What determines this sequence?

### Bito Priority

The *Gemara* at the bottom of 2b says: *ella bito, keivan de'ati midrasha chaviva lei* — the reason the *mishna* begins with a daughter is that this prohibition is learned from *derasha* rather than being explicit in the verse, and it is therefore dear to the *tanna*. Tannaim show greater interest in foregrounding material they have themselves uncovered or derived through interpretation, rather than what is explicit in the text.

### Bito vs Bat Ishto

What does this mean — that the prohibition of a man's daughter is not explicit? *Rashi* quotes the relevant *pasuk*: *ervat isha u'vitah lo tegaleh, et bat bnah v'et bat bitah lo tikach legalot ervatah* — you shall not uncover the nakedness of a woman and her daughter, nor take her son's daughter or her daughter's daughter. Every time the *Torah* addresses this category, it speaks about a man's wife and *her* daughter, or *her* descendants. The most natural reading of *isha u'vitah* — a woman and her daughter — is: your wife and her daughter. The *rabbis* interpret this as: you are forbidden from your wife's daughter.

But what about his biological daughter who is not his wife's daughter — born, for instance, from a non-marital union? That prohibition nowhere appears explicit in the *Torah*. The *Gemara* therefore designates it a *milta de'ita bidrasha* — something learned through Rabbinic interpretation — and that is why the *tanna* opens with it. The *Gemara* refers to this case specifically as *bito me'anusato* (his daughter from a woman he violated), a formulation that is, it must be said, disturbing. Why the *Gemara* chose this example rather than *bito mi'mefutato* (his daughter from a woman he seduced) — itself already a painful category, but at least one that implies some measure of consent — is not entirely clear.

### Gezeira Shava

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא: בִּתּוֹ, כֵּיוָן דְּאָתְיָא מִדְּרָשָׁא, חַבִּיבָא לֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** the Gemara retracts the previous explanation in favor of the following: With regard to **his daughter, since** this prohibition is actually **derived from a homiletical interpretation** and is not explicitly stated in the Torah, **it is** therefore **dear** to the *tanna*. Consequently, he listed this novel case of a daughter first.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כּוּלְּהוּ נָמֵי מִדְּרָשָׁא אָתוּ! נְהִי דִּלְעִנְיַן יִיבּוּם אָתְיָין מִדְּרָשָׁא — עִיקָּר אִיסּוּרַיְיהוּ בְּהֶדְיָא כְּתִיב בְּהוּ. בִּתּוֹ, עִיקָּר אִיסּוּרָא מִדְּרָשָׁא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara challenges this conclusion: **All** of the exemptions from levirate marriage for forbidden relatives listed in the mishna **are also derived from a homiletical interpretation.** The Gemara responds: **Although the matter of levirate marriage is derived from a homiletical interpretation, the main** aspect **of their prohibition is explicitly written.** By contrast, with regard to **his daughter** from a woman he raped but did not marry, **the main** aspect **of** this **prohibition is** derived **by homiletical interpretation,** as the prohibition itself is not written explicitly in the Torah.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּאָמַר רָבָא: אֲמַר לִי רַב יִצְחָק בַּר אַבְדִּימִי: אָתְיָא ״הֵנָּה״ ״הֵנָּה״,
> **Translation:** **As Rava said: Rav Yitzḥak bar Avdimi said to me:** This prohibition is **derived** by means of a verbal analogy between the word ***hena*,** in the verse: “The nakedness of your son’s daughter, or of your daughter’s daughter, even their nakedness you shall not uncover; for theirs [*hena*] is your own nakedness” (Leviticus 18:10) and the word ***hena*** in a different verse: “You shall not uncover the nakedness of a woman and her daughter; you shall not take her son’s daughter, or her daughter’s daughter, to uncover her nakedness: they [*hena*] are near kinswomen; it is wickedness” (Leviticus 18:17), indicating that every daughter is prohibited, even one from rape, just like one’s daughter through his wife.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָתְיָא ״זִמָּה״ ״זִמָּה״.
> **Translation:** Furthermore, it is **derived** from a verbal analogy between the word **“wickedness”** (Leviticus 18:17) and the word **“wickedness”** in the verse: “And if a man take with his wife also her mother, it is wickedness; they shall be burnt with fire, both he and they, that there be no wickedness among you” (Leviticus 20:14), that one who has sexual intercourse with a daughter from a rape is liable to burning.

The *Gemara* then asks: *kulu nami midrasha atu* — but isn't everything in the *mishna* learned from *derasha*? The entire principle that *arayot* are exempt from *yibum* is derived through Rabbinic interpretation, not stated explicitly. So why should *bito* be privileged for this reason?

The *Gemara* answers: *nehi de'inyan yibum atyan midrasha* — true, the *yibum* exemption of all these *arayot* is learned from *derasha*, but *ikkar issuraihu bahedaya ketiv v'hu* — the fundamental fact that they are *arayot* at all is explicit in the text. Except for *bito*: *ikkar issura midrasha* — the very foundation of the prohibition of his biological daughter is itself a *derasha*. *De'amar Rava*, as *Rav Yitzchak bar Avdimi* taught him: *atya heina heina, atya zima zima* — we derive it through a *gezeira shava* from the words *heina-heina* and *zima-zima*.

How does this work? The *Torah* states concerning biological granddaughter: *ervat bat bincha o bat bitecha lo tegaleh ervatan ki ervat-cha heina* — the nakedness of your son's daughter or your daughter's daughter you shall not uncover, for their nakedness is your own. Notably, this verse addresses biological grandchildren — there is no mention of wife. The word *heina* appears here. Separately, in the passage about a wife's daughter and granddaughter, the *Torah* states: *isha u'vito lo tegaleh, sh'eira heina zima hi* — a woman and her daughter, their nakedness is *heina*, and it is *zima*. And in yet another verse: *zima hi, ba-esh yisrefu oto v'et-hen* — it is *zima*, they shall be burned in fire. And: *v'lo tihye zima betochechem* — there shall be no *zima* among you.

The *gezeira shava* operates as follows: the word *heina* in the verse about biological granddaughter links it to the verse about wife's daughter and granddaughter, where *heina* also appears. In the latter context, daughter is explicitly prohibited and the punishment of *sreifa* (burning) is stated. Through the *heina-heina* link, we establish that just as the wife's granddaughter is forbidden, so too is the biological granddaughter — and, by daisy-chaining, since the biological is linked via *heina* to a context where daughter is mentioned, daughter is also forbidden. Furthermore, since that context contains the word *zima*, and *zima* is linked to *sreifa*, the biological daughter is also subject to the same punishment. The prohibition of biological daughter is thus learned entirely from this *gezeira shava* — hence *ikkar issura midrasha*, and hence its pride of place at the opening of the *mishna*.

The *Gemara* then observes: *hashta de'amarta kol milta de'atya midrasha chaviva lei* — now that we have said the *tanna* favors things learned from *derasha*, *lisni la'achot ishto libasof* — the *mishna* should then have placed *achot ishto* (wife's sister) at the very end of the list. The reason is that *achot ishto* is the one *erva* in the list whose exemption from *yibum* is most explicit in the *pasuk* — as we will see when we turn the page. If the organizing principle is to lead with *derasha*-derived categories and defer what is explicit, wife's sister should come last.

The *Gemara* responds: *aidei de'ayei be'issur achvaso, tana achot ishto* — since we had already moved into discussing the category of siblings, the *mishna* included wife's sister there naturally. The organization shifted from being governed by *derasha* to being governed by logical grouping. Fine. But the *Gemara* pushes further: *lisni lehai bava libasof* — even if we accept that wife's sister belongs in the sibling section, why doesn't the entire sibling section come after the wife-category section, given that *drashot* should dominate?

### Korvei Ordering

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הַשְׁתָּא דְּאָמְרַתְּ כׇּל מִלְּתָא דְּאָתְיָא מִדְּרָשָׁא חַבִּיבָא לֵיהּ, לִיתְנֵי לַאֲחוֹת אִשָּׁה לְבַסּוֹף! אַיְּידֵי דְּאַיְירִי בְּאִיסּוּר אַחְווֹתָא, תְּנָא אֲחוֹת אִשְׁתּוֹ.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks: **Now that you said** that **all matters that are derived from a homiletical interpretation are dear to** the *tanna*, and therefore he gives them precedence, **let him teach** the case of **a wife’s sister last,** as this is the source of the *halakha* and is therefore the most straightforward case. The Gemara responds: **Since** the *tanna* **was dealing with the prohibition** with regard **to** different types of **sisters, he** also **taught** together with them the prohibition with regard to **his wife’s sister.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִיתְנְיֵיהּ לְהַאי בָּבָא לְבַסּוֹף! אֶלָּא: תַּנָּא קוּרְבֵי קוּרְבֵי נָקֵט: תְּנָא בִּתּוֹ וּבַת בִּתּוֹ וּבַת בְּנוֹ, דִּקְרוֹבֵי עַצְמוֹ,
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** if so, **let him teach this** entire **section** involving sisters **at the end,** when he mentions a wife’s sister. **Rather,** the Gemara rejects the above answer in favor of an alternative explanation: **The *tanna* cited** the cases in order of **closeness,** i.e., the mishna is ordered in accordance with the relative closeness of the various incestuous relations. How so? The *tanna* **taught** the cases of **one’s daughter, and the daughter of his daughter, and the daughter of his son, who are his own** blood **relatives.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַיְּידֵי דִּתְנָא שְׁלֹשָׁה דּוֹרוֹת לְמַטָּה דִּידֵיהּ, תְּנָא נָמֵי שְׁלֹשָׁה דּוֹרוֹת לְמַטָּה דִּידַהּ. וְאַיְּידֵי דִּתְנָא שְׁלֹשָׁה דּוֹרוֹת לְמַטָּה דִּידַהּ, תְּנָא שְׁלֹשָׁה דּוֹרוֹת לְמַעְלָה דִּידַהּ.
> **Translation:** **And since he taught three generations of one’s** offspring **below,** i.e., his daughter, his daughter’s daughter, and his son’s daughter, he **also taught three generations of** his wife’s offspring **below,** i.e., his wife herself, her daughter, and her daughter’s daughter. **And since he taught three generations of** his wife’s offspring **below, he** also **taught three generations of her** family **above,** i.e., from previous generations, namely herself, her mother, i.e., his mother-in-law, and his mother-in-law’s mother.

At this point the *Gemara* abandons the *derasha* theory entirely and offers what is clearly the straightforward explanation: *tana korvei korvei nakat* — the *mishna* lists the categories in order of closeness of relationship.

To see how this works, it helps to map out the structure of the *mishna* as a whole. The list opens with *bito* and her descendants — *bat bito*, *bat bno*, and so on. It then moves to *bat ishto* and her descendants. Next comes *chamoto* and *em chamoto*, moving upward through the mother-in-law line. Then comes *achoto* and the various sibling-category *arayot* — *achoto me'imo*, *achot ishto*, and so on. Finally, the *mishna* deals with three cases involving a wife: *eshet achiv me'imo*, *eshet achiv shelo haya beolamo*, and *kallato* — which is to say *eshet bno*. The organizing skeleton, then, is: daughter, mother, sister, wife. These are the four structural categories, and within each the *mishna* works through its relevant subcases before moving on.

*Tana bito u'bat bito u'bat bno de'korvei atzmo* — first come his own descendants: daughter, granddaughter. These are the people most closely related to him. *Ve'aidei de'tana shlosha dorot lemata didei, tana nami shlosha dorot lemata didah* — once having dealt with three generations downward on his own side, the *mishna* naturally turns to three generations downward on his wife's side — wife's daughter and granddaughter.

### Three Generations

Before arriving at the upward vertical, the *gemara* completes the downward movement on the wife's side. *Ve'aidei de'tana shlosha dorot lemata didei, tana nami shlosha dorot lemata didah* — having dealt with three generations downward on his own side, the *mishna* naturally turns to three generations downward on her side as well: wife's daughter and granddaughter. The logic is one of directional momentum: you are already moving downward, so you continue downward before changing direction. The same lateral impulse — *aydi detana* — then carries the *mishna* upward: *ve'aidei de'tana shlosha dorot lemata didah, tana shlosha dorot lemala didah* — and having gone three generations downward on her side, the *mishna* goes three generations upward on her side: mother-in-law and grandmother-in-law. The vertical dimension is thus exhausted — down three generations on his side, down three on hers, and up three on hers.

### Sister Inclusion

Then: *ve'tana achota* — we move horizontally to siblings. *Ve'achot ima, shekrovei atzma* — mother's sister, who is a blood relative of his wife. *Ve'achot ishto, achot ishto* — and then wife's sister, who is not his blood relative but fits within the horizontal relational category. The ordering is thus: vertical (downward, his side), vertical (downward, her side), vertical (upward, her side), horizontal (blood relatives — sisters), horizontal (his wife's sister by association with the sibling category).

### Wives Ordering

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּתְנָא אֲחוֹתוֹ וַאֲחוֹת אִמּוֹ דִּקְרוֹבֵי עַצְמוֹ. וְאַיְּידֵי דְּאַיְירִי בְּאִיסּוּר אַחְוָה, תְּנָא אֲחוֹת אִשְׁתּוֹ. וּבְדִין הוּא דְּלַיקְדְּמַהּ לְכַלָּתוֹ מִקַּמֵּי אֵשֶׁת אָחִיו שֶׁלֹּא הָיָה בְּעוֹלָמוֹ, דְּלָא מִשּׁוּם קוּרְבָּא הוּא דַּאֲסִירָא. אֶלָּא, אַיְּידֵי דְּאַיְירִי בְּאִיסּוּר אַחְוָה, תְּנָא אֵשֶׁת אָחִיו שֶׁלֹּא הָיָה בְּעוֹלָמוֹ, וַהֲדַר תְּנָא כַּלָּתוֹ.
> **Translation:** **And** afterward **he taught** the case of **his sister and his mother’s sister, who are his own** blood **relatives** but less closely related to him than his daughter. **And since he was dealing with the prohibition** with regard **to sisters, he** also **taught** the case of **his wife’s sister. And by right** the *tanna* should have **preceded** the case of **his daughter-in-law before** that of **a wife of a brother with whom he did not coexist, as it is not due to their relationship that she is prohibited** but rather because she is excluded from the mitzva of levirate marriage. **However, since he was dealing with the prohibition** with regard **to siblings, he taught** the case of **a wife of a brother with whom he did not coexist,** as this also involves a sibling. **And then** the *tanna* **taught** the case of **his daughter-in-law.**

Having exhausted the sibling category, the *mishna* turns to wives. Here a further wrinkle emerges: *badin hu dilekadmei lekallato mikamei eshet achiv shelo haya beolamo, delav mishum korva hu de-assira* — it would have been logical to list *kallato* (daughter-in-law, i.e., his son's wife) before *eshet achiv shelo haya beolamo* (the wife of a brother who was not yet born when the dead brother married her). The reason *kallato* should come first is that her prohibition is rooted in *korva* — closeness of relationship, the core organizing principle. *Eshet achiv shelo haya beolamo*, by contrast, is not primarily an *issur* of kinship; it arises from the absence of any *mitzat yibum* (obligation of *yibum*).

To clarify: if *Shimon* was married and died, *Reuven* performed *yibum*, and then *Levi* was subsequently born — when *Reuven* later dies, *Levi* has no obligation of *yibum* toward *Reuven*'s first wife (who was originally *Shimon*'s wife), because *Levi* was never in the world at the time of that original marriage. There was never a potential *mitzat yibum* between them. This is what *eshet achiv shelo haya beolamo* means: the wife of a brother toward whom he never had any obligation of *yibum*.

Logically, then, *kallato* should have preceded this case. But the *Gemara* explains: *ela aidei de'ayrei be'issur achva, tani eshet achiv shelo haya beolamo* — since we were already dealing with wives of siblings in the sibling section, the *mishna* listed the wives of siblings first: *eshet achiv me'imo* (wife of a maternal half-brother) and *eshet achiv shelo haya beolamo* both belong in the sibling-wives category. *Ve'hadar tani kallato* — only then does the *mishna* reach daughter-in-law. And this, the *Gemara* concludes, is entirely cogent: the organization proceeds by vertical relations, then horizontal relations, leading naturally into wives of those horizontal relatives, and finally arriving at *kallato*.

## Language: Potrot



### Potrot Mechanism

Having resolved the questions of ordering, the *Gemara* turns to a different linguistic issue: *u-mai irya detani potrot* — why does the *mishna* use the language "they exempt" (*potrot*) their *tzarot*, rather than "they forbid" (*osrot*) their *tzarot* from *yibum*?

### Assur vs Patur

*Ei tana osrot* — had the *mishna* said "they forbid," *hava amina assur leyabbem aval michlatz chaltza* — one might have thought it is forbidden to perform *yibum* but perhaps *chalitza* is still required. This is not an idle concern: there are indeed categories of women for whom, due to *safek* or rabbinic considerations, one does not perform *yibum* but does perform *chalitza*. If the *mishna* merely said "forbidden," a reader might conclude that a biblical obligation still lurks and requires *chalitza* to discharge it. The word "exempt" (*potrot*) forecloses that misunderstanding: these women are entirely exempt, from both *yibum* and *chalitza*.

The *Gemara* immediately challenges: *u-litni assura lachlotz* — be explicit then, say it is forbidden to perform *chalitza*, which conveys the same total exemption. The *Gemara* replies: *mai ka avid?* — but how could it be forbidden to perform *chalitza*? *Yibum* is forbidden because it is an act of sexual relations with *arayot*, carrying *kares* absent the *mitzvah*. But what *issur* attaches to removing a shoe and spitting in a court proceeding? *Alma lo? Im ata omer choletzes, misyabbemes* — because if we say she may perform *chalitza*, the next person will come along and perform *yibum*, reasoning that if there is a *chalitza* option there must be a *yibum* option. So while we cannot properly say it is *assur* to perform *chalitza* (since *chalitza* itself is a neutral act), we also cannot allow it, lest it signal that *yibum* is on the table.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַאי אִירְיָא דִּתְנָא ״פּוֹטְרוֹת״? לִיתְנֵי ״אוֹסְרוֹת״! אִי תְּנָא ״אוֹסְרוֹת״, הֲוָה אָמֵינָא אָסוּר לְיַיבֵּם, אֲבָל מִיחְלָץ — חָלְצָה, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara continues to analyze the language of the mishna: **And why does** the *tanna* **specifically teach: They exempt** their rival wives? **Let him teach: They prohibit** them, as ultimately the rival wives are not only exempt from levirate marriage but each is actually forbidden to her *yavam*. The Gemara explains that **if** he had **taught: They prohibit** them, **I would say** that this means **it is prohibited to enter into levirate marriage, but she must perform *ḥalitza*.** Therefore, **it teaches us** using the language of exemption to indicate that she is entirely exempt and does not even perform *ḥalitza*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִיתְנֵי ״אֲסוּרָה לַחְלוֹץ״! מַאי קָעָבֵיד? אַלְּמָה לֹא, אִם אַתָּה אוֹמֵר ״חוֹלֶצֶת״, מִתְיַיבֶּמֶת!
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: If so, **let him teach: She is prohibited from performing *ḥalitza*.** The Gemara rejects this suggestion: It is impossible to formulate the *halakha* in this manner, as even if they perform *ḥalitza*, **what has he** really **done?** What is wrong with a man allowing a woman to take off his shoe, which is the act of *ḥalitza*? Consequently, it is not possible to state: Prohibited from performing *ḥalitza*. The Gemara asks: **Why not?** There is in fact a prohibited element here: **If you say she performs *ḥalitza*,** it might be said that **she may** also **enter into levirate marriage** and that in this specific instance he performed *ḥalitza* merely because he did not wish to marry her. In that case, it might erroneously be claimed that if another wished to marry the rival wife of his *yevama* he is permitted to enter into levirate marriage.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כֵּיוָן דְּבִמְקוֹם מִצְוָה הוּא דַּאֲסִירָה צָרָה, וְשֶׁלֹּא בִּמְקוֹם מִצְוָה שַׁרְיָא, מִשּׁוּם הָכִי תָּנֵי ״פּוֹטְרוֹת״.
> **Translation:** In light of the previous argument, the Gemara suggests another reason that the mishna does not use the expression: Prohibited. **Since** it is only **in cases where the mitzva** of levirate marriage **applies that the rival wife is forbidden** to him, **and where no mitzva applies she is permitted,** as it is permitted for the man to marry the widowed rival wife of a non-relative, **due to that** reason **he taught: They exempt** their rival wives, and not: **They prohibit** them. In other words, the *tanna* is teaching that they are not prohibited to him in their own right.

### Tzara Mechanism


So why not simply say *assur lachlotz* to communicate this? The *Gemara* explains: *kevan debimakom mitzvah hu de-assira tzara, ve-shelo bimakom mitzvah sharya, mishum hachi tana potrot* — because the *tzara* becomes forbidden specifically in the context of *mitzva*, that is, in the context of a brother's wife. If there is no *mitzat yibum*, then the *tzara* of an *erva* is perfectly permitted to the man — as long as they were not married to his brother.

This distinction is crucial. The mechanism is not that an *erva* renders her co-wife forbidden to the man as such. Rather, the *erva* removes the *mitzat yibum* entirely. Once the *mitzvah* of *yibum* is removed, the other woman defaults back to being prohibited as a brother's wife — because there is no *mitzva* to override that prohibition. But in a case where the same woman is an *erva* to someone she is not related to through a brother — say, her father is married to two women and one of them is his daughter — the daughter does not make the other wife forbidden to the father, because there was never any *mitzat yibum* to remove in the first place.

This is why the *mishna* had to say *potrot* — "exempt" — rather than anything conveying a direct prohibition on the *tzara*. The mechanism is the removal of the *mitzva*, not the imposition of a new *issur*. *Mishum hachi tana potrot*: that is precisely the conceptual reality being described.

### Chalitza Priority

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמַאי אִירְיָא דְּתָנֵי ״מִן הַחֲלִיצָה וּמִן הַיִּיבּוּם״? לִיתְנֵי ״מִן הַיִּיבּוּם״ לְחוֹדֵיהּ! אִי תְּנָא ״מִן הַיִּיבּוּם״ הֲוָה אָמֵינָא מִיחְלָץ — חָלְצָה, יַבּוֹמֵי — לָא מְיַיבְּמָה, קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן כׇּל הָעוֹלֶה לְיִיבּוּם — עוֹלֶה לַחֲלִיצָה, וְכֹל שֶׁאֵינוֹ עוֹלֶה לַיִּיבּוּם — אֵינוֹ עוֹלֶה לַחֲלִיצָה.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara continues its analysis of the wording of the mishna. **And why does** the *tanna* **specifically teach:** Exempt **from *ḥalitza* and from levirate marriage? Let him teach:** Exempt **from levirate marriage, alone.** The Gemara answers: **If** he were to **teach: From levirate marriage, I would say** that **she must perform *ḥalitza*** and **she must not enter into levirate marriage.** The *tanna* therefore **teaches us** that **every** woman **who is eligible for levirate marriage is eligible for *ḥalitza*, and anyone who is ineligible for levirate marriage is** likewise **ineligible for *ḥalitza*,** as no obligation of *ḥalitza* applies unless there is an obligation of a levirate marriage.

*U-mai arya detani min hachalitzah u-min hayibbum* — the *Gemara* next asks: why does the *mishna* list *chalitza* before *yibum*? Since *yibum* is the primary *mitzvah*, should it not have been mentioned first? *Ei tani min hayibbum, hava amina michlatz chaltza yibumi lo mivama* — if the *mishna* said only "exempt from *yibum*," one might have thought: do not perform *yibum* but do perform *chalitza*, since perhaps the *yibum* exemption is only rabbinic while a biblical *chalitza* obligation remains. *Ka mashma lan* that this is not so: *kol ha'oleh leyibum oleh lachalitza, ve-chol she'eino oleh leyibum eino oleh lachalitza* — whoever is subject to *yibum* is subject to *chalitza*, and whoever is not subject to *yibum* is not subject to *chalitza*. At the biblical level they are a package: one implies the other. Hence the *mishna* must mention both, to prevent any inference that exemption from one leaves the other in place.

But then the *Gemara* asks: *ve-litni min hayibum umin hachalitza* — fine, say both, but why list *chalitza* first? *Abba Shaul hi* — the *mishna* follows the position of *Abba Shaul*, *de'amar mitzvat chalitza kodemet lemitzvat yibum* — who held that *chalitza* is the preferred mode, taking precedence over *yibum*.

### Leshem Mitzvah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְלִיתְנֵי ״מִן הַיִּיבּוּם וּמִן הַחֲלִיצָה״, אִי נָמֵי ״מִן הַחֲלִיצָה״ לְחוֹדַהּ? אַבָּא שָׁאוּל הִיא, דְּאָמַר: מִצְוֹת חֲלִיצָה קוֹדֶמֶת לְמִצְוֹת יַיבּוּם.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara further inquires: **And let him teach: From levirate marriage and from *ḥalitza*,** as the Torah states the option of levirate marriage first. **Alternatively,** let him teach: **From *ḥalitza*, alone,** as this would indicate that she may not enter into levirate marriage either. The Gemara answers: **This** mishna **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Abba Shaul, who said: The mitzva of *ḥalitza* takes precedence over the mitzva of levirate marriage,** as he maintains that one who does not enter into levirate marriage for the sake of Heaven transgresses the prohibition against marrying one’s brother’s wife, and therefore it would be better in every case to perform *ḥalitza*. Consequently, the *tanna* mentions *ḥalitza* before levirate marriage.

*Rashi* at this juncture quotes *Abba Shaul* from a later chapter: *Abba Shaul omer hachones yevamto leshum noy leshum ishut ke'ilu pogeah be'erva* — one who takes his *yevama* in marriage for reasons of attraction or desire for marriage is as one who transgresses the *issur erva*. The concern is that if a man actually wishes to marry this woman — because he finds her attractive or genuinely desires to wed her — the act of *yibum* is no longer *leshem mitzvah*, no longer performed as a religious obligation, but as an ordinary marital pursuit. *Ve-karov be'einai lihyot ha-vlad mamzer* — and it is close in his view to the child being a *mamzer*, because it approaches the quality of *issur eshet ach* when the act is not performed entirely for the sake of the *mitzvah*. This is a striking position: one might assume that genuine mutual interest between the parties would be a positive factor, yet *Abba Shaul* regards motivation by attraction as what transforms the act from *mitzvah* to something approaching transgression. Following *Abba Shaul*'s view, the *mishna* places *chalitza* first to underscore that it is the preferred approach.

## Fifteen Women



[DAF:3b]
### Rav Disputes

The *Gemara* now turns to the number itself: *minyana derisha lema'utei mai, minyana deseifa lema'utei mai* — why does the *mishna* need to specify fifteen? And why does it reiterate at the end "these women are exempt"? These specifications serve to exclude certain positions: *lema'utei derav vederav Assi* — to exclude the views of *Rav* and *Rav Assi*, who wished to add additional categories of women exempt from *yibum* to the list.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִנְיָנָא דְּרֵישָׁא לְמַעוֹטֵי מַאי, וּמִנְיָנָא דְּסֵיפָא לְמַעוֹטֵי מַאי?
> **Translation:** § The Gemara asks another question with regard to the language of the mishna: **The enumeration of the first clause** of the mishna: Fifteen women, which indicates that those women alone are included in this list, serves **to exclude what?** Which other cases might have been included? **And the enumeration of the latter clause,** which states: These exempt their rival wives, again meaning these and no others, serves **to exclude what?** Since the mishna specifies only these women and no others, the Gemara asks which other women might have been included in these lists.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְמַעוֹטֵי דְּרַב וּדְרַב אַסִּי. לְרַב וּלְרַב אַסִּי לְמַעוֹטֵי מַאי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers that these enumerations come **to exclude** those additions **of Rav and of Rav Asi.** Rav added the rival wife of a woman suspected by her husband of adultery [*sota*], while Rav Asi added the rival wife of an *aylonit*. The Gemara asks: **And according to** the opinion of **Rav and according to** the opinion of **Rav Asi,** the enumeration of the mishna comes **to exclude what?**

### Suta/Aylonit


*Rav* proposed the case of *tzarat sotah* — the co-wife of a woman who has committed adultery (or is at minimum suspected of it). A *sotah* is forbidden to her husband; their relationship is, in terms of conjugal life and the possibility of conceiving a child together, broken. The principle of *yibum* exists because the surviving brother steps into the dead brother's place to continue the possibility of children and continuity. A dead brother who could not have had licit relations with his wife — because she was a *sotah* — never had that viable conjugal relationship. Therefore, *Rav* argued, the surviving brother has no *mitzat yibum* with her. Fine. But *Rav* went further: if there is no *yibum* obligation with respect to the *sotah* herself, her *tzara* (co-wife) should likewise be exempt. This is the case of *tzarat sotah*. Crucially, the *sotah* is not an *erva* — the *issur* does not arise from kinship but from the state of the marital relationship. *Rav* nonetheless maintained that the *tzara* principle applies.

*Rav Assi*'s case is *tzarat aylonit* — the co-wife of a woman who is incapable of bearing children. An *aylonit* presents a different kind of problem: not a marital breakdown through prohibited conduct, but a physical impossibility of conception. *Rav Assi* held that since there is no viable prospect of progeny from the union with the *aylonit*, there is no *mitzat yibum* to her, and therefore her *tzara* is also exempt. Again, the *aylonit* is not an *erva*; the exemption arises from the nature of the relationship's inability to fulfill the purpose of *yibum*.

Both *Rav* and *Rav Assi* thus held that the *tzara* principle — which the *mishna* applies only to cases of *arayot* — extends to cases where there is no *yibum* obligation for a different reason. The *mishna*'s specification of precisely fifteen women, the *Gemara* argues, rejects this expansion: the co-wife of a *sotah* and the co-wife of an *aylonit* are not exempt; *yibum* would still apply to them because the *tzara* principle operates only where the primary woman is an *erva*.

### Tzara Exclusions

The *Gemara* then poses the question from *Rav* and *Rav Assi*'s perspective: *u'l'Rav u'l'Rav Assi l'mautei mai?* If they believe their cases are correct and not excluded by the *mishna*, why does the *mishna* specify only fifteen? What do *they* say the number is excluding?

The *Gemara* answers: *ei sevira lehu d'hadadei* — if *Rav* holds of *Rav Assi*'s case and *Rav Assi* holds of *Rav*'s case, so that they agree with one another on both, then the two instances of numerical specification in the *mishna* — "fifteen" at the beginning and "these women" at the close — each exclude one of two other cases: *chada l'mautei tzarat m'ma'enet v'chada l'mautei tzarat machzir gerushato* — one excludes the *tzara* of a *mema'enet* and the other excludes the *tzara* of a *machzir gerushato*.

And if they do not hold of one another — if *Rav* does not accept *Rav Assi*'s case and vice versa — then *chada l'mautei d'chavreih* — one specification excludes the other's case, and *v'chada l'mautei idach m'ma'enet o tzarat machzir gerushato* — the other specification excludes one of the remaining two cases: either *mema'enet* or *machzir gerushato*.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי סְבִירָא לְהוּ דַּהֲדָדֵי — חֲדָא לְמַעוֹטֵי צָרַת מְמָאֶנֶת, וַחֲדָא לְמַעוֹטֵי צָרַת מַחֲזִיר גְּרוּשָׁתוֹ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara responds: **If each maintains** in accordance with the opinion **of the other,** then **one** enumeration of the mishna comes **to exclude the rival wife of a wife who performed refusal.** If the deceased brother had two wives, one of whom was a minor, and she refused the *yavam*, her rival wife is prohibited from levirate marriage with him. However, the latter is not entirely exempt and must perform *ḥalitza*. **And** the other **one comes to exclude the rival wife** of the wife **of one who remarries his divorcée,** i.e., a woman who was illicitly remarried by her former husband after she had been married to another man.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי לָא סְבִירָא לְהוּ דַּהֲדָדֵי — חֲדָא לְמַעוֹטֵי דְּחַבְרֵיהּ, וַחֲדָא לְמַעוֹטֵי אוֹ צָרַת מְמָאֶנֶת, אוֹ צָרַת מַחֲזִיר גְּרוּשָׁתוֹ.
> **Translation:** **And if** Rav and Rav Asi **do not** each **maintain** in accordance with the opinion **of the other,** then **one** enumeration comes **to exclude** the opinion **of the other,** as they do not agree that the *halakha* stated by the other should be included in the mishna, **and** the other **one** comes **to exclude** one of the above suggestions, **either the rival wife of** a wife **who performed refusal or the rival wife** of the wife **of one who remarries his divorcée.**

### Mema'enes Case


What are these two cases? *Mema'enet* refers to an underage girl who was married off by her brothers or mother (rather than by her father) after her father's death. Such a marriage is only rabbinic in character, and the girl retains the right of *mi'un* — refusal — to dissolve the marriage. The connection between *Reuven* and this underage wife is, at every level, a creation of rabbinic law. When *Reuven* dies, a *zika* — a quasi-marital attachment — transfers in some sense to *Shimon*, the surviving brother. But just as the girl could have performed *mi'un* to dissolve her marriage to *Reuven*, she can perform *mi'un* against *Shimon* and walk away from the *zika*. Once she does *mi'un*, *Shimon* can no longer perform *yibum* with her. The case of *tzarat mema'enet* would be: *Reuven* had two wives, and one was this underage girl who did *mi'un* — can *Shimon* still perform *yibum* with the other wife? The answer is yes. The entire structure here is rabbinic: the girl's marriage was rabbinic, her *mi'un* is rabbinic, the effect on the *zika* is rabbinic. It is therefore not at all surprising that her *mi'un* does not exempt the *tzara*, and the *mishna*'s fifteen cases necessarily exclude this scenario.

### Machzir Grushaso

The case of *machzir gerushato* — remarrying one's divorced wife — is more straightforward. The *Torah* prohibits a man from remarrying a woman he divorced if in the interim she was married to another man. In this scenario, *Yaakov* was married to *Leah*, divorced her, and then remarried her after she had been with another man (he was not permitted to do so, but he did). He was also always married to *Rachel*, whom he never divorced. When *Yaakov* dies, *Leah* cannot be subject to *yibum* because of the prohibition of *machzir gerushato*. But would *Rachel*'s obligation of *yibum* be affected? No. The *tzara* principle does not apply here. The *issur* of *machzir gerushato* differs in quality from that of *sotah*: the *sotah* prohibition bears primarily on sexual relations, whereas *machzir gerushato* is more precisely an *issur* on the act of remarriage itself. For that reason — which the *Gemara* leaves for later elaboration — the *issur* does not create a *tzara* effect that would exempt *Rachel*.

### Tzarat Tzara

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְרַב וּלְרַב אַסִּי, לִיתְנִינְהוּ?
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: If so, **according to** the opinion of **Rav and according to** the opinion of **Rav Asi, let** the *tanna* **teach these** cases. Since in their opinions there are more than fifteen women to whom the principle of the mishna applies, why weren’t they all stated by the *tanna* of the mishna? The Gemara answers: They were not taught because they do not completely fit all of the halakhic rulings here.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְפִי שֶׁאֵינָהּ בְּצָרַת צָרָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara elaborates: This is **because they do not** involve the case **of a rival wife of a rival wife.** With regard to the fifteen women listed, the discussion of the mishna concerning rival wives and rival wives of rival wives is appropriate. However, the two cases cited by Rav and Rav Asi do not leave room for such deliberations, as both a *sota* and an *aylonit* are exempt and forbidden equally to all of the brothers, because their prohibition does not result from a familial relation to one of the living brothers but from a personal issue relating to the women themselves. Since none of the brothers may marry her rival wife, there is no possibility of a rival wife of a rival wife, and consequently these cases were omitted from the mishna’s list of fifteen women.

The *Gemara* concludes by asking why *Rav* and *Rav Assi* did not simply include their cases in the list in the *mishna* if they believed the *tzara* principle applies there. The answer is: *lefi she'eino betzaras tzara* — because even according to *Rav* and *Rav Assi*, the *tzara* in their cases (whether *sotah* or *aylonit*) would not go further. In the *mishna*'s fifteen cases, the *erva* exempts not only her direct *tzara* but also the *tzarat tzara* — the co-wife of the co-wife — and this extends indefinitely. In the cases of *sotah* and *aylonit*, even if one accepts that the immediate *tzara* is exempt, that exemption does not propagate to subsequent levels. Since the *mishna*'s principle encompasses *tzarat tzara* and theirs does not, their cases are categorically different and were therefore deliberately excluded from the list.

*Rashi*, in his comment on the *mishna*, notes on the phrase *tzarat tzarata ad sof ha'olam* — the co-wife of the co-wife to the end of the world — that this is explained *b'masnisin meforesh*: the *mishna* itself, further along, will spell out what this means. *Rashi* normally writes *b'gemara meforesh* — the *Gemara* will explain — but here he notes that it is the *mishna* itself that provides the explanation, in a remarkably self-referential way, with the *mishna* later formulating its own question and then answering it by spelling out the scenario in detail.
