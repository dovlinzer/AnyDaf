# Sukkah 3 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Review: Sechach & Height

### Day One Summary

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כּוּלְּהוּ כְּרַבָּה לָא אָמְרִי, הָהוּא יְדִיעָה לְדוֹרוֹת הִיא.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains why each of the Sages cited his own source and did not accept the sources cited by the others. **All of them,** Rabbi Zeira and Rava, **did not say** that the fact that a *sukka* more than twenty cubits high is unfit is derived from the verse: "So that your future generations will know that I caused the children of Israel to reside in *sukkot* when I took them out of the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 23:43), **as** did **Rabba,** because in their opinion **that** verse does not mandate one to be aware that he is sitting in a *sukka*; rather, it mandates **knowledge for** future **generations** of the exodus from Egypt.

Today's *daf* is *daf* 3 of *Sukkah*. By way of review, the previous session introduced several major themes centered on the discussion of the height limit of twenty *amot*.

### Sechach Definition

The first and most fundamental point established is that the concept of *sukkah* is primarily defined by *sechach*. This was made clear from the way multiple *amoraim* explain the problem of excessive height — the concern is with the *sechach* being too high — and from the focus on *pesukim* that mention the word *sukkah*, which is understood to refer essentially to the *sechach*. The identity of a *sukkah* is thus very much tied up in its roof covering.

### Three Themes

Three major themes emerged from that discussion. The first is the question of permanence versus temporary dwelling: one reason given for the height restriction is that a *sukkah* must constitute a *dirat arai*, a temporary dwelling, and a structure too tall to be recognized as such would be invalid. The second theme is the visibility of the *sechach*: perhaps the requirement is that one be able to perceive the *sechach* overhead, and if it is too high, it will not register meaningfully in one's awareness — an idea loosely connected to the notion of *lema'an yedu doroteichem*, so that generations will know. The third theme is the *sechach*'s practical capacity to provide shade: if the *sechach* is too high, it will not effectively shade the space below.

It is worth noting an important qualification: the *Gemara* states that the question of temporary dwelling is not determined by building material — one may construct a *sukkah* from brick — so the permanence concern pertains to other features of the structure.

### Tosafot: Sechach

*Tosafot* raise a pointed question that goes beyond mere dimensions: can the *sechach* itself be so permanent that it comes to be defined as one's house? Their conclusion is that it can. One criterion *Tosafot* offer for *sechach* being considered permanent — and therefore no longer qualifying as a temporary roof covering — is that it prevents rain from penetrating entirely. There is also a tradition, apparently rooted in the *Yerushalmi*, regarding the ability to see the stars through the *sechach*, though *Tosafot*'s primary focus is on the rain criterion. Additional examples were raised: *sechach* nailed down with planks, nails, and plaster. Whatever the precise standard, the underlying principle is clear — the *sechach* cannot take on the character of a permanent roof.

## Queen Hilni Case

### Hilni Proof

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַעֲשֶׂה בְּהֵילֶנִי הַמַּלְכָּה בְּלוֹד שֶׁהָיְתָה סוּכָּתָהּ גְּבוֹהָה מֵעֶשְׂרִים אַמָּה, וְהָיוּ זְקֵנִים נִכְנָסִין וְיוֹצְאִין לְשָׁם וְלֹא אָמְרוּ לָהּ דָּבָר. אָמְרוּ לוֹ: מִשָּׁם רְאָיָיה? אִשָּׁה הָיְתָה וּפְטוּרָה מִן הַסּוּכָּה. אָמַר לָהֶן: וַהֲלֹא שִׁבְעָה בָּנִים הֲווֹ לָהּ, וְעוֹד: כׇּל מַעֲשֶׂיהָ לֹא עָשְׂתָה אֶלָּא עַל פִּי חֲכָמִים.
>
> **Translation:** There was **an incident involving Queen Helene in Lod where her *sukka* was more than twenty cubits high, and the Elders were entering and exiting** the *sukka* **and did not say anything to her** about the *sukka* not being fit. The Rabbis **said to him:** Is there **proof from there? She was,** after all, **a woman and** therefore **exempt from the** mitzva of ***sukka*.** Consequently, the fact that her *sukka* was not fit did not warrant a comment from the Elders. Rabbi Yehuda **said to them** in response: **Didn't she have seven sons** and therefore require a fit *sukka*? **And furthermore, she performed all of her actions only in accordance with** the directives of **the Sages.**

The *Gemara* now proceeds to test which of the three explanations for the twenty *amot* height limit is correct by examining the famous case of Queen *Hilni*, who sat in a *sukkah* taller than twenty *amot*. This case was invoked by *Rabbi Yehuda* as a proof that a *sukkah* exceeding twenty *amot* is valid. The Sages responded that her case proves nothing, since she was a woman and exempt from the obligation. *Rabbi Yehuda* replied that her sons were with her, and as the *Gemara* elaborated, her sons were at minimum rabbinically obligated from the standpoint of *chinuch*. The *Gemara* now attempts to determine which theoretical approach to the height limit can actually accommodate this case as a genuine point of debate.

### Walls Not Reaching

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר בְּשֶׁאֵין דְּפָנוֹת מַגִּיעוֹת לַסְּכָךְ מַחְלוֹקֶת, דַּרְכָּהּ שֶׁל מַלְכָּה לֵישֵׁב בְּסוּכָּה שֶׁאֵין דְּפָנוֹת מַגִּיעוֹת לַסְּכָךְ, מִשּׁוּם אַוֵּירָא.
>
> **Translation:** **Granted, according to the one,** Rabbi Yoshiya, **who said that** it is specifically **in** a case **where the walls** of the *sukka* **do not reach** up to **the roofing** that there is a **dispute** between Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis, the *baraita* can be explained as dealing with a *sukka* of that type, as **it is customary for a queen to reside in a *sukka* in which the walls do not reach** up to **the roofing, due to** the fresh **air** that circulates through the openings in the wall.

According to this view, the concern is whether one will perceive the *sechach*: if the walls reach the *sechach*, one's gaze is naturally drawn upward to where the walls meet the roof, and height poses no problem. The debate applies only when the walls fall short of the *sechach*. This explanation fits the *Hilni* case perfectly, since it is entirely plausible that her *sukkah* — a grand royal structure — was built with walls that did not extend to the *sechach* in order to allow for air circulation.

### Small Sukkah Case

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא לְמַאן דְּאָמַר בְּסוּכָּה קְטַנָּה מַחְלוֹקֶת, וְכִי דַּרְכָּהּ שֶׁל מַלְכָּה לֵישֵׁב בְּסוּכָּה קְטַנָּה?
>
> **Translation:** **However, according to the one who said** that it is specifically in the case of **a small *sukka*** that there is a **dispute** between Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis, in which case the *baraita* is referring to a case where Queen Helene resided in a small *sukka*, **is it customary for a queen to reside in a small *sukka***?

The *Gemara* then poses a difficulty against the alternative approach. But according to the view that the problem is the *sechach*'s inability to provide shade, and that this is a problem only for a small *sukkah* (since a small *sechach* covering a limited area will not provide shade if too high, whereas a larger *sechach* will shade effectively regardless of height), the debate applies only when the *sukkah* is very small, perhaps four by four *amot*. Given that, how could *Hilni*'s *sukkah* have been the subject of this debate? Would a queen sit in such a tiny *sukkah*? Surely her *sukkah* must have been large, in which case there would be no issue of height at all according to this opinion, and her case would prove nothing.

### Compartments

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר רַב אַדָּא: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא סוּכָּה הָעֲשׂוּיָה קִיטוֹנִיּוֹת קִיטוֹנִיּוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** **Rabba bar Rav Adda said:** This ruling **is necessary only** in the case of **a *sukka* that is constructed** with several **small rooms [*kitoniyyot*].** The *sukka* was large, but it was subdivided into many small rooms, each of which was smaller than four square cubits.

The solution is that the *sukkah* was constructed with small private compartments. The outer structure may indeed have been enormous, like a large building with rooms, but halachically the relevant *sukkah* is not judged by the scale of the outer structure. What matters is the space in which one sits, enclosed by its own four walls. If one sits within an inner chamber, the *sukkah* being evaluated is that chamber — and if the *sechach* directly above that chamber covers only a small area, the question of whether it provides shade at twenty *amot* of height is entirely germane. One might ask whether the walls of such a chamber would also need to reach the *sechach*. The answer is no: that requirement is the concern of the first opinion, which holds that the problem is the visibility of the *sechach*. Under the present opinion — that the problem is the *sechach*'s ability to provide shade — what matters is simply how large an area the *sechach* covers, not whether the walls reach it.

The *Gemara* then challenges this answer as well:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי דַּרְכָּהּ שֶׁל מַלְכָּה לֵישֵׁב בְּסוּכָּה הָעֲשׂוּיָה קִיטוֹנִיּוֹת קִיטוֹנִיּוֹת?
>
> **Translation:** **Is it** then **customary for a queen to reside in a *sukka* constructed** with several **small rooms** without leaving a large room in which she could assemble her family and servants?

### Outer & Side

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב אָשֵׁי: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא לְקִיטוֹנִיּוֹת שֶׁבָּהּ.
>
> **Translation:** **Rav Ashi said:** This ruling **is necessary only** with regard **to the compartments in** the *sukka*. It was indeed a large *sukka* with a large central room; however, there were many small rooms adjacent to the main room. It is with regard to this type of *sukka* that there is a tannaitic dispute.

### Rashi & Yehuda

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבָּנַן סָבְרִי: בָּנֶיהָ בְּסוּכָּה מְעַלְּיָא הֲווֹ יָתְבִי, וְאִיהִי יָתְבָה בְּקִיטוֹנִיּוֹת מִשּׁוּם צְנִיעוּתָא, וּמִשּׁוּם הָכִי לָא אָמְרִי לַהּ דָּבָר. וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה סָבַר: בָּנֶיהָ גַּבָּהּ הֲווֹ יָתְבִי, וַאֲפִילּוּ הָכִי לָא אָמְרִי לַהּ דָּבָר.
>
> **Translation:** **The Rabbis hold: Her sons were residing in a full-fledged *sukka*** with a large central room, which everyone agrees was fit. **However, she** often **resided in the small rooms due to modesty,** to avoid being in the public eye. **And for that** reason the Elders **did not say anything to her,** as even if the small rooms were too small relative to the height of the *sukka*, there was no problem since her sons did not sit in them. **And Rabbi Yehuda holds: Her sons would** occasionally **reside with her** in the small room, **and even so,** the Elders **did not say anything to her,** indicating that a *sukka* more than twenty cubits high is fit even in a small *sukka*.

*Rashi* explains the positions of the disputants as follows: the Sages reasoned that the sons were eating in the large main hall. Since there is no debate about whether a large *sukkah* poses a problem of height under this opinion (height only matters for a small *sukkah*), the sons' presence in a properly sized *sukkah* proves nothing. But *Rabbi Yehuda* held that the sons were with her in one of the small private chambers. And yet no one said anything to protest, which *Rabbi Yehuda* cites as proof that even in a small *sukkah*, the height above twenty *amot* is not disqualifying.

This raises an obvious difficulty: if all the sons were crowded into a tiny chamber, how could they all fit? Even a chamber of four *amot* by four *amot* would be very cramped, and if the minimum dimension is only seven *tefachim* by seven *tefachim*, it is inconceivable that seven sons could fit inside.

### Tosafot Dimensions

*Tosafot* resolve this difficulty by articulating an important principle: in order for a structure to constitute a *sukkah* of minimum size, it is not sufficient merely that it possess the minimum area — it must satisfy the minimum measurement in both length and width independently. A space that is three and a half *amot* wide but twenty *amot* long is not a minimum-sized *sukkah*, because in one dimension it falls short of the minimum. Accordingly, *Tosafot* suggest that the chamber could have been quite narrow in one direction — say, three and a half *amot* — while extending to twenty *amot* in the other direction, giving ample room for all seven sons to recline side by side, while still counting as a *sukkah* that is too small in the relevant dimension. The same logic would apply regarding the shade concern: for the *sechach* to provide shade, it must achieve the minimum span in both directions; it is not enough for the structure to be very long if it is insufficiently wide.

## Minimum Size

### Shmuel: Head/Body

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר יִצְחָק: הֲלָכָה, צְרִיכָה שֶׁתְּהֵא מַחְזֶקֶת רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ.
>
> **Translation:** **Rav Shmuel bar Yitzḥak said:** The ***halakha*** is that one's *sukka* **must be** large enough to **hold his head, and most of his** body, **and his table.**

Having examined the various approaches to the twenty *amot* height limit through the lens of the *Hilni* case, the *Gemara* now pivots entirely. The question of height is set aside for the moment, and attention turns to a new issue that arose incidentally — what is the minimum size of a *sukkah*? This applies to an ordinary *sukkah* of normal height, whether six or eight feet tall. The *halacha* is that a *sukkah* must be able to contain one's head, the majority of one's body, and one's table. This is significantly smaller than four *amot* by four *amot*, which is the position of *Rebbe* that will be introduced shortly. The requirement of "the majority of one's body" reflects the posture of reclining: since one eats reclining, the relevant measurement runs from the waist to the top of the head, approximately. The table — a small serving tray — measures roughly seven *tefachim* by seven *tefachim*, approximately two feet square.

### Rabbi Abba Query

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַבִּי אַבָּא: כְּמַאן, כְּבֵית שַׁמַּאי? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אֶלָּא כְּמַאן?!
>
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Abba said to him,** astonished: **In accordance with whose** opinion did you rule? Was it **in accordance with** the opinion of **Beit Shammai?** This is the subject of a dispute between Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel, and according to Beit Hillel it is sufficient for the *sukka* to be large enough to hold one's head and most of his body; it need not be large enough to hold his table as well. The halakhic ruling that you issued is in accordance with the opinion of Beit Shammai, despite the fact that in disputes between Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel, the *halakha* is in accordance with the opinion of Beit Hillel. Rav Shmuel bar Yitzḥak **said to him: Rather, in accordance with whose** opinion should I rule?

Who is this position going like? Is it following *Bet Shammai*? He replied: yes, and who else would I be following? This brief exchange raises a question: what does *Bet Shammai* have to do with the minimum size of a *sukkah*?

### Mishnah 28a

To understand this, the *Gemara* directs attention to the *mishna* on 28a, which will serve as the reference text for much of what follows on this *daf*.

## Table in House

### Mishna: Table Out

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִי שֶׁהָיָה רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ בַּסּוּכָּה וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ בְּתוֹךְ הַבַּיִת.
>
> **Translation:** In the case of **one whose head and most of his** body **were in the *sukka* and his table was in the house.**

### Shammai vs Hillel

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בֵּית שַׁמַּאי פּוֹסְלִין וּבֵית הִלֵּל מַכְשִׁירִין.
>
> **Translation:** **Beit Shammai deem it unfit and Beit Hillel deem it fit.**

### Yochanan Story

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרוּ לוֹ: מִמֶּנּוּ רְאָיָה? לֹא כָּךְ הָיָה מַעֲשֶׂה שֶׁהָלְכוּ זִקְנֵי בֵית שַׁמַּאי וְזִקְנֵי בֵית הִלֵּל לְבַקֵּר אֶת יוֹחָנָן בֶּן הַחוֹרַנִּי וּמָצְאוּ אוֹתוֹ שֶׁהָיָה יוֹשֵׁב רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ בַּסּוּכָּה וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ בְּתוֹךְ הַבַּיִת, וְלֹא אָמְרוּ לוֹ דָּבָר.
>
> **Translation:** *Bet Hillel* then presents a proof to *Bet Shammai*: the Rabbis **said to him:** Is there **proof from** such **an incident?** As a matter of fact, **was there not the following incident:** There **went the elders of Beit Shammai and the elders of Beit Hillel to visit Yochanan ben haChoranis, and they found him** sitting **with his head and the majority of his** body **inside the *sukka* and his table inside the house, and they said nothing to him.** This, *Bet Hillel* contends, demonstrates that the arrangement is perfectly acceptable.

### Shammai Rebuttal

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמְרוּ לוֹ: מִשָּׁם רְאָיָה? אַף הֵם אָמְרוּ לוֹ: אִם כֵּן הַיִּתָּה נוֹהַג לְךָ, לֹא קִיַּמְתָּ מִצְוַת סוּכָּה מִיָּמֶיךָ.
>
> **Translation:** *Bet Shammai* responded: **Is there proof from there?** In fact, **they said to him: If this is how you have been conducting yourself, you have never in your life fulfilled the** *mitzvah* **of *sukka*.** So the incident is not a validation of the practice; it was an occasion on which *Bet Shammai*'s elders actually rebuked him.

The scenario being described is worth picturing clearly. The "table" here need not refer to a large dining table; it is more like a serving tray on which the food rests. One sits in the *sukkah*, but the food tray is just inside the house — the *sukkah* is built right up against the house. One reaches over or steps briefly inside to take a piece of food, brings it back, eats it, then goes back for more. The concern *Bet Shammai* articulates is evident: if one allows the food to remain in the house, one will inevitably be drawn back into the house and will end up eating there, not in the *sukkah*. *Bet Hillel* is unconcerned, holding that a person can manage to eat within the *sukkah* even if the food tray is just across the threshold.

Now the *mishna* appears on its face to be debating a scenario — a behavioral question about how one conducts one's eating — not a question about the dimensions of the *sukkah*. Yet the *Gemara* will now show that this same debate is also understood to address the minimum size of the *sukkah*.

### Size Debate?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִמַּאי דְּבֵית שַׁמַּאי וּבֵית הִלֵּל בְּסוּכָּה קְטַנָּה פְּלִיגִי? דִּלְמָא בְּסוּכָּה גְּדוֹלָה פְּלִיגִי, וּכְגוֹן דְּיָתֵיב אַפּוּמָּא דִּמְטוּלְּתָא וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ בְּתוֹךְ הַבַּיִת.
>
> **Translation:** **From where** do you conclude **that Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel disagree with regard to** the minimum measure of **a small *sukka*? Perhaps** it is **with regard to a large *sukka* that they disagree, and** in a case **where one is sitting at the entrance of the *sukka* and his table is inside the house.**

What compels us to say that *Bet Shammai* and *Bet Hillel* are debating the minimum size of a *sukkah*? Perhaps they are debating the case of a perfectly normal large *sukkah*. Specifically the case where one sits right at the entrance of the *sukkah*, with the table just inside the house.

### Gezerah Concern

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּבֵית שַׁמַּאי סָבְרִי: גָּזְרִינַן שֶׁמָּא יִמָּשֵׁךְ אַחַר שׁוּלְחָנוֹ, וּבֵית הִלֵּל סָבְרִי: לָא גָּזְרִינַן. וְדַיְקָא נָמֵי, דְּקָתָנֵי: מִי שֶׁהָיָה רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ בַּסּוּכָּה וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ בְּתוֹךְ הַבַּיִת, בֵּית שַׁמַּאי פּוֹסְלִין וּבֵית הִלֵּל מַכְשִׁירִין. וְאִם אִיתָא, מַחְזֶקֶת וְאֵינָהּ מַחְזֶקֶת מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ.
>
> **Translation:** **Beit Shammai hold that we issue a decree** to prohibit one from sitting that way **lest he be drawn after his table** while eating, to the extent that his head and most of his body will be inside the house and not inside the *sukka*. **And Beit Hillel hold that we do not** issue that **decree.** And the language of the mishna **is also precise, as it teaches:** In the case of **one whose head and most of his** body **were in the *sukka* and his table was in the house, Beit Shammai deem it unfit and Beit Hillel deem it fit. And if it is so** that the dispute is with regard to the minimum measure of the *sukka*, the formulation of the mishna is missing the essential point. The distinction between a *sukka* that **holds and** a *sukka* that **does not hold** his head and most of his body is **what** the mishna **needed** to say. Since the mishna does not make that distinction, apparently the dispute is not with regard to the minimum measure of a *sukka*.

*Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak* then adds a textual argument: this interpretation can actually be inferred from the precise language of the *mishna* itself. The *mishna* describes the scenario as *mi she'hayah rosho v'rubo b'sukkah v'shulchano b'toch ha'bayit* — it depicts the act of eating with the table in the house, not the capacity of the *sukkah*. That they were debating the minimum size, the *mishna* should have framed the question as whether the *sukkah* can or cannot contain the table, describing the structure's dimensions rather than the scenario of the table being in the house. Since the *mishna* describes the scenario and not the dimensions, it evidently concerns the rabbinic *gezeirah* about being drawn after one's table, not the question of minimum size.

## Rabbi: 4x4 Amot

### Braitot Resolved

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּבְסוּכָּה קְטַנָּה לָא פְּלִיגִי? וְהָתַנְיָא: מַחְזֶקֶת רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ — כְּשֵׁרָה. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: עַד שֶׁיְּהֵא בָּהּ אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת עַל אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara questions this conclusion: **And in the case of a small *sukka* do** Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel **not disagree? But isn't it taught** in another *baraita*: A *sukka* that **holds his head, and most of his** body, **and his table is fit. Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **says:** It is unfit **until it measures** at least **four cubits by four cubits.**

The Gemara does not rest with this conclusion. But do *Bet Shammai* and *Bet Hillel* not also debate the minimum size? We have the following *baraita*: a *sukkah* that can contain one's head, body, and table is valid. *Rabbi Yehuda* says it must have four *amot* by four *amot*. This is manifestly a debate about dimensions and minimum size. And from a second *baraita*:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתַנְיָא אִידַּךְ, רַבִּי אוֹמֵר: כׇּל סוּכָּה שֶׁאֵין בָּהּ אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת עַל אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת — פְּסוּלָה. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: אֲפִילּוּ אֵינָהּ מַחְזֶקֶת אֶלָּא רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ — כְּשֵׁרָה. וְאִילּוּ שׁוּלְחָנוֹ לָא קָתָנֵי. קַשְׁיָין אַהֲדָדֵי. אֶלָּא לָאו, שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ: הָא בֵּית שַׁמַּאי, הָא בֵּית הִלֵּל.
>
> **Translation:** **And it is taught in** yet **another** *baraita* that **Rabbi** Yehuda HaNasi **says: Any *sukka* that does not have** an area of at least **four cubits by four cubits is unfit. And the Rabbis say: Even if it holds only his head and most of his** body, **it is fit. However,** the term: **His table, is not taught** in this *baraita*. If so, these two tannaitic sources **contradict each other,** as each attributes a different opinion to the Rabbis. **Rather,** must one **not conclude from it that this** *baraita*, in which: His table, is taught, **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Beit Shammai,** while **that** *baraita*, in which: His table, is not taught, **is** in accordance with the opinion of **Beit Hillel?** Apparently, they do dispute the minimum measure of a small *sukka*.

## Table in House…

### Language Evidence

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר מָר זוּטְרָא: מַתְנִיתִין נָמֵי דַּיְקָא, מִדְּקָתָנֵי: ״בֵּית שַׁמַּאי פּוֹסְלִין, וּבֵית הִלֵּל מַכְשִׁירִין״, וְאִם אִיתָא, ״בֵּית שַׁמַּאי אוֹמְרִים לֹא יָצָא, וּבֵית הִלֵּל אוֹמְרִים יָצָא״ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ.
>
> **Translation:** **Mar Zutra said: And** the language of **the mishna is also precise,** and it indicates that Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel indeed dispute the minimum measure of a small *sukka* **from** the fact **that it teaches: Beit Shammai deem it unfit and Beit Hillel deem it fit. And if it is so** that the dispute is with regard to the conduct of one sitting at the entrance of a large *sukka*, then **Beit Shammai say: He did not fulfill** his obligation, **and Beit Hillel say: He fulfilled** his obligation, is **what** the mishna **needed** to say. However, the terms fit and unfit indicate that the dispute is with regard to the halakhic status of the *sukka* itself, not the individual's behavior.

*Mar Zutra* now offers further textual support. One might have tried to infer from the *mishna* that the debate concerns a large *sukkah* (from the language describing the scenario of the table in the house), but a close reading of other language in the *mishna* points in the opposite direction. The *mishna* uses the terms *poslin* (invalidate) and *machshirin* (validate), which are normally applied to an *object* — the *sukkah* itself is declared valid or invalid. This is the language one uses when ruling on the status of a thing, not when ruling on whether a person fulfilled an obligation. If the entire debate were only about a rabbinic *gezeirah* regarding one's conduct in a large *sukkah*, the *mishna* should have said *Bet Shammai* declares that one has not fulfilled one's obligation (*lo yatza*) and *Bet Hillel* says one has fulfilled it (*yatza*). The use of *poslin* and *machshirin* — language that declares the object valid or invalid — implies that they are ruling on the status of the *sukkah* itself, i.e., its minimum dimensions. So the *mishna*'s language actually points both ways: some of its language (the description of the scenario) suggests a debate about conduct in a large *sukkah*, while other language (*poslin* / *machshirin*) suggests a debate about the *sukkah*'s status.

### Two Debates

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לְעוֹלָם בְּתַרְתֵּי פְּלִיגִי: פְּלִיגִי בְּסוּכָּה קְטַנָּה, וּפְלִיגִי בְּסוּכָּה גְּדוֹלָה. וְחַסּוֹרֵי מִיחַסְּרָא וְהָכִי קָתָנֵי: מִי שֶׁהָיָה רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ בַּסּוּכָּה וְשׁוּלְחָנוֹ בְּתוֹךְ הַבַּיִת, בֵּית שַׁמַּאי אוֹמְרִים: לֹא יָצָא, וּבֵית הִלֵּל אוֹמְרִים: יָצָא. וְשֶׁאֵינָהּ מַחְזֶקֶת אֶלָּא כְּדֵי רֹאשׁוֹ וְרוּבּוֹ בִּלְבַד, בֵּית שַׁמַּאי פּוֹסְלִין, וּבֵית הִלֵּל מַכְשִׁירִין.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **Actually, they disagree with regard to two** issues; **they disagree with regard to** the minimum measure of **a small *sukka*, and they disagree with regard to** where one may sit in **a large *sukka*.** And the mishna **is incomplete and this** is what **it is teaching: One whose head and most of his** body **were in the *sukka* and his table was in the house, Beit Shammai say** that **he did not fulfill** his obligation **and Beit Hillel say** that **he fulfilled** his obligation. **And** with regard to a *sukka* **that holds only his head and most of his** body, **Beit Shammai deem it unfit and Beit Hillel deem it fit.** In this way, the dispute in the mishna is understood as relating to the measure of a small *sukka* and the manner in which one fulfills his obligation in a large *sukka*.

Both debates are present simultaneously. They debate both the minimum size and the scenario of the large *sukkah* with the table in the house. The text must be understood as having omitted some words. The complete reading would be: one whose head and body was in the *sukkah* and whose table was in the house — *Bet Shammai* says he did not fulfill his obligation, *Bet Hillel* says he did. That is the first debate, concerning a large *sukkah* with the table in the house. Then the second case: a *sukkah* that can contain only one's head and body — *Bet Shammai* invalidate it, *Bet Hillel* validate it. That is the second, separate debate about minimum size.

It is worth acknowledging that when the *Gemara* employs the formula *hisurei mechsara* — that words are missing from the text — one is sometimes left with the feeling that the reading is forced. Some *Acharonim*, including the *Gra* explicitly, maintain that when the *Gemara* says *hisurei mechsara* it is not genuinely claiming there was a different manuscript tradition (*girsa*), but rather consciously reworking a text that does not fit the position being advanced. Here, however, the reading is reasonably persuasive, because there is genuine independent evidence that both debates exist: the *braitot* clearly use dimensional language, and the *mishna*'s language, as shown, points in both directions. The claim that two debates became conflated in a single text is not implausible.

### Linked Debates

Are these two debates logically connected? They appear to be. The question of minimum size is ultimately a question about what constitutes meaningful habitation of a *sukkah*: what does it mean to *dwell* in a *sukkah*? As will emerge later in the *masechta*, dwelling means eating and sleeping. A *sukkah* that can contain one's head and the majority of one's body provides sufficient shelter for sleeping — one's head and most of one's body will be covered and protected. But can such a space also serve as a setting for eating? Eating requires a table. Is a space a genuine dwelling for eating purposes if it cannot accommodate the table?

This connects directly to the *gezeirah* debate. *Bet Shammai*, who does not trust a person to remain rooted in the *sukkah* when the table is in the house, evidently holds that a space without room for the table cannot be considered an eating space at all — there is no permanence of presence there, since one is constantly being pulled outside to retrieve food. If the space cannot contain the table, it is not the kind of space in which a person can genuinely inhabit and eat. *Bet Hillel*, who is unconcerned that the person will be drawn after the table, holds that even without the table present one can be genuinely present and rooted in the *sukkah* — and thus a space of just *rosho v'rubo* is a sufficient minimum for habitation.

This logical linkage raises a further practical question: since we rule — at least in the matter of minimum size — like *Bet Shammai*, do we also rule like *Bet Shammai* regarding the large *sukkah* with the table in the house? This is in fact a *machloket Rishonim*. Several *Rishonim* rule like *Bet Shammai* for minimum size but hold that if the *sukkah* is large and the table is in the house, one still fulfills one's obligation. Others argue that the two positions are logically bound together and must be ruled upon consistently.

*Tosafot* address this question, and also take up the broader issue of when we rule like *Bet Shammai* — itself a rare occurrence. *Tosafot* quote *Rav Amram* among the *Geonim*, who lists six places where the *halacha* follows *Bet Shammai*, and this is one of them: *Rav Amram* ruled in six places according to *Bet Shammai*, one of which is that a *sukkah* must be able to contain one's head, body, and table.

One might also ask whether the ruling of *machshirin* represents a *lechatchila* or *bedieved* position. The term *machshirin* is generally understood as *lechatchila* — it does not merely validate after the fact, but declares the thing kosher categorically. By contrast, *yatza* sometimes carries a *bedieved* implication and sometimes does not, depending on context.

There is a further remarkable dimension to this *sugya* that emerges from *Bet Shammai*'s rebuke of *Yochanan ben haChoranis*: you have never in your life fulfilled the *mitzvah* of *sukkah*. What is the force of this statement? If *Yochanan* was sitting in a large *sukkah* with only his table in the house, at the very least he should have been fulfilling his biblical obligation, even if he was violating a rabbinic *gezeirah*. The implication of *Bet Shammai*'s words is that by violating the rabbinic requirement, he failed to fulfill even the biblical obligation. This is a striking and difficult principle: can the Sages, through a rabbinic edict, deprive a person of credit for a biblical *mitzvah* that he objectively performed?

*Tosafot* confront this directly. Reading the relevant passage: in a case where *Bet Hillel* is the more stringent position rabbinically and *Bet Shammai* maintains the biblical baseline without the rabbinic stringency, if one acts in accordance with *Bet Shammai*'s position and neglects *Bet Hillel*'s rabbinic addition, one has fulfilled one's obligation even on the biblical level. But the reverse case, by contrast — where one has a large *sukkah* and the table is in the house and *Bet Shammai* imposed a *gezeirah* out of concern that one will be drawn after the table, *Bet Shammai*'s declaration is that one has not fulfilled the *mitzvah* even on a biblical level.

The precise mechanics of how a rabbinic edict in the domain of *mitzvot asei* can undermine the fulfillment of a biblical obligation is genuinely difficult. Various possibilities are raised — *mitzvah haba'ah b'aveirah*, or the principle of *yesh koach beyad chachamim* — but no definitive explanation is offered. What is clear is that the principle operates differently in the realm of positive commandments than in the more familiar territory of prohibitions. When the Sages legislate in the area of *lo ta'asei*, the consequence of violation is a rabbinic transgression; the question of biblical fulfillment simply does not arise. But when they specify how a *mitzvat asei* must be performed, and someone fails to follow their specification, the question of whether the biblical *mitzvah* was nonetheless performed becomes live and genuinely complex. This does not mean every rabbinic requirement attached to *sukkah* carries this consequence — there are many rabbinic rules regarding valid *sechach*, for instance — but the principle that it can sometimes occur is established here.

## Rabbi: 4x4 Amot…

### Size Braitot

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאן תְּנָא לְהָא דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן: בַּיִת שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת עַל אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת — פָּטוּר מִן הַמְּזוּזָה, וּמִן הַמַּעֲקֶה, וְאֵינוֹ מִטַּמֵּא בִּנְגָעִים, וְאֵינוֹ נֶחְלָט בְּבָתֵּי עָרֵי חוֹמָה,
>
> **Translation:** § Apropos the above discussion, the Gemara asks: **Who** is the *tanna* who **taught that which the Sages taught:** The halakhic status of **a house in which there is not** an area of **four cubits by four cubits** is not that of a house? Therefore, *halakhot* in the Torah or the mishna that are relevant to a house do not apply to a house that size. Consequently, it is **exempt from** the mitzva of placing a ***mezuza*** on its doorpost; **and** it is exempt **from the** obligation of establishing a **parapet** around its roof; **and it does not become ritually impure with leprosy** of the house. **And** its sale is **not rendered final** in the same manner as the sale of **houses within walled cities.**

Who is the *tanna* of the following *baraita*? The *baraita* teaches: a house that does not contain four *amot* by four *amot* — is, as the *baraita* will proceed to list, exempt from a range of *halachos* that require a "house." The thrust of the *baraita* is that four *amot* by four *amot* is the universal minimum dimension for something to be halachically classified as a house, and the *Gemara* raises the question of whether this *baraita* represents the position of *Rebbe* alone or can be attributed to the Sages as well.

## 4x4: House

### Mezuzah/Tzaraat

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵין חוֹזְרִין עָלָיו מֵעוֹרְכֵי הַמִּלְחָמָה, וְאֵין מְעָרְבִין בּוֹ, וְאֵין מִשְׁתַּתְּפִין בּוֹ, וְאֵין מַנִּיחִין בּוֹ עֵירוּב,
>
> **Translation:** **And one does not return from** the ranks **of** soldiers **waging war for** a house that size, as would one who built a house with an area greater than four by four cubits (see Deuteronomy 20:5). **And one** need **not join** the houses in the courtyards **for** a house with that area. If there is more than one house in a courtyard, it is prohibited by rabbinic law to carry in that courtyard unless the residents of each of the houses contribute food that is placed in one of the houses, thereby rendering them joint-owners of the courtyard. The resident of a house with an area of less than four by four cubits need not participate in this joining of courtyards. **And one** need **not merge** the courtyards that open into an alleyway **for** a courtyard in which the area of its only house is less than four by four cubits. In this case, too, the resident of that courtyard need not participate in the merging of alleyways. **And one does not place the** food collected for the aforementioned **joining** [***eiruv***] of courtyards **in** this house but rather in a house with an area of at least four by four cubits.

The *baraita* lists the following consequences of a structure being less than four *amot* by four *amot*: it is exempt from the obligation of a *mezuzah*, for the verse states on the doorposts of your *house* — and this does not constitute a house. It does not require a railing around the roof, for the verse states when you build a new *house* — and this is not a house. If it develops spots it does not become ritually impure with the impurity of plague-spots on houses, for the relevant verse speaks of an appearance of a plague-spot in a *house*. A sale of such a structure in a walled city does not become irrevocable after a year. The law of houses in walled cities, whereby the buyer acquires the property permanently once the year of redemption has passed, applies only to a *house*. Something smaller than four by four is treated like an open field or courtyard, and can always be redeemed, reverting at the Jubilee. One who built such a structure does not qualify for an exemption from military service. The draft exemption is granted to one who built a new *house* — and this is not a house.

### Sale & Draft

It does not have to participate in an *eruv* of a courtyard, and is not required to join the *shituf mevo'ot* of an alleyway. Why? Because it is not fit for dwelling. The principle here differs slightly from the foregoing: this is not primarily about the conceptual definition of a "house," but about the practical requirement that those who *dwell* in the courtyard join together. Since one does not genuinely dwell in so small a structure, one need not participate.

### Eruv Exemption

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵין מַנִּיחִין בּוֹ עֵירוּב, אֲבָל שִׁיתּוּף מַנִּיחִין בּוֹ.
>
> **Translation:** **And one does not place the** food collected for the **joining** of courtyards **in** this house; **however, the** food of the **merging** of alleyways, **one places in it.**

### Eruv Storage

The collected bread for the *eruv* of the courtyard may not be stored in it, because the rule is that the *eruv* must be placed in one of the houses of the courtyard, and this does not qualify. However, the bread for the *shituf mevo'ot* (the joining of the alleyways) may be stored in it. Why? Because it is no worse than the courtyard itself.

The basic configuration to keep in mind: houses open onto a *chatzer* (courtyard), and courtyards open onto a *mavoi* (alleyway). When the houses of a courtyard join together in an *eruv chatzeirot*, the bread must be placed in one of the houses. When the courtyards of an alleyway join together in a *shituf mevo'ot*, the bread is placed in one of the courtyards. The *Gemara* then notes that the *mishna* can be misread as saying the *eruv chatzeirot* goes in the courtyard itself, but the correct reading, as a *baraita* clarifies, is: the *eruv chatzeirot* goes in a house within the courtyard; and the *shituf mevo'ot* goes in a courtyard within the alleyway. Since the *shituf mevo'ot* can be placed in the yard itself, a small structure that is no worse than a yard is equally acceptable for this purpose, even though it is too small to qualify as a house.

## Sukkah vs House

### Inference

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲפִילּוּ תֵּימָא רַבָּנַן, עַד כָּאן לָא קָאָמְרִי רַבָּנַן הָתָם אֶלָּא לְעִנְיַן סוּכָּה — דְּדִירַת עֲרַאי הִיא, אֲבָל לְגַבַּי בַּיִת — דְּדִירַת קֶבַע הוּא, אֲפִילּוּ רַבָּנַן מוֹדוּ דְּאִי אִית בֵּיהּ אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת עַל אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת — דָּיְירִי בֵּיהּ אִינָשֵׁי, וְאִי לָא — לָא דָּיְירִי בֵּיהּ אִינָשֵׁי.
>
> **Translation:** **Even** if **you say** that the *tanna* of the *baraita* is **the Rabbis, the Rabbis say** that a structure with an area smaller than four by four cubits is fit **only there, with regard to a *sukka*, which is a temporary residence,** because in a temporary residence one is willing to confine himself to a small area. **However, with regard to** *halakhot* relating to **a house, which is a permanent residence, even the Rabbis concede that if it has** an area of **four cubits by four cubits, people reside in it,** as it is a functional house, **and if not, people do not reside in it,** and its legal status is not that of a house at all.

The *Gemara* has now established that four by four *amot* is the universal halachic minimum for a "house." The question that naturally arises is: does this imply that *Rebbe*'s ruling — that a *sukkah* must be four by four *amot* — is the only opinion that could endorse this *baraita*, meaning the *baraita* follows *Rebbe* and not the Sages?

Even the Sages can agree to this *baraita*. The Sages said that a *sukkah* can be as small as *rosho v'rubo veshulchano* only because of a specific characteristic of *sukkah*: it is a temporary dwelling. The leniency regarding a *sukkah*'s minimum size is grounded in its nature as something other than a permanent residence. But for a permanent dwelling, everyone agrees that the minimum size is four by four *amot*: four by four is the threshold at which people will genuinely dwell in an ongoing, regular fashion, and below that threshold, the space is not one in which people would regularly live.

### Arai vs Keva

The introduction of this principle generates an important conceptual point: four by four *amot* is the halachic minimum for a permanent dwelling, and this is apparently uncontested. The smaller size — *rosho v'rubo veshulchano* — is sanctioned specifically because a *sukkah* is a *dirat arai*, a temporary dwelling, and therefore can be smaller. This raises a significant question regarding *Rebbe*'s position: if *Rebbe* requires a *sukkah* to be four by four *amot*, it would appear that *Rebbe* treats a *sukkah* not as a temporary dwelling but as a permanent one. This may indeed be the heart of the dispute: perhaps both *Rebbe* and the Sages agree that four by four characterizes a permanent dwelling and that *rosho v'rubo* characterizes a temporary one, and their dispute is precisely whether a *sukkah* must function as a permanent dwelling or whether its temporary character suffices.

### Temporary Allows

This ties back to the opening theme of the *masechta*. The discussion began with the principle that a *sukkah* must not be so tall as to constitute a permanent structure. Now the inverse is being explored: perhaps a *sukkah* must actually achieve a degree of permanence in order to fulfill the obligation of *basukkot teshvu* — you shall dwell in *sukkot* — since genuine dwelling requires a habitable, somewhat permanent space. The tension between *dirat arai* and *dirat keva* as competing definitions of what a *sukkah* must be is thus a thread running through multiple *sugyot* in this *masechta*.

## 4x4: House…

### City Joining

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֵין עוֹשִׂין אוֹתוֹ עִיבּוּר בֵּין שְׁתֵּי עֲיָירוֹת, וְאֵין הָאַחִין וְהַשּׁוּתָּפִין חוֹלְקִין בּוֹ.
>
> **Translation:** **And one does not render it an extension** of the city limits when it is located **between two cities.** Two cities between which there is a distance of more than 141⅓ cubits cannot be joined and considered as a single city for the purpose of measuring the Shabbat limit for one city from the edge of the second city. However if there is a house equidistant between the two cities, i.e., a bit more than seventy cubits from each town, the house joins the two cities together for the purpose of measuring the Shabbat limit. A house in which there is an area of less than four by four cubits cannot serve this function; **and brothers and partners do not divide it,** as it is too small to be divided.

The *baraita* continues with additional laws requiring a four by four structure: it cannot serve to join two cities halachically.

The background: each city has a *techum* — a boundary of two thousand *amot* — extending from its edge in all directions. The question of where a city's edge is drawn follows the principle that any two structures within 70⅔ *amot* of each other are considered part of the same settlement. If two cities are each within 70⅔ *amot* of a midpoint structure, they are treated as one city, and the two-thousand *amot* is measured from the outermost edges of the combined settlement. This is called making the structure a *ibur* — a pregnancy — between the two cities, because it causes them to "carry" one another as a single unit. However, the structure serving this connecting function must qualify as a house; if it is too small to be a house, it cannot extend the city limits in this way.

### Burginin vs House

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּתְנַן: עִיבּוּר בֵּין שְׁתֵּי עֲיָירוֹת, בּוּרְגָּנִין חֲזוּ לְמִילְּתַיְיהוּ, וְהַאי לָא חֲזֵי לְמִילְּתֵיהּ.
>
> **Translation:** The answer is: **we learned** that a **burganin** [watchman's hut] **is suited for** what it is made for. A watchman's hut is a legitimate structure of its type; it was built to serve the function of temporary sheltering in a field. **But this** house with an area less than four by four cubits **is not suited for its** intended purpose. The analogy is to the difference between a condemned house and a tent. Both may provide similar shelter, but a condemned house carries no recognition or legitimacy as a structure — it is built as a house but fails as one — whereas a tent, built for its purpose, is fully legitimate of its kind. A *burganin* is like the tent: it is what it purports to be. A small house-like structure is like the condemned house: it is constructed as a house but is not fit for that function, and therefore cannot serve any halachic role that requires a genuine house or even a genuine temporary shelter. *Rashi* adds that a *burganin* is built for a single night's stay, whereas a house-like structure implicitly represents an attempt at multiple-night, more regular dwelling — and if it is too small for that, it is entirely unfit for its purpose.

The *Gemara* raises an immediate question: surely a *burganin* — a lean-to or watchman's hut in a field — can serve to join two cities even though it is not a four by four house? If a lean-to suffices, why is a small structure disqualified?

## Sukkah vs House…

### Rebbe Permanent?

With the principle of *dirat keva* versus *dirat arai* now articulated, the question returns: does *Rebbe*'s requirement of four by four *amot* for a *sukkah* reflect a position that a *sukkah* must constitute a permanent dwelling? If so, *Rebbe* and the Sages may be debating not just a number but the fundamental character of the *sukkah* itself — whether the obligation of *teshvu* demands that it be a *dirat keva*, or whether it is fulfilled in a space that is genuinely *arai*. This remains an open and important question as the *masechta* continues.

## 4x4: House……

### Brother Division

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אֵימָא: אֵין בּוֹ דִּין חֲלוּקָּה כְּחָצֵר. דְּאָמַר רַב הוּנָא: חָצֵר לְפִי פְּתָחֶיהָ מִתְחַלֶּקֶת. וְרַב חִסְדָּא אָמַר: נוֹתֵן לְכׇל פֶּתַח וָפֶתַח אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת, וְהַשְּׁאָר חוֹלְקִין אוֹתוֹ בְּשָׁוֶה.
>
> **Translation:** **Rather,** emend the *baraita* and **say** that **the *halakha* of division like** that of **a courtyard** does **not** apply **to it. As Rav Huna said: A courtyard is divided according to** the number of **its entrances.** When the residents of the houses in a courtyard divide the courtyard between them, the division is not based on the number of houses in the courtyard, nor is it based on the size of the houses. Instead, it is divided based on the number of entrances that open into the courtyard. **Rav Ḥisda said: One gives** the homeowner **for each and every entrance four cubits, and the rest** of the courtyard **is divided equally** among the residents of the courtyard.

The *baraita* lists one further case: brothers or partners do not divide a house that is smaller than four by four *amot*.

The *Gemara* objects: the *baraita* implies that once the structure does have four by four *amot*, it can be divided. But how can a four by four *amah* house be divided between two people? Each half would then be only two by four *amot*, and the *mishna* elsewhere rules: a courtyard is not divided unless each person receives four by four *amot*. The same logic would apply to a house: dividing a four by four *amah* house would leave each party with less than four by four, which is insufficient. The *Gemara* therefore reinterprets: rather, it does not get divided the way a *chatzer* gets divided.

## Courtyard Division

### Rav Huna

The *Gemara* now explains how a courtyard is divided. A courtyard is divided according to its doorways.

### Rav Chisda

Every doorway receives four *amot*, and the remainder is divided equally.

To illustrate the dispute: suppose two brothers, *Reuven* and *Shimon*, inherit a courtyard from their father. *Reuven*'s portion includes one large mansion with a single door onto the courtyard; *Shimon*'s includes three small houses each with one door. According to *Rav Huna*, since the courtyard exists to serve the houses — providing access for unloading and movement — it should be divided in proportion to the number of doors: *Shimon* has three doors and *Reuven* one, so *Shimon* receives three-quarters of the courtyard and *Reuven* one-quarter. According to *Rav Chisda*, one agrees that each doorway requires some portion of the courtyard in front of it — specifically, four *amot* — but the rest of the courtyard is not allocated by the number of doors; it is divided equally. *Shimon* gets four *amot* per door (three portions) and *Reuven* four *amot* for his door (one portion), but the remaining courtyard space beyond these allocations is split evenly between them regardless of the disparity in doorways.

### Counting Houses

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּהָנֵי מִילֵּי בַּיִת, דִּלְמֶהֱוֵי קָאֵי — יָהֲבִינַן לֵיהּ חָצֵר. הַאי, דִּלְמִיסְתַּר קָאֵי — לָא יָהֲבִינַן לֵיהּ חָצֵר.
>
> **Translation:** The principle that entrances are factored into the division of a courtyard **applies only** with regard to **a house that stands to endure,** as the owner needs use of the yard to ease access to his house, so **we provide him** with four cubits according to Rav Ḥisda, or part of the **courtyard** according to Rav Huna. However, in the case of **this** small house, **which stands to be leveled,** its owner has no need for the adjacent courtyard, so **we do not provide him** with any part of the **courtyard,** as if it were not even there.

The relevance to the preceding discussion now becomes clear: under either approach, a doorway belonging to a house that is less than four by four *amot* does not count. This applies to a doorway of a house that is going to remain standing as such; in that case, we allocate it a portion of the yard. But a condemned house, one that is effectively ready to be torn down, is not allocated any portion of the yard, because it has no standing as a structure deserving of consideration. A house that is too small to qualify as a house falls into this category: it is constructed in the form of a house but lacks the minimum dimensions, and therefore its doorway does not factor into either *Rav Huna*'s proportional division or *Rav Chisda*'s allocation of four *amot* per doorway.
