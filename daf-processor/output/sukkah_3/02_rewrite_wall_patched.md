# Sukkah 3 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Review: Sechach & Height

### Day One Summary

Today's *daf* is *daf* 3 of *Sukkah*. By way of review, the previous session introduced several major themes centered on the discussion of the height limit of twenty *amot*.

### Sechach Definition

The first and most fundamental point established is that the concept of *sukkah* is primarily defined by *sechach*. This was made clear from the way multiple *amoraim* explain the problem of excessive height — the concern is with the *sechach* being too high — and from the focus on *pesukim* that mention the word *sukkah*, which is understood to refer essentially to the *sechach*. The identity of a *sukkah* is thus very much tied up in its roof covering.

### Three Themes

Three major themes emerged from that discussion. The first is the question of permanence versus temporary dwelling: one reason given for the height restriction is that a *sukkah* must constitute a *dirat arai*, a temporary dwelling, and a structure too tall to be recognized as such would be invalid. The second theme is the visibility of the *sechach*: perhaps the requirement is that one be able to perceive the *sechach* overhead, and if it is too high, it will not register meaningfully in one's awareness — an idea loosely connected to the notion of *lema'an yedu doroteichem*, so that generations will know. The third theme is the *sechach*'s practical capacity to provide shade: if the *sechach* is too high, it will not effectively shade the space below.

It is worth noting an important qualification: the *Gemara* states that the question of temporary dwelling is not determined by building material — one may construct a *sukkah* from brick — so the permanence concern pertains to other features of the structure.

### Tosafot: Sechach

*Tosafot* raise a pointed question that goes beyond mere dimensions: can the *sechach* itself be so permanent that it comes to be defined as one's house? Their conclusion is that it can. One criterion *Tosafot* offer for *sechach* being considered permanent — and therefore no longer qualifying as a temporary roof covering — is that it prevents rain from penetrating entirely. There is also a tradition, apparently rooted in the *Yerushalmi*, regarding the ability to see the stars through the *sechach*, though *Tosafot*'s primary focus is on the rain criterion. Additional examples were raised: *sechach* nailed down with planks, nails, and plaster. Whatever the precise standard, the underlying principle is clear — the *sechach* cannot take on the character of a permanent roof.

## Queen Hilni Case

### Hilni Proof

The *Gemara* now proceeds to test which of the three explanations for the twenty *amot* height limit is correct by examining the famous case of Queen *Hilni*, who sat in a *sukkah* taller than twenty *amot*. This case was invoked by *Rabbi Yehuda* as a proof that a *sukkah* exceeding twenty *amot* is valid. The Sages responded that her case proves nothing, since she was a woman and exempt from the obligation. *Rabbi Yehuda* replied that her sons were with her, and as the *Gemara* elaborated, her sons were at minimum rabbinically obligated from the standpoint of *chinuch*. The *Gemara* now attempts to determine which theoretical approach to the height limit can actually accommodate this case as a genuine point of debate.

### Walls Not Reaching

The *Gemara* opens: *bishlama leman de'amar beshein defanot megi'ot lasechach* — this works well according to the approach that the entire problem of excessive height arises only when the walls do not reach the *sechach*. According to this view, the concern is whether one will perceive the *sechach*: if the walls reach the *sechach*, one's gaze is naturally drawn upward to where the walls meet the roof, and height poses no problem. The debate applies only when the walls fall short of the *sechach*. This explanation fits the *Hilni* case perfectly, since it is entirely plausible that her *sukkah* — a grand royal structure — was built with walls that did not extend to the *sechach*: *darkah shel malka leishev besukkah she'ein defanot megi'ot lasechach* — it is quite natural for a queen's *sukkah* to have walls not reaching the *sechach*, *mishum avira*, in order to allow for air circulation.

### Small Sukkah Case

The *Gemara* then poses a difficulty against the alternative approach. *Ella leman de'amar besukkah ketana machloket* — but according to the view that the problem is the *sechach*'s inability to provide shade, and that this is a problem only for a small *sukkah* (since a small *sechach* covering a limited area will not provide shade if too high, whereas a larger *sechach* will shade effectively regardless of height), the debate applies only when the *sukkah* is very small, perhaps four by four *amot*. Given that, how could *Hilni*'s *sukkah* have been the subject of this debate? *Vechi darkah shel malka leishev besukkah ketana?* — Would a queen sit in such a tiny *sukkah*? Surely her *sukkah* must have been large, in which case there would be no issue of height at all according to this opinion, and her case would prove nothing.

### Compartments

*Amar Rabba bar Rav Adda*: *lo nitzrecha ela l'sukkah asuyah kitoniyot kitoniyot* — the solution is that the *sukkah* was constructed with small private compartments. The outer structure may indeed have been enormous, like a large building with rooms, but halachically the relevant *sukkah* is not judged by the scale of the outer structure. What matters is the space in which one sits, enclosed by its own four walls. If one sits within an inner chamber, the *sukkah* being evaluated is that chamber — and if the *sechach* directly above that chamber covers only a small area, the question of whether it provides shade at twenty *amot* of height is entirely germane. One might ask whether the walls of such a chamber would also need to reach the *sechach*. The answer is no: that requirement is the concern of the first opinion, which holds that the problem is the visibility of the *sechach*. Under the present opinion — that the problem is the *sechach*'s ability to provide shade — what matters is simply how large an area the *sechach* covers, not whether the walls reach it.

The *Gemara* then challenges this answer as well: *V'chi darkah shel malka leishev besukkah asuyah kitoniyot kitoniyot?* — Would a queen sit in a *sukkah* consisting entirely of tiny little chambers, with no general sitting room at all?

### Outer & Side

*Amar Rav Ashi*: *lo nitzrecha ela l'kitoniyot shebah* — it need only apply to the side chambers within the *sukkah*. The structure in fact contained a large and impressive main hall where guests could be received, but also featured small private side chambers. The queen presumably would greet guests in the large hall, but would retire to a private chamber to eat.

### Rashi & Yehuda

*Rashi* explains the positions of the disputants as follows: *rabbanan savri b'nah b'sukkah ma'alya hava yatvi* — the Sages reasoned that the sons were eating in the large main hall. Since there is no debate about whether a large *sukkah* poses a problem of height under this opinion (height only matters for a small *sukkah*), the sons' presence in a properly sized *sukkah* proves nothing. *Rabbi Yehuda savar b'nah gabah hava yatvi* — but *Rabbi Yehuda* held that the sons were with her in one of the small private chambers. *Ve'afilu hachi lo amru leih davar* — and yet no one said anything to protest, which *Rabbi Yehuda* cites as proof that even in a small *sukkah*, the height above twenty *amot* is not disqualifying.

This raises an obvious difficulty: if all the sons were crowded into a tiny chamber, how could they all fit? Even a chamber of four *amot* by four *amot* would be very cramped, and if the minimum dimension is only seven *tefachim* by seven *tefachim*, it is inconceivable that seven sons could fit inside.

### Tosafot Dimensions

*Tosafot* resolve this difficulty by articulating an important principle: in order for a structure to constitute a *sukkah* of minimum size, it is not sufficient merely that it possess the minimum area — it must satisfy the minimum measurement in both length and width independently. A space that is three and a half *amot* wide but twenty *amot* long is not a minimum-sized *sukkah*, because in one dimension it falls short of the minimum. Accordingly, *Tosafot* suggest that the chamber could have been quite narrow in one direction — say, three and a half *amot* — while extending to twenty *amot* in the other direction, giving ample room for all seven sons to recline side by side, while still counting as a *sukkah* that is too small in the relevant dimension. The same logic would apply regarding the shade concern: for the *sechach* to provide shade, it must achieve the minimum span in both directions; it is not enough for the structure to be very long if it is insufficiently wide.

## Minimum Size

### Shmuel: Head/Body

Having examined the various approaches to the twenty *amot* height limit through the lens of the *Hilni* case, the *Gemara* now pivots entirely. The question of height is set aside for the moment, and attention turns to a new issue that arose incidentally — what is the minimum size of a *sukkah*? This applies to an ordinary *sukkah* of normal height, whether six or eight feet tall. *Amar Shmuel bar Yitzchak*: *halacha tsricha kidei machzeket rosho verubo veshulchano* — the *halacha* is that a *sukkah* must be able to contain one's head, the majority of one's body, and one's table. This is significantly smaller than four *amot* by four *amot*, which is the position of *Rebbe* that will be introduced shortly. The requirement of "the majority of one's body" reflects the posture of reclining: since one eats reclining, the relevant measurement runs from the waist to the top of the head, approximately. The table — a small serving tray — measures roughly seven *tefachim* by seven *tefachim*, approximately two feet square.

### Rabbi Abba Query

*Amar lei Rabbi Abba*: *keman?* — Who is this position going like? *Kebet Shammai?* Are you following *Bet Shammai*? *Amar lei, ela keman?* — He replied: yes, and who else would I be following? This brief exchange raises a question: what does *Bet Shammai* have to do with the minimum size of a *sukkah*?

### Mishnah 28a

To understand this, the *Gemara* directs attention to the *mishna* on 28a, which will serve as the reference text for much of what follows on this *daf*.

## Table in House

### Mishna: Table Out

The *mishna* on 28a reads: *mi shehaya rosho verubo besukkah veshulchano betoch habayit* — one whose head and the majority of his body was inside the *sukkah* but whose table was inside the house.

### Shammai vs Hillel

*Beit Shammai poslin u'Beit Hillel machshirin* — *Bet Shammai* invalidate and *Bet Hillel* validate.

### Yochanan Story

*Bet Hillel* then presents a proof to *Bet Shammai*: *amru lehei Bet Hillel leBet Shammai* — *Bet Hillel* said to *Bet Shammai*: *lo kach haya maaseh shehalchu ziknei Bet Shammai veziknei Bet Hillel levaker et Yochanan ben haChoranis* — was there not the following incident, that the elders of *Bet Shammai* and the elders of *Bet Hillel* went to visit *Yochanan ben haChoranis*? *Umatsuhu shehaya yoshev rosho verubo besukkah veshulchano betoch habayit* — and they found him sitting with his head and the majority of his body inside the *sukkah* and his table inside the house. *Velo amru lo davar* — and they said nothing to him. This, *Bet Hillel* contends, demonstrates that the arrangement is perfectly acceptable.

### Shammai Rebuttal

*Bet Shammai* responded: *misham raya?* — You want to bring a proof from that incident? *Af hem amru lo* — that was not the whole story. *Bet Shammai* says that their elders did in fact say something to him: *im ken hayita noheg lo kiyamta mitzvat sukkah miyamecha* — if this is how you have been conducting yourself, you have never in your life fulfilled the *mitzvah* of *sukkah*. So the incident is not a validation of the practice; it was an occasion on which *Bet Shammai*'s elders actually rebuked him.

The scenario being described is worth picturing clearly. The "table" here need not refer to a large dining table; it is more like a serving tray on which the food rests. One sits in the *sukkah*, but the food tray is just inside the house — the *sukkah* is built right up against the house. One reaches over or steps briefly inside to take a piece of food, brings it back, eats it, then goes back for more. The concern *Bet Shammai* articulates is evident: if one allows the food to remain in the house, one will inevitably be drawn back into the house and will end up eating there, not in the *sukkah*. *Bet Hillel* is unconcerned, holding that a person can manage to eat within the *sukkah* even if the food tray is just across the threshold.

Now the *mishna* appears on its face to be debating a scenario — a behavioral question about how one conducts one's eating — not a question about the dimensions of the *sukkah*. Yet the *Gemara* will now show that this same debate is also understood to address the minimum size of the *sukkah*.

### Size Debate?

*Matkif la Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak*: *mimmay d'Beit Shammai u'Beit Hillel b'sukkah ketanna pligi?* — What compels us to say that *Bet Shammai* and *Bet Hillel* are debating the minimum size of a *sukkah*? *Dilma b'sukkah gedolah pligi* — perhaps they are debating the case of a perfectly normal large *sukkah*. *U'k'gon d'yatbi a'pumma d'metulta* — specifically the case where one sits right at the entrance of the *sukkah* (note: *metulta* is the Aramaic word for *sukkah*), *veshulchano b'toch ha'bayit* — with the table just inside the house.

### Gezerah Concern

*D'Beit Shammai savri gazrinan shema yimashekh achar shulchano* — *Bet Shammai* holds that this is a *gezeirah*: we are concerned that if we permit this, the person will be drawn after his table and will end up eating inside the house. *U'Beit Hillel savri lo gazrinan* — *Bet Hillel* holds that we do not make this rabbinic edict.

*Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak* then adds a textual argument: *v'dayka nami* — this interpretation can actually be inferred from the precise language of the *mishna* itself. The *mishna* describes the scenario as *mi she'hayah rosho v'rubo b'sukkah v'shulchano b'toch ha'bayit* — it depicts the act of eating with the table in the house, not the capacity of the *sukkah*. *V'i salka da'atach* that they were debating the minimum size, *makhziket v'einah makhziket mibba'ei lei* — the *mishna* should have framed the question as whether the *sukkah* can or cannot contain the table, describing the structure's dimensions rather than the scenario of the table being in the house. Since the *mishna* describes the scenario and not the dimensions, it evidently concerns the rabbinic *gezeirah* about being drawn after one's table, not the question of minimum size.

## Rabbi: 4x4 Amot

### Braitot Resolved

The *Gemara* observes a contradiction between two *braitot*. In both, *Rabbi* holds that a *sukkah* must measure at least four by four *amot*, but the opposing opinions differ: one *baraita* sets the minimum as whatever can contain one's head, body, and table; the other requires only head and body. *Kashyan adadi* — the *braitot* contradict one another. The resolution: *ela lav shema mina ha Beit Shammai ha Beit Hillel* — the *baraita* requiring the table reflects *Beit Shammai*, consistent with their concern that one's table not remain in the house, while the *baraita* requiring only head and body reflects *Beit Hillel*, who are not concerned with the table's location. This maps directly onto the *Mishnah*'s debate. *Mar Zutra* then adds that a careful reading of the *Mishnah*'s own language — *matnitin nami dika* — independently supports this conclusion, as the precise wording *Beit Shammai* employ points to the same inference.

## Bet Shammai/Hillel: Table

### Language Evidence

*Amar mar zutra* — *Mar Zutra* now offers further textual support. One might have tried to infer from the *mishna* that the debate concerns a large *sukkah* (from the language describing the scenario of the table in the house), but a close reading of other language in the *mishna* points in the opposite direction. *Midikatani Beit Shammai poslin u-Beit Hillel machshirin* — the *mishna* uses the terms *poslin* (invalidate) and *machshirin* (validate), which are normally applied to an *object* — the *sukkah* itself is declared valid or invalid. This is the language one uses when ruling on the status of a thing, not when ruling on whether a person fulfilled an obligation. *V'im itai* — if the entire debate were only about a rabbinic *gezeirah* regarding one's conduct in a large *sukkah*, *Beit Shammai eima lo yatza u-Beit Hillel eima yatza miba'ei lei* — the *mishna* should have said *Bet Shammai* declares that one has not fulfilled one's obligation (*lo yatza*) and *Bet Hillel* says one has fulfilled it (*yatza*). The use of *poslin* and *machshirin* — language that declares the object valid or invalid — implies that they are ruling on the status of the *sukkah* itself, i.e., its minimum dimensions. So the *mishna*'s language actually points both ways: some of its language (the description of the scenario) suggests a debate about conduct in a large *sukkah*, while other language (*poslin* / *machshirin*) suggests a debate about the *sukkah*'s status.

### Two Debates

*Le-olam bitarti pligi* — both debates are present simultaneously. *Pligi be-sukkah ketana, pligi be-sukkah gedola* — they debate both the minimum size and the scenario of the large *sukkah* with the table in the house. *Ve-hisurei mechsara ve-hachi katani* — the text must be understood as having omitted some words, and here is the full version: *Mi shehaya rosho ve-rubo ba-sukkah ve-shulchano betoch habayit* — one whose head and body was in the *sukkah* and whose table was in the house. *Beit Shammai omrim lo yatza* — *Bet Shammai* says he did not fulfill his obligation. *U-Beit Hillel omrim yatza* — *Bet Hillel* says he did. That is the first debate, concerning a large *sukkah* with the table in the house. Then the second case: *ve-she'eina machzika ela kidei rosho ve-rubo bilvad* — a *sukkah* that can contain only one's head and body. *Beit Shammai poslin* — *Bet Shammai* invalidate it. *U-Beit Hillel machshirin* — *Bet Hillel* validate it. That is the second, separate debate about minimum size.

It is worth acknowledging that when the *Gemara* employs the formula *hisurei mechsara* — that words are missing from the text — one is sometimes left with the feeling that the reading is forced. Some *Acharonim*, including the *Gra* explicitly, maintain that when the *Gemara* says *hisurei mechsara* it is not genuinely claiming there was a different manuscript tradition (*girsa*), but rather consciously reworking a text that does not fit the position being advanced. Here, however, the reading is reasonably persuasive, because there is genuine independent evidence that both debates exist: the *braitot* clearly use dimensional language, and the *mishna*'s language, as shown, points in both directions. The claim that two debates became conflated in a single text is not implausible.

### Linked Debates

Are these two debates logically connected? They appear to be. The question of minimum size is ultimately a question about what constitutes meaningful habitation of a *sukkah*: what does it mean to *dwell* in a *sukkah*? As will emerge later in the *masechta*, dwelling means eating and sleeping. A *sukkah* that can contain one's head and the majority of one's body provides sufficient shelter for sleeping — one's head and most of one's body will be covered and protected. But can such a space also serve as a setting for eating? Eating requires a table. Is a space a genuine dwelling for eating purposes if it cannot accommodate the table?

This connects directly to the *gezeirah* debate. *Bet Shammai*, who does not trust a person to remain rooted in the *sukkah* when the table is in the house, evidently holds that a space without room for the table cannot be considered an eating space at all — there is no permanence of presence there, since one is constantly being pulled outside to retrieve food. If the space cannot contain the table, it is not the kind of space in which a person can genuinely inhabit and eat. *Bet Hillel*, who is unconcerned that the person will be drawn after the table, holds that even without the table present one can be genuinely present and rooted in the *sukkah* — and thus a space of just *rosho v'rubo* is a sufficient minimum for habitation.

This logical linkage raises a further practical question: since we rule — at least in the matter of minimum size — like *Bet Shammai*, do we also rule like *Bet Shammai* regarding the large *sukkah* with the table in the house? This is in fact a *machloket Rishonim*. Several *Rishonim* rule like *Bet Shammai* for minimum size but hold that if the *sukkah* is large and the table is in the house, one still fulfills one's obligation. Others argue that the two positions are logically bound together and must be ruled upon consistently.

*Tosafot* address this question, and also take up the broader issue of when we rule like *Bet Shammai* — itself a rare occurrence. *Tosafot* quote *Rav Amram* among the *Geonim*, who lists six places where the *halacha* follows *Bet Shammai*, and this is one of them: *beseder Rav Amram pasak beshisha mekomot halacha keBeit Shammai chada hai deSukkah tzericha shetehe machzeket rosho verubo veshulchano* — *Rav Amram* ruled in six places according to *Bet Shammai*, one of which is that a *sukkah* must be able to contain one's head, body, and table.

One might also ask whether the ruling of *machshirin* represents a *lechatchila* or *bedieved* position. The term *machshirin* is generally understood as *lechatchila* — it does not merely validate after the fact, but declares the thing kosher categorically. By contrast, *yatza* sometimes carries a *bedieved* implication and sometimes does not, depending on context.

There is a further remarkable dimension to this *sugya* that emerges from *Bet Shammai*'s rebuke of *Yochanan ben haChoranis*: *lo kiyamta mitzvat sukkah miyamecha* — you have never in your life fulfilled the *mitzvah* of *sukkah*. What is the force of this statement? If *Yochanan* was sitting in a large *sukkah* with only his table in the house, at the very least he should have been fulfilling his biblical obligation, even if he was violating a rabbinic *gezeirah*. The implication of *Bet Shammai*'s words is that by violating the rabbinic requirement, he failed to fulfill even the biblical obligation. This is a striking and difficult principle: can the Sages, through a rabbinic edict, deprive a person of credit for a biblical *mitzvah* that he objectively performed?

*Tosafot* confront this directly. Reading the relevant passage: *heicha deBeit Hillel machmirin mid'rabbanan uBeit Shammai mokmi ledi-Oraisa, velo gazrinan* — in a case where *Bet Hillel* is the more stringent position rabbinically and *Bet Shammai* maintains the biblical baseline without the rabbinic stringency, if one acts in accordance with *Bet Shammai*'s position and neglects *Bet Hillel*'s rabbinic addition, *yatza yedei chovaso afilu d'Oraisa* — one has fulfilled one's obligation even on the biblical level. But the reverse case, by contrast — *l'Beis Shammai heicha deyisiv afischa d'matalaltah degazri shema yimashach achar shulchano d'amri lei Beis Shammai lo kiyamtem mitzvas sukkah miyamechem d'afilu m'Oraisa lo kiyam* — where one has a large *sukkah* and the table is in the house and *Bet Shammai* imposed a *gezeirah* out of concern that one will be drawn after the table, *Bet Shammai*'s declaration is that one has not fulfilled the *mitzvah* even on a biblical level.

The precise mechanics of how a rabbinic edict in the domain of *mitzvot asei* can undermine the fulfillment of a biblical obligation is genuinely difficult. Various possibilities are raised — *mitzvah haba'ah b'aveirah*, or the principle of *yesh koach beyad chachamim* — but no definitive explanation is offered. What is clear is that the principle operates differently in the realm of positive commandments than in the more familiar territory of prohibitions. When the Sages legislate in the area of *lo ta'asei*, the consequence of violation is a rabbinic transgression; the question of biblical fulfillment simply does not arise. But when they specify how a *mitzvat asei* must be performed, and someone fails to follow their specification, the question of whether the biblical *mitzvah* was nonetheless performed becomes live and genuinely complex. This does not mean every rabbinic requirement attached to *sukkah* carries this consequence — there are many rabbinic rules regarding valid *sechach*, for instance — but the principle that it can sometimes occur is established here.

## Rabbi: 4x4 Amot Minimum

### Size Braitot

*Man tana l'ha d'sanu rabbanan* — who is the *tanna* of the following *baraita*? The *baraita* teaches: *bayis she'ein bo arba amot al arba amot* — a house that does not contain four *amot* by four *amot* — is, as the *baraita* will proceed to list, exempt from a range of *halachos* that require a "house." The thrust of the *baraita* is that four *amot* by four *amot* is the universal minimum dimension for something to be halachically classified as a house, and the *Gemara* raises the question of whether this *baraita* represents the position of *Rebbe* alone or can be attributed to the Sages as well.

The *Gemara* resolves that even the Sages can agree to this standard, because the two debates are not in conflict: *ad kan lo kamri rabbanan hatam le'inyan sukkah d'dirat arai hi* — the Sages only said what they said there regarding *sukkah* because a *sukkah* is a temporary dwelling, and its entire definitional framework is one of impermanence. *Aval legabi bayit, d'dirat keva hu* — but with respect to a house, which is by definition a permanent dwelling, even the Sages would concede that *arba amot al arba amot* is the threshold below which *dayri inshi* does not apply — people simply do not inhabit such a space on any ongoing, settled basis. The minimum size of a *sukkah*, in other words, is smaller than the minimum size of a house precisely because a *sukkah* is not a house: it is defined as a temporary dwelling, and the standards appropriate to permanence do not apply to it.

This resolution introduces a significant conceptual axis. The debate between *Rebbe* and the Sages over the minimum size of a *sukkah* may in fact be a debate about how a *sukkah* is to be categorized. If the Sages hold that a *sukkah* is a *dirat arai* — a temporary dwelling — then a smaller minimum suffices, because the standards of permanent habitation simply do not govern it. *Rebbe*, who demands four *amot* by four *amot*, may hold that a *sukkah* must attain the status of a *dirat keva* — a permanent dwelling — because *basukkot teshvu* demands genuine habitation, and genuine habitation requires the kind of space in which a person could actually live on a sustained basis. This connects to a broader tension that runs through *masechet Sukkah* from its opening pages: is the point of *sukkah* to create something meaningfully different from your house — something essentially *arai* — or is it to relocate your full domestic life into a structure that, for seven days, functions as your home? The present *sugya* makes clear that however one resolves that question for *sukkah*, the category of *bayit* for all other *halachic* purposes operates on an independent and universal standard.

## 4x4: House

### Mezuzah/Tzaraat

The *baraita* lists the following consequences of a structure being less than four *amot* by four *amot*:

*Patur min hamezuzah* — it is exempt from the obligation of a *mezuzah*, for the verse states *al mezuzot beitecha* — on the doorposts of your *house* — and this does not constitute a house.

*U'min hama'akeh* — it does not require a railing around the roof, for the verse states *ki sivneh bayit chadash* — when you build a new *house* — and this is not a house.

*V'eino mitamei binega'im* — if it develops spots it does not become ritually impure with the impurity of plague-spots on houses, for the relevant verse speaks of *kenega nir'ah li babayit* — an appearance of a plague-spot in a *house*.

### Sale & Draft

*V'eino nechlat bevatei arei chomah* — a sale of such a structure in a walled city does not become irrevocable after a year. The law of houses in walled cities, whereby the buyer acquires the property permanently once the year of redemption has passed, applies only to a *house*. Something smaller than four by four is treated like an open field or courtyard, and can always be redeemed, reverting at the Jubilee.

*V'ein chozrim alav me'orchei hamilchama* — one who built such a structure does not qualify for an exemption from military service. The draft exemption is granted to one who *asher banah bayit chadash* — who built a new *house* — and this is not a house.

### Eruv Exemption

*V'ein me'arvin bo v'ein mishtatfin bo* — it does not have to participate in an *eruv* of a courtyard, and is not required to join the *shituf mevo'ot* of an alleyway. *Mai ta'ama?* — Why? *D'lo chazi l'dira* — because it is not fit for dwelling. The principle here differs slightly from the foregoing: this is not primarily about the conceptual definition of a "house," but about the practical requirement that those who *dwell* in the courtyard join together. Since one does not genuinely dwell in so small a structure, one need not participate.

### Eruv Storage

*V'ein manichin bo eruv* — the collected bread for the *eruv* of the courtyard may not be stored in it, because the rule is that the *eruv* must be placed in one of the houses of the courtyard, and this does not qualify.

The *Gemara* then refines this: *aval shituf manichin bo* — however, the bread for the *shituf mevo'ot* (the joining of the alleyways) may be stored in it. *Mai ta'ama? Delo gara michatzer* — because it is no worse than the courtyard itself.

The basic configuration to keep in mind: houses open onto a *chatzer* (courtyard), and courtyards open onto a *mavoi* (alleyway). When the houses of a courtyard join together in an *eruv chatzeirot*, the bread must be placed in one of the houses. When the courtyards of an alleyway join together in a *shituf mevo'ot*, the bread is placed in one of the courtyards. The *Gemara* then notes that the *mishna* can be misread as saying the *eruv chatzeirot* goes in the courtyard itself, but the correct reading, as a *baraita* clarifies, is: *eiruvi chatzeiros b'bayis shebachatzer* — the *eruv chatzeirot* goes in a house within the courtyard; *v'shitufai mavo'os bachatzer shebamavoi* — and the *shituf mevo'ot* goes in a courtyard within the alleyway. Since the *shituf mevo'ot* can be placed in the yard itself, a small structure that is no worse than a yard is equally acceptable for this purpose, even though it is too small to qualify as a house.

## Sukkah vs House

### Inference

The *Gemara* has now established that four by four *amot* is the universal halachic minimum for a "house." The question that naturally arises is: does this imply that *Rebbe*'s ruling — that a *sukkah* must be four by four *amot* — is the only opinion that could endorse this *baraita*, meaning the *baraita* follows *Rebbe* and not the Sages?

### Arai vs Keva

*Afilu teima Rabbanan* — even the Sages can agree to this *baraita*. The Sages said that a *sukkah* can be as small as *rosho v'rubo veshulchano* only because of a specific characteristic of *sukkah*: *d'dirat arai hi* — it is a temporary dwelling. The leniency regarding a *sukkah*'s minimum size is grounded in its nature as something other than a permanent residence. But for a permanent dwelling — *d'dirat keva hu* — everyone agrees that the minimum size is four by four *amot*: *d'ee ika arba amot al arba amot dayri inshi* — four by four is the threshold at which people will genuinely dwell in an ongoing, regular fashion, *v'ee lo lo dayri inshi* — and below that threshold, the space is not one in which people would regularly live.

### Temporary Allows

The introduction of this principle generates an important conceptual point: four by four *amot* is the halachic minimum for a permanent dwelling, and this is apparently uncontested. The smaller size — *rosho v'rubo veshulchano* — is sanctioned specifically because a *sukkah* is a *dirat arai*, a temporary dwelling, and therefore can be smaller. This raises a significant question regarding *Rebbe*'s position: if *Rebbe* requires a *sukkah* to be four by four *amot*, it would appear that *Rebbe* treats a *sukkah* not as a temporary dwelling but as a permanent one. This may indeed be the heart of the dispute: perhaps both *Rebbe* and the Sages agree that four by four characterizes a permanent dwelling and that *rosho v'rubo* characterizes a temporary one, and their dispute is precisely whether a *sukkah* must function as a permanent dwelling or whether its temporary character suffices.

This ties back to the opening theme of the *masechta*. The discussion began with the principle that a *sukkah* must not be so tall as to constitute a permanent structure. Now the inverse is being explored: perhaps a *sukkah* must actually achieve a degree of permanence in order to fulfill the obligation of *basukkot teshvu* — you shall dwell in *sukkot* — since genuine dwelling requires a habitable, somewhat permanent space. The tension between *dirat arai* and *dirat keva* as competing definitions of what a *sukkah* must be is thus a thread running through multiple *sugyot* in this *masechta*.

## 4x4 Amot: Universal House

### City Joining

The *baraita* continues with additional laws requiring a four by four structure: *ve'ein osin oto ibur bein shtei ayarot* — it cannot serve to join two cities halachically.

The background: each city has a *techum* — a boundary of two thousand *amot* — extending from its edge in all directions. The question of where a city's edge is drawn follows the principle that any two structures within 70⅔ *amot* of each other are considered part of the same settlement. If two cities are each within 70⅔ *amot* of a midpoint structure, they are treated as one city, and the two-thousand *amot* is measured from the outermost edges of the combined settlement. This is called making the structure a *ibur* — a pregnancy — between the two cities, because it causes them to "carry" one another as a single unit. However, the structure serving this connecting function must qualify as a house; if it is too small to be a house, it cannot extend the city limits in this way.

### Burginin vs House

The *Gemara* raises an immediate question: surely a *burganin* — a lean-to or watchman's hut in a field — can serve to join two cities even though it is not a four by four house? If a lean-to suffices, why is a small structure disqualified?

The answer is: *burganin chazu l'milsaihu* — a lean-to is fit for what it is made for. A watchman's hut is a legitimate structure of its type; it was built to serve the function of temporary sheltering in a field. *Vehai lo chazi l'milsai* — but a structure built in the manner of a house, yet too small to function as one, is not fit for its intended purpose. The analogy is to the difference between a condemned house and a tent. Both may provide similar shelter, but a condemned house carries no recognition or legitimacy as a structure — it is built as a house but fails as one — whereas a tent, built for its purpose, is fully legitimate of its kind. A *burganin* is like the tent: it is what it purports to be. A small house-like structure is like the condemned house: it is constructed as a house but is not fit for that function, and therefore cannot serve any halachic role that requires a genuine house or even a genuine temporary shelter. *Rashi* adds that a *burganin* is built for a single night's stay, whereas a house-like structure implicitly represents an attempt at multiple-night, more regular dwelling — and if it is too small for that, it is entirely unfit for its purpose.

## Sukkah vs House: Status

### Rebbe Permanent?

With the principle of *dirat keva* versus *dirat arai* now articulated, the question returns: does *Rebbe*'s requirement of four by four *amot* for a *sukkah* reflect a position that a *sukkah* must constitute a permanent dwelling? If so, *Rebbe* and the Sages may be debating not just a number but the fundamental character of the *sukkah* itself — whether the obligation of *teshvu* demands that it be a *dirat keva*, or whether it is fulfilled in a space that is genuinely *arai*. This remains an open and important question as the *masechta* continues.

## 4x4 Amot: House

### Brother Division

The *baraita* lists one further case: *ve'ein achin ve'shutafin cholkin bo* — brothers or partners do not divide a house that is smaller than four by four *amot*.

The *Gemara* objects: the *baraita* implies that once the structure does have four by four *amot*, it can be divided. But how can a four by four *amah* house be divided between two people? Each half would then be only two by four *amot*, and the *mishna* elsewhere rules: *ein cholkin es hachatzer ad sheyehei bo arba amot l'zeh v'arba amot l'zeh* — a courtyard is not divided unless each person receives four by four *amot*. The same logic would apply to a house: dividing a four by four *amah* house would leave each party with less than four by four, which is insufficient. The *Gemara* therefore reinterprets: *ella eima ein bo din chalukah k'chatzer* — rather, it does not get divided the way a *chatzer* gets divided. The point is not that it can never be divided, but that it is not subject to the special rules governing the division of a courtyard.

## Courtyard Division

### Rav Huna

The *Gemara* now explains how a courtyard is divided. *D'amar Rav Huna*: *chatzer l'fi pesachim mischalekes* — a courtyard is divided according to its doorways.

### Rav Chisda

*V'Rav Chisda amar*: *nosin l'chol pesach u'pesach arba amot v'hashar chokin oso b'shaveh* — every doorway receives four *amot*, and the remainder is divided equally.

To illustrate the dispute: suppose two brothers, *Reuven* and *Shimon*, inherit a courtyard from their father. *Reuven*'s portion includes one large mansion with a single door onto the courtyard; *Shimon*'s includes three small houses each with one door. According to *Rav Huna*, since the courtyard exists to serve the houses — providing access for unloading and movement — it should be divided in proportion to the number of doors: *Shimon* has three doors and *Reuven* one, so *Shimon* receives three-quarters of the courtyard and *Reuven* one-quarter. According to *Rav Chisda*, one agrees that each doorway requires some portion of the courtyard in front of it — specifically, four *amot* — but the rest of the courtyard is not allocated by the number of doors; it is divided equally. *Shimon* gets four *amot* per door (three portions) and *Reuven* four *amot* for his door (one portion), but the remaining courtyard space beyond these allocations is split evenly between them regardless of the disparity in doorways.

### Counting Houses

The relevance to the preceding discussion now becomes clear: under either approach, a doorway belonging to a house that is less than four by four *amot* does not count. *Dahani milei bayis d'leimeha vei kai* — this applies to a doorway of a house that is going to remain standing as such; in that case, *yahavinan leh chatzer* — we allocate it a portion of the yard. But *d'limistar kai* — a condemned house, one that is effectively ready to be torn down, *lo yahavinan leh chatzer* — is not allocated any portion of the yard, because it has no standing as a structure deserving of consideration. A house that is too small to qualify as a house falls into this category: it is constructed in the form of a house but lacks the minimum dimensions, and therefore its doorway does not factor into either *Rav Huna*'s proportional division or *Rav Chisda*'s allocation of four *amot* per doorway.
