# Avodah Zarah 36 — Daf Yomi Shiur
[DAF:36a]

## Oil Kashrut & Repealed D…

### Oil Prohibition Overview

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַשֶּׁמֶן שֶׁלָּהֶן. שֶׁמֶן, רַב אָמַר: דָּנִיאֵל גָּזַר עָלָיו, וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: זְלִיפָתָן שֶׁל כֵּלִים טְמֵאִים אוֹסַרְתָּן.
>
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And their oil** was originally prohibited but later permitted by Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and his court. The Gemara cites a dispute with regard to the origin of the prohibition of **oil. Rav says: Daniel decreed** that oil is prohibited, **and Shmuel says:** The **secretion of ritually impure vessels prohibits** the oil that gentiles pour into them.

The final line of 35b introduces the next topic of the *mishnah*: the *kashrut* of oil produced by idolaters. The *mishnah* records that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and his *beit din* — permitted the oil, implying that at some earlier point a prohibition had been in force and was subsequently repealed. The discussion therefore proceeds on two tracks: when was the prohibition first instituted, and why, and how and why was it later repealed.

This case is notable as one of the relatively few examples in *Shas* of a rabbinic *gezeirah* that was formally abrogated, and it becomes an important test case for understanding the scope and parameters of rabbinic legislative authority.

### Cheese Prohibition Digre…

Before turning to the oil, it is worth noting the prior discussion of why non-Jewish cheese was prohibited. The *sugyah* canvassed numerous explanations, and *Tosafot*'s conclusion appears to follow Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's position that the concern is snake venom — a matter of *sakana*. Characterizing it as a *sakana* opens the question of whether, if the danger could be demonstrably eliminated, the prohibition might no longer apply, at least according to those authorities who reason that way. Indeed, some have argued that the *Rama*, the *Chazon Ish*, and even Rav Soloveitchik ruled in line with *Tosafot* in various circumstances.

By contrast, the *Rambam* and many other prominent *rishonim* hold that the prohibition stems from the *neveilah* concern: the rennet used in cheese production is derived from the stomach lining of an animal slaughtered without *shechita*, making it a piece of actual meat. Because this *ma'amid* — the coagulating agent — imparts the flavor of *neveilah* into the cheese and cannot be *batel*, the rabbis decreed against all cheese produced by non-Jewish manufacturers. On this view, even cheeses produced today with microbial or vegetable-derived curdling agents enter a debate already found among the *geonim* and *rishonim* about whether non-animal curdling agents fall under the *gezeirah* at all. The *Rambam* and many *geonim* were *machmir* on this point, though the Talmud itself leaves the matter open.

### Oil Repeal Question

Returning to the *shemen*: the discussion that follows asks both why the prohibition was originally instituted and what justification Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi had for repealing it.

## Rav's Daniel Theory

### Daniel Gazar Alav

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב אָמַר: דָּנִיאֵל גָּזַר עָלָיו
>
> **Translation:** **Rav says: Daniel decreed** that oil is prohibited

It was Daniel and his *beit din*, during the period of the Babylonian exile, who decreed against the use of oil produced by idolaters. Daniel is not classified as a *navi* in the strict sense within the rabbinic tradition, but he occupies a role close to that of a prophet, and this attribution grants the *gezeirah* considerable antiquity and authority.

### Shmuel's Tamei Vessel Th…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: זְלִיפָתָן שֶׁל כֵּלִים טְמֵאִים אוֹסַרְתָּן.
>
> **Translation:** **and Shmuel says:** The **secretion of ritually impure vessels prohibits** the oil that gentiles pour into them.

However, offers a different rationale: the prohibition arose because of the seepage of *tamei* liquids from the vessels in which the oil was stored. The concern is that a jar already containing absorbed *tamei* substances would leach those substances into the olive oil placed within it, rendering the oil *tamei*.

### Tahara Not Universal

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אַטּוּ כּוּלֵּי עָלְמָא אוֹכְלֵי טְהָרוֹת נִינְהוּ?
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **Is that to say that all** people **are consumers of** only ritually **pure** substances?

The *gemara* challenges Shmuel's formulation immediately: is everyone who eats food required to maintain *tahara*? Eating *chulin* in a state of *tahara* was a *midat chasidut*, a special stringency practiced by portions of the rabbinic class during the *mishnaic* period, but it was never obligatory for the general population. A *gezeirah* premised on that concern would rest on a very narrow foundation.

### Prohibited Vessels Seepa…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, זְלִיפָתָן שֶׁל כֵּלִים אֲסוּרִים אוֹסַרְתָּן.
>
> **Translation:** **Rather, the secretion of prohibited vessels prohibits** the oil, as it absorbs the prohibited substances.

Shmuel accordingly revises his position: the concern is not *tamei* vessels but prohibited (*assur*) vessels, that is, containers that had previously held non-kosher wine, lard, or other prohibited substances. The absorbed flavor of those prohibited materials would seep into the olive oil stored in the same vessels. One might initially object that such contamination would degrade the quality of the oil and therefore constitute *noten ta'am lifgam* — a detrimental flavor — which is ordinarily permissible. The *gemara* will address this directly.

## Shmuel vs. Rav Debate

### Shmuel's Logic for Repeal

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ שְׁמוּאֵל לְרַב: בִּשְׁלָמָא לְדִידִי דְּאָמֵינָא זְלִיפָתָן שֶׁל כֵּלִים אֲסוּרִים אוֹסַרְתָּן, הַיְינוּ דְּכִי אֲתָא רַב יִצְחָק בַּר שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר מָרְתָא וְאָמַר: דָּרֵישׁ רַבִּי שִׂמְלַאי בִּנְצִיבִין: שֶׁמֶן, רַבִּי יְהוּדָה וּבֵית דִּינוֹ נִמְנוּ עָלָיו וְהִתִּירוּהוּ.
>
> **Translation:** **Shmuel said to Rav: Granted,** according **to my** opinion, **as I say** that **the secretion of prohibited vessels prohibits** the oil, **this is** how one can understand **that when Rav Yitzḥak bar Shmuel bar Marta came, he said** that **Rabbi Simlai taught in Netzivin:** With regard to **oil, Rabbi Yehuda** HaNasi **and his court were counted,** i.e., voted, **on this** matter **and permitted it.**

Shmuel now presses his advantage. His own position explains coherently why the prohibition could later be repealed. As he notes, *Rav Yitzchak bar Shmuel bar Marta* reported that *Rabi Simlai* taught in *Ntzivin* that when Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and his *beit din* took a vote on the oil, they voted to permit it. Their rationale, according to Shmuel, was *kasavar noten ta'am lifgam mutar*: they ruled, or reassessed, that any flavor that would be transferred from the prohibited vessels into the oil would damage rather than improve the oil's taste, and a *noten ta'am lifgam* imparts no halachic prohibition.

One might ask whether *noten ta'am lifgam* was simply not a recognized principle before Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's time, or whether the earlier authorities had assessed the flavor transfer differently, or whether Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and his court were clarifying the proper application of an already-established rule. These are significant questions the *gemara* leaves open.

In any case, Shmuel's point is that if the original prohibition had a concrete and limited rationale — the seepage of non-kosher flavors into the oil — then a later *beit din* can reevaluate whether that concern still applies under current circumstances and repeal the *gezeirah* accordingly.

### Repeal Authority Challen…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ שְׁמוּאֵל לְרַב: בִּשְׁלָמָא לְדִידִי... אֶלָּא לְדִידָךְ דְּאָמְרַתְּ דָּנִיאֵל גָּזַר עָלָיו, דָּנִיאֵל גְּזַר וַאֲתָא רַבִּי יְהוּדָה הַנָּשִׂיא וּמְבַטֵּל לֵיהּ? וְהָתְנַן: אֵין בֵּית דִּין יָכוֹל לְבַטֵּל דִּבְרֵי בֵּית דִּין חֲבֵירוֹ, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן גָּדוֹל הֵימֶנּוּ בְּחָכְמָה וּבְמִנְיָן!
>
> **Translation:** **But according to you,** Rav, **who said** that **Daniel decreed** a prohibition **upon** the oil of gentiles, how can this be understood? Can it be said that **Daniel decreed** it, **and Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi came and voided** the decree? **But didn't we learn** in a mishna (*Eduyyot* 1:5): **A court cannot void the statements of another court, unless it is greater than it in wisdom and in number?**

Shmuel then turns the argument against Rav: but on your account, Rav, that it was Daniel who decreed against the oil, how can you maintain that Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi could subsequently come and repeal it? The concern here is one of institutional hierarchy and legal precedent. A *gezeirah* instituted by a figure of Daniel's stature, centuries earlier, represents a deeply embedded legal norm. And indeed, one must also consider how much of this was Daniel's personal stringency versus an actual promulgated decree — the *gemara* at this stage does not say *Daniel u-veit dino* but simply attributes the *gezeirah* to Daniel himself, which will become significant shortly.

### Ein Beit Din Yachol Leva…

The challenge is rooted in the well-known principle: one court cannot abrogate the rulings of another court unless it is greater in wisdom and in number. This principle, discussed extensively in the first chapter of *Beitzah*, raises a fundamental question about the architecture of rabbinic law. There are at least two ways to read the principle. One reading addresses competing courts of equal standing within the same era, neither of which can override the other's local rulings — the remedy is to appeal to a higher authority, namely the *Sanhedrin*. The other reading, which appears to be the relevant one here, addresses the relationship between *batei din* across historical periods: can a later *Sanhedrin* repeal decrees issued by an earlier *Sanhedrin*?

The word *minyan* in the phrase *gadol heimenu be-chokhma u-ve-minyan* is itself subject to debate among the *rishonim*. It could refer literally to the number of members of the court — the *Sanhedrin* was normally seventy-one, though the *Anshei Knesset HaGedolah* had one hundred twenty members, which may be why certain enactments of Ezra's court are considered unrepeatable. Alternatively, *minyan* might refer not to the number of members but to the size of the court's constituency — the number of students and followers who adhere to that court's rulings, and thus a measure of its authority and reach. Both interpretations carry significant implications for how *dinim d'rabbanan* function over time.

### Ad Hominem vs. Lod

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ: שִׂמְלַאי לוּדָּאָה קָא אָמְרַתְּ?
>
> **Translation:** Rather than answering Shmuel's question directly, Rav first rejects his proof: Rav **said to him:** Was it Rabbi **Simlai of Lod** of whom **you spoke?**

At this point in the debate, Rav attempts a different tactic. He questions whether the position that the oil prohibition was repealed really rests on a reliable source, asking: is this really what Simlai from *Lod* said? And then:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שָׁאנֵי לוּדָּאֵי דִּמְזַלְזְלוּ.
>
> **Translation:** Residents of Lod are different, as they disparage** the Sages' decrees

people from *Lod* are known to be lax in their observance of rabbinic decrees, and therefore their opinions on the weight and bindingness of *dinim d'rabbanan* cannot be taken as authoritative. This is a remarkable move — an *ad hominem* attack on an entire community rather than engagement with the legal substance.

### Rav's Tradition Defense

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִיכְּסִיף.
>
> **Translation:** Rav **became embarrassed.**

The *gemara* indicates that this approach backfired: Rav was embarrassed. The direction the argument had taken, attacking the community rather than the legal principle, was not the kind of reasoning Rav wanted to be associated with. He pivots to a substantive defense.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב: אִם הֵם לֹא דָּרְשׁוּ, אֲנַן לָא דָּרְשִׁינַן?
>
> **Translation:** **Rav said: If they,** Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and his court, **did not expound** the following verse, **will we not expound it?**

In other words, Rav concedes that the *pasuk* in Daniel is not independently compelling as a source for the prohibition; what makes the *drashah* meaningful is that it serves as a *mnemonic* for a law received through reliable tradition. The *drashah* is an *asmachta* that confirms what is already known from *mesorah*, not the source from which the law is derived.

## Daniel Proof & Binding N…

### Daniel 1:8 Citation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָכְתִיב: ״וַיָּשֶׂם דָּנִיֵּאל עַל לִבּוֹ אֲשֶׁר לֹא יִתְגָּאַל בְּפַת בַּג הַמֶּלֶךְ וּבְיֵין מִשְׁתָּיו״ — בִּשְׁתֵּי מִשְׁתָּאוֹת הַכָּתוּב מְדַבֵּר, אֶחָד מִשְׁתֵּה יַיִן וְאֶחָד מִשְׁתֵּה שֶׁמֶן.
>
> **Translation:** **Isn't it written: "But Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the king's food, nor with the wine which he drank [*mishtav*]"** (Daniel 1:8)? The word *mishtav* is in the plural, indicating that **the verse speaks of two** types of **drinking: One is the drinking of wine, and one is the drinking of oil.**

Rav proceeds to cite the verse: Daniel resolved not to defile himself with the king's bread or with his wine. The word *mishtav* is spelled *malei*, with a *yud*, which leads the *gemara* to read it as referring to two distinct types of drink: *bishtei mishta'ot hakasuv medaber*, the verse speaks of two kinds of drinking — one of wine and one of oil.

### Weak Drashah Admission

Rav openly acknowledges the weakness of this reading as an independent *drashah*. Left to its own devices, no interpreter would read the verse as referencing oil. The *drashah* is valid only because the tradition already establishes that Daniel avoided non-Jewish oil, and the verse provides a post-hoc textual anchor for that tradition.

### Personal vs. Binding Dec…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַב סָבַר: עַל לִבּוֹ שָׂם, וּלְכׇל יִשְׂרָאֵל הוֹרָה
>
> **Translation:** **Rav maintained:** Daniel **placed** the prohibition against consuming the oil of gentiles **upon his heart,** i.e., for himself, **and he instructed all Jews** to adhere to it as well.

The real disagreement between Rav and Shmuel crystallizes around the question of whether Daniel's avoidance of oil constituted a personal practice or a binding legal decree. Rav holds that the phrase *al libo sam*, "he resolved in his heart," indicates that Daniel decided this for himself personally, but then *hora* — he issued a *hora'ah*, a legal ruling, making it binding upon all of Israel. The verb *hora* is significant: it implies a formal *psak*, not merely a personal stringency.

### Shmuel: Personal Decisio…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּשְׁמוּאֵל סָבַר: עַל לִבּוֹ שָׂם, וּלְכׇל יִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא הוֹרָה.
>
> **Translation:** **And Shmuel held:** Daniel **placed** the prohibition **upon his heart, but he did not instruct all Jews** to accept it.

Shmuel holds that Daniel's resolution was purely personal. He decided this for himself but never promulgated it as a binding decree upon the community. On Shmuel's reading, there was therefore no *gezeirah* in the time of Daniel at all; the prohibition arose for entirely different reasons at a later period, as Shmuel explained, and Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's court had full authority to reassess and repeal it.

## Eighteen Decrees Question

### Oil Among Eighteen?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְשֶׁמֶן, דָּנִיאֵל גְּזַר? וְהָאָמַר בָּאלִי אֲבִימִי נִוְתָאָה מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַב: פִּיתָּן וְשַׁמְנָן, יֵינָן וּבְנוֹתֵיהֶן, כּוּלָּן מִשְּׁמוֹנָה עָשָׂר דָּבָר הֵן.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** is it correct that **Daniel decreed** that **oil** is prohibited? **But doesn't Balei say** that **Avimi of Nota** says **in the name of Rav:** The prohibitions of gentiles' **bread and their oil, their wine and their daughters, are all from the eighteen matters** decreed in a single day in the days of the students of Shammai and Hillel.

A new question is raised: was the oil prohibition counted among the eighteen decrees enacted by *Beit Shammai* and *Beit Hillel* on the famous day when *Beit Shammai* outnumbered *Beit Hillel* and pushed through eighteen *gezeirot*? If oil was among those eighteen, the problem of repeal becomes even more acute.

### Daniel Rejected, Later A…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי תֵּימָא: אֲתָא דָּנִיאֵל גְּזַר וְלָא קַיבֻּל, וַאֲתוֹ תַּלְמִידֵי דְּהִלֵּל וְשַׁמַּאי וּגְזוּר וְקַיבֻּל
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara adds: **And if you would say** that **Daniel came** and **decreed but** the people **did not accept** it, **and** later **the students of Hillel and Shammai came and decreed** with regard to gentiles' oil **and** the people **accepted** it

One possibility offered: Daniel did attempt to institute the decree, but the people did not accept it. Then, in a later generation, the students of *Hillel* and *Shammai* reinstated the same *gezeirah* and this time it was accepted. If this is the case, one might ask: what was the point of Rav citing the Danielic origin of the decree if it was not accepted until the time of the students of *Hillel* and *Shammai*? The *gemara* regards this as a valid challenge.

### Urban vs. Rural Scope

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, דָּנִיאֵל גָּזַר עָלָיו בָּעִיר, וַאֲתוֹ אִינְהוּ וּגְזוּר אֲפִילּוּ בַּשָּׂדֶה.
>
> **Translation:** **Rather, Daniel decreed upon** the oil of gentiles **in the city, and** the students of Shammai and Hillel **came and decreed** that it is prohibited **even in the field.**

An alternative resolution: Daniel originally decreed against oil in urban settings, where the risk of adulteration or social intermingling was more acute. Later, the students of *Shammai* and *Hillel* extended the *gezeirah*: they extended it to rural settings as well.

### Repeal Authority vs. Sha…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרַבִּי יְהוּדָה הַנָּשִׂיא הֵיכִי מָצֵי לְמִישְׁרֵא תַּקַּנְתָּא דְּתַלְמִידֵי שַׁמַּאי וְהִלֵּל? וְהָתְנַן: אֵין בֵּית דִּין יָכוֹל לְבַטֵּל דִּבְרֵי בֵּית דִּין חֲבֵירוֹ, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן גָּדוֹל הֵימֶנּוּ בְּחָכְמָה וּבְמִנְיָן!
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And how could Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi permit an ordinance** issued **by the students of Shammai and Hillel? But didn't we learn** in a mishna that **a court cannot void the statements of another court, unless it is greater than it in wisdom and in number?**

Either way, a serious difficulty remains: how could Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi repeal a *takkanah* established by the students of *Shammai* and *Hillel*, who are of obviously far greater antiquity and seniority? The same principle reasserts itself: *ein beit din yachol levatel divrei beit din chaveiro ela im ken gadol heimenu be-chokhma u-ve-minyan*.

## Majority Acceptance Prin…

### All Decrees Repeatable E…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעוֹד, הָא אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר בַּר חָנָה אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: בַּכֹּל יָכוֹל לְבַטֵּל בֵּית דִּין דִּבְרֵי בֵּית דִּין חֲבֵירוֹ, חוּץ מִשְּׁמוֹנָה עָשָׂר דָּבָר, שֶׁאֲפִילּוּ יָבֹא אֵלִיָּהוּ וּבֵית דִּינוֹ אֵין שׁוֹמְעִין לוֹ!
>
> **Translation:** **And furthermore, doesn't Rabba bar bar Ḥana say** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: With regard to all** issues, **a court can void the statements of another court, except the eighteen matters** decreed by the students of Beit Shammai, **as, even** if **Elijah and his court** were to **come** and rescind them, **one** would **not listen to him.**

The *gemara* invokes an important principle from *Rabbah bar bar Chana* in the name of *Rabbi Yochanan*: in general, any *beit din* can repeal the rulings of an earlier *beit din*, except for those eighteen specific matters.

### Eliyahu Cannot Repeal Ei…

The principle is underscored dramatically: even if Eliyahu himself arrives in the Messianic era and convenes a *beit din* to repeal those eighteen *gezeirot*, the ruling is not to be heeded. This raises a fascinating question: why specifically those eighteen? They were enacted on a single day when *Beit Shammai* achieved a majority over *Beit Hillel* — what makes those eighteen uniquely immune to repeal while every other *gezeirah* from earlier or later periods remains in principle reversible? The *gemara* does not pause to address this directly, but notes that Eliyahu's *beit din* would be subject to the same rule — prophetic authority and legislative authority are categorically distinct, as the *Rambam* elaborates extensively: prophets legislate only in their capacity as heads of the *Beit Din HaGadol*, never by virtue of their prophetic powers alone.

### Rav Mesharshiya: Spread…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב מְשַׁרְשְׁיָא: מַה טַּעַם? הוֹאִיל וּפָשַׁט אִיסּוּרוֹ בְּרוֹב יִשְׂרָאֵל, שֶׁמֶן לֹא פָּשַׁט אִיסּוּרוֹ בְּרוֹב יִשְׂרָאֵל
>
> **Translation:** **Rav Mesharshiyya said: What is the reason** that none of the eighteen decrees can be voided? **Since** the **prohibition spread among the majority of the Jewish people,** it cannot be voided. But with regard to **oil, its prohibition did not spread among the majority of the Jewish people,** and therefore it can be voided.

Rav Mesharshiya provides the underlying rationale: those eighteen *gezeirot* are irreversible because they spread throughout the majority of the Jewish people and became universally accepted. the oil prohibition, by contrast, never achieved that broad acceptance. This is why Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi was not only legally empowered to repeal it but had good reason to do so: the people were not observing it in any case.

### Investigation of Oil Acc…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּאָמַר רַبִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר אַבָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: יָשְׁבוּ רַבּוֹתֵינוּ וּבָדְקוּ עַל שֶׁמֶן שֶׁלֹּא פָּשַׁט אִיסּוּרוֹ בְּרוֹב יִשְׂרָאֵל
>
> **Translation:** **As Rabbi Shmuel bar Abba says** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: Our Sages sat and inspected** the matter of gentiles' **oil** and determined **that its prohibition had not spread among the majority of the Jewish people**

This factual claim is supported by *Rabbi Shmuel bar Abba* in the name of *Rabbi Yochanan*: the teachers of that generation — Rabbi Yochanan's era, shortly after Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi — conducted an investigation and found that the prohibition against oil had not spread throughout the majority of Israel.

### Majority Uphold Principle

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְסָמְכוּ רַבּוֹתֵינוּ עַל דִּבְרֵי רַבָּן שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל וְעַל דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר בַּר צָדוֹק, שֶׁהָיוּ אוֹמְרִים: אֵין גּוֹזְרִין גְּזֵירָה עַל הַצִּבּוּר אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן רוֹב צִבּוּר יְכוֹלִין לַעֲמוֹד בָּהּ
>
> **Translation:** **and our Sages relied upon the statement of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel and upon the statement of Rabbi Elazar bar Tzadok, who would say:** The Sages **issue a decree upon the community only if most of the community is able to abide by it.**

Upon this finding, they relied on the ruling of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel and Rabbi Elazar bar Tzadok, two *Tannaim* who stated: a *beit din* should not, or perhaps cannot, decree a law upon the community unless the majority of the community is capable of upholding it.

This formulation leaves a critical ambiguity: is this a formal limitation on the legal power of the *beit din* — such that a *gezeirah* which fails to achieve majority acceptance is retroactively invalid — or is it merely a counsel of prudence, warning against enacting decrees that will generate widespread violation? If the former, then the failure of a *gezeirah* to take hold might in principle render it void. If the latter, it remains a judgment call about good legislative practice, not a juridical nullity.

Further, even if the principle does function as a formal limitation, it does not appear to operate automatically. What is required is active judicial intervention: a *beit din* must investigate, find that the *gezeirah* failed to spread, and formally repeal it. That is precisely what Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi did. The law does not simply evaporate because people are not observing it; the authority of the *Beit Din HaGadol* is still necessary to give the repeal legal effect.

It is also worth noting that this position was in the minority: *Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel* and *Rabbi Elazar bar Tzadok* represent a dissenting Tannaitic view, one the majority of *Tannaim* apparently did not share. The mainstream position seems to have been that *gezeirot* may be enacted even if the community will not fully observe them. Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi found it necessary to invoke this minority view as the basis for the repeal.

## Malachi Source & Rov Tzi…

### Malachi 3:9 Proof

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּאָמַר רַב אַדָּא בַּר אַהֲבָה: מַאי קְרָא? ״בַּמְּאֵרָה אַתֶּם נֵאָרִים וְאֹתִי אַתֶּם קֹבְעִים הַגּוֹי כֻּלּוֹ״, אִי אִיכָּא גּוֹי כּוּלּוֹ — אִין, אִי לָא — לָא.
>
> **Translation:** **Rav Adda bar Ahava said: What is the verse** from which it is derived? It is the verse: **"You are cursed with the curse, yet you rob Me, even this whole nation"** (Malachi 3:9). This teaches that **if there is** the acceptance of **the whole nation, yes,** an ordinance may be instituted, but **if not, no,** the ordinance may not be instituted.

On the last line of 36a, *Rav Adda bar Ahava* provides a Scriptural peg for the principle that a *gezeirah* requires broad communal acceptance. The verse cited is from *Malachi* 3:9: "you are cursed with a curse, and you are robbing Me, the entire nation."

### Entire Nation Requirement

The operative phrase is *ha-goy kulo* — the entire nation. The verse associates the full force of a covenantal curse with the participation of the entire nation, not a partial constituency. Analogously, the argument runs, a rabbinic decree that binds with the force of law — and whose violation carries the weight of transgression — requires the acceptance of the entire community, or at minimum the majority. Only when *ha-goy kulo* is in play does the decree carry full operative force.

### Curse Acceptance Conditi…

*Rashi* notes that *bame'era atem ne'arim* refers to accepting a *gezeirah* in the manner of a binding oath or covenantal commitment, analogous to the acceptance at *Har Gerizim* and *Har Eival*. The implication: if the entire community is present as the accepting body, the decree takes effect; if not, it does not.

## Four Categories & Daught…

[DAF:36b]
### Four Things Listed

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גּוּפָא, אָמַר בָּאלִי אָמַר אֲבִימִי נִוְתָאָה מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַב: פִּיתָּן וְשַׁמְנָן, יֵינָן וּבְנוֹתֵיהֶן — כּוּלָּן מִשְּׁמוֹנָה עָשָׂר דָּבָר הֵן.
>
> **Translation:** § The Gemara discusses **the** matter **itself: Balei says** that **Avimi of Nota says in the name of Rav:** The prohibitions with regard to gentiles' **bread and their oil, their wine and their daughters, are all from the eighteen matters** issued in a single day in the time of the students of Shammai and Hillel.

The *gemara* now takes up a teaching attributed to *Bali* in the name of *Avimi*, quoting *Rav*: bread, oil, wine, and their "daughters" are all counted among the eighteen decrees of *Beit Shammai* and *Beit Hillel*.

### Benoteihem Definition

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּנוֹתֵיהֶן מַאי הִיא?
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: With regard to **their daughters, what is** the decree?

This prompts the question: what does "their daughters" mean in this context?

### Non-Jewish Women as Nidd…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן בַּר יִצְחָק: גָּזְרוּ עַל בְּנוֹתֵיהֶן נִידּוֹת מֵעֲרִיסוֹתָן.
>
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Naḥman bar Yitzḥak says: They decreed upon their daughters** that they should be classified as **menstruating women from** the time they are in **their cradle,** i.e., they decreed that from when they are young, gentile women are always considered to be menstruating.

*Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak* explains: the rabbis decreed that non-Jewish women are to be considered *niddot* from infancy, from the cradle. The background is that *mi-de'oraita*, the laws of *tumah ve-taharah* do not apply to non-Jews at all — with the possible exception of a non-Jewish corpse, itself subject to Tannaitic dispute. Non-Jews are not subject to *tumah* and cannot become *tamei* in the biblical sense. However, *mi-de-rabbanan* several forms of *tumah* were instituted with respect to non-Jews, presumably as an extension of the social boundaries constructed by the laws of *tumah ve-taharah* for the Jewish community itself. In this particular case, all non-Jewish women are treated rabbinically as if they were *niddot* from birth. The practical consequence is that any otherwise-prohibited sexual contact with a non-Jewish woman carries the added rabbinic violation of *niddah* as well.

### Gneiva: Avodah Zarah Rea…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּגְנִיבָא מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרַב אָמַר: כּוּלָּן מִשּׁוּם עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה גָּזְרוּ בָּהֶן
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara presents another opinion. **And Geneiva says in the name of Rav:** Gentiles' bread, oil, wine, and daughters were **all decreed upon due to** the concern that Jews might participate in **idol worship** with gentiles as a result of intermingling with them.

*Gneiva*, however, quoting *Rav*, offers a different explanation: all four of these categories were decreed upon for the same underlying reason, namely *avodah zarah*, concern about idolatrous practice.

## Causal Chain of Decrees

### Bread-Oil-Wine Chain

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּכִי אֲתָא רַב אַחָא בַּר אַדָּא אָמַר רַבִּי יִצְחָק: גָּזְרוּ עַל פִּיתָּן מִשּׁוּם שַׁמְנָן, מַאי אוּלְמֵיהּ דְּשֶׁמֶן מִפַּת? אֶלָּא, עַל פִּיתָּן וְשַׁמְנָן מִשּׁוּם יֵינָן
>
> **Translation:** **As, when Rav Aḥa bar Adda came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia he said that **Rabbi Yitzḥak says: They decreed** a prohibition **upon their bread due to their oil.** The Gemara asks: In **what** way **is the** prohibition with regard to **oil stronger than** the prohibition with regard to **bread?** The Gemara offers a different interpretation: **Rather,** they issued a decree prohibiting **their bread and their oil due to their wine.**

*Rav Acha bar Adda* came from *Eretz Yisrael* and quoted *Rabbi Yitzchak* elaborating the causal chain: they prohibited the bread because of the oil. One might ask why the oil is considered a stronger basis for the decree than the bread itself, which prompts the clarification: in fact both bread and oil are prohibited because of wine. The connection is social: eating together leads to drinking together, and oil is part of the food that accompanies the shared meal, bringing one in proximity to the prohibited wine.

### Wine → Daughters

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעַל יֵינָן מִשּׁוּם בְּנוֹתֵיהֶן, וְעַל בְּנוֹתֵיהֶן מִשּׁוּם דָּבָר אַחֵר
>
> **Translation:** **And** they issued the decree prohibiting **their wine due to** the fact that this leads to familiarity, and Jews will come to marry **their daughters. And** they issued a decree prohibiting **their daughters due to something else,** idolatry.

and wine itself is prohibited because of "their daughters," meaning that sharing wine leads to social and ultimately sexual proximity with non-Jewish women.

### Daughters: Davar Acher

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעַל דָּבָר אַחֵר מִשּׁוּם דָּבָר אַחֵר.
>
> **Translation:** **And** they further issued a decree **on something else due to something else,** which will be explained by the Gemara.

and the prohibition regarding their daughters is itself rooted in something referred to as *davar acher*, a rabbinic euphemism. According to *Rashi*, the first *davar acher* refers to *avodah zarah* — the concern is not so much the daughters themselves as the idolatrous practices to which proximity with them might lead.

### Davar Acher Reason Unkno…

and the *avodah zarah* concern itself rests on some further reason, a second *davar acher* whose precise referent remains opaque. As *Rashi* notes, this second *davar acher* will be explained later in the *gemara*. The term *davar acher* is a well-known Talmudic euphemism that can refer variously to pork, sexual immorality, *avodah zarah*, *tzara'at*, or other sensitive matters depending on context.

## Intermarriage Prohibitio…

### Seven Nations Exception

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בְּנוֹתֵיהֶן, דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא הִיא, דִּכְתִיב: ״לֹא תִתְחַתֵּן בָּם״, דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא שִׁבְעָה גּוֹיִם, אֲבָל שְׁאָר אוּמּוֹת — לָא, וַאֲתוֹ אִינְהוּ וּגְזוּר אֲפִילּוּ דִּשְׁאָר אוּמּוֹת.
>
> **Translation:** It was stated that the prohibition against marrying the daughters of gentiles was decreed on account of idolatry. The Gemara raises an objection: But the prohibition against marrying **their daughters is** prescribed **by Torah law, as it is written: "Neither shall you make marriages with them"** (Deuteronomy 7:3). The Gemara explains: **By Torah law** intermarriage is prohibited only with the **seven** Canaanite **nations, but** intermarriage with **the other nations** of the world is **not** prohibited, **and** the students of Shammai and Hillel **came and decreed** that intermarriage is prohibited **even** with **the other nations.**

The *gemara* probes the claim that the prohibition against non-Jewish daughters is rabbinic: isn't it a biblical prohibition? The verse *lo titchaten bam* prohibits intermarriage with the Canaanite nations. The answer is that the Torah's explicit prohibition against intermarriage applies specifically to the seven Canaanite nations enumerated in the text. With respect to other idolaters, there is no explicit *de-oraita* prohibition against a Jewish man marrying an idolatrous woman. It was the rabbis who came and extended the prohibition to *shear ovdei kochavim*, all idolaters.

### Shimon ben Yochai: All I…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּלְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן יוֹחַי, דְּאָמַר: ״כִּי יָסִיר אֶת בִּנְךָ מֵאַחֲרַי״ לְרַבּוֹת כׇּל הַמְּסִירוֹת, מַאי אִיכָּא לְמֵימַר? אֶלָּא דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא אִישׁוּת דֶּרֶךְ חַתְנוּת, וַאֲתוֹ אִינְהוּ גְּזוּר אֲפִילּוּ דֶּרֶךְ זְנוּת.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And according to** the opinion of **Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai, who says** that the subsequent verse: **"For he will turn away your son from following Me"** (Deuteronomy 7:4) serves **to include all who turn away** one's son from God, i.e., all gentiles, **what is there to say? Rather, by Torah law** only **sexual relations by way of marriage** are prohibited, **and they came** and **decreed** that sexual relations are prohibited **even by way of licentiousness.**

One might challenge this on the basis of Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai's position: the verse "for she will turn your son away from Me" — teaches that the underlying concern is the risk of religious seduction, and Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai holds that *l'rabot kol ha-masirot*, this includes any idolatrous woman, since the same risk exists with all of them. On his reading, the *de-oraita* prohibition would apply universally, not only to the seven nations.

### Chatnut vs. Znut

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** זְנוּת נָמֵי בְּבֵית דִּינוֹ שֶׁל שֵׁם גָּזְרוּ, דִּכְתִיב: ״וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוּדָה הוֹצִיאוּהָ וְתִשָּׂרֵף״!
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises an objection: **Licentious** sexual intercourse was **also** prohibited earlier, as **they decreed** a prohibition in this regard **in the court of Shem, as it is written:** "It was told to Judah, saying: Tamar your daughter-in-law has played the harlot; and moreover, behold, she is with child by harlotry. **And Judah said: Bring her forth, and let her be burned"** (Genesis 38:24).

The *gemara* resolves the difficulty: the biblical prohibition, even on Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai's reading, applies to formal marriage, to the long-term relationship of *chatnut*, because it is in such a relationship that the risk of religious seduction is most acute. The later rabbis then came and extended the prohibition: they prohibited even casual sexual relations between a Jewish man and a non-Jewish woman, not only marriage, on the ground that even such encounters fall under the concern of social and religious contamination.

### Asymmetric Prohibition

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא — גּוֹי הַבָּא עַל בַּת יִשְׂרָאֵל, דְּמָשְׁכָה בָּתְרֵיהּ; אֲבָל יִשְׂרָאֵל הַבָּא עַל הַגּוֹיָה — לָא, וַאֲתוֹ אִינְהוּ גְּזוּר אֲפִילּוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל הַבָּא עַל הַגּוֹיָה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: **Rather,** the prohibition prescribed **by Torah law** applies to the case of **a gentile who engaged in intercourse with a Jewish woman, as she is drawn after him** toward idolatry, **but** the case of **a Jew who engaged in intercourse with a gentile woman** is **not** included in the prohibition by Torah law. **And** the students of Shammai and Hillel **came** and **decreed** that the prohibition applies **even** to **a Jew who engaged in intercourse with a gentile woman.**

One might then ask: but was not *znut* with a non-Jewish woman already prohibited by *Beit Dino shel Shem* — the *beit din* of Shem, which Rav Elchanan Wasserman discusses in his *Kuntras Divrei Sofrim* as the source of universal rabbinic-style enactments in the wake of the Flood? The *gemara* distinguishes: the primary biblical concern is a non-Jewish man who takes a Jewish woman into his environment, drawing her after him into his culture and away from Torah. A Jewish man going to a non-Jewish woman presents a different profile of risk. That is the asymmetry that underlies the *de-oraita* formulation.

The rabbinic extension then fills in the gap: the *de-rabbanan* decree addresses the case of a Jewish man with a non-Jewish woman as well. However, the *gemara* then notes that the prohibition against a Jewish man having relations with a non-Jewish woman is in fact *halacha le-Moshe mi-Sinai*, derived from the episode of Pinchas — anyone who witnesses the act may take the law into his own hands and kill the transgressor. This applies *de-oraita* only *be-farhesya*, in public, like the case of Zimri, who committed the act openly before *Ohel Moed*, in full view of Moshe and the *zekenim*. The *de-rabbanan* then extends the prohibition to *bitzina*, in private, as will be seen.

## Yichud Decree Progression

### Chashmonaim: Biah Decree

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי אֲתָא רַב דִּימִי אָמַר: בֵּית דִּין שֶׁל חַשְׁמוֹנַאי גָּזְרוּ, יִשְׂרָאֵל הַבָּא עַל הַגּוֹיָה חַיָּיב מִשּׁוּם נשג״א.
>
> **Translation:** **As when Rav Dimi came** from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia, he **said: The court of the Hasmoneans decreed** that **a Jew who engaged in intercourse with a gentile woman bears** liability **for** transgressing four prohibitions, represented by the mnemonic: ***Nun*, *shin*, *gimmel*, *alef*.** These letters stands for: Menstruating woman [*nidda*], maidservant [*shifḥa*], gentile [*goya*], and married woman [*eshet ish*].

The *gemara* traces a layered historical progression of prohibitions. *Beit dino shel Chashmonai* decreed against the act of *biah* — sexual relations — between a Jewish man and a non-Jewish woman, formulating the violation as *nashga*: the act constitutes a compound rabbinic transgression combining the prohibitions of *niddah*, *shifcha* (a slave woman), *goya* (a non-Jewish woman), and *eshet ish* (a married woman), even if she is not in fact married. *Ravin*, when he arrived from *Eretz Yisrael*, reported a slightly variant formulation of *nashga*: *niddah, shifcha, goya,* and *zonah* — a promiscuous woman — rather than *eshet ish*, on the reasoning that she may not be married but certainly falls into the category of *znut*.

### Biah vs. Yichud

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי גָּזְרוּ בֵּית דִּינוֹ שֶׁל חַשְׁמוֹנַאי בִּיאָה, אֲבָל יִיחוּד לָא
>
> **Translation:** **When the court of the Hasmoneans decreed,** they prohibited only **sexual intercourse, but** with regard to **seclusion** with a gentile woman, **no,** they did not prohibit that.

The *gemara* emphasizes the precise scope of the Hasmonean decree: the Hasmonean court prohibited the act of *biah* itself, but did not extend the prohibition to *yichud* — seclusion — with a non-Jewish woman.

### David: Yichud with Jewis…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַאֲתוֹ אִינְהוּ גְּזוּר אֲפִילּוּ יִיחוּד. יִיחוּד נָמֵי בֵּית דִּינוֹ שֶׁל דָּוִד גָּזְרוּ!
>
> **Translation:** **And** the students of Shammai and Hillel **came** and **decreed** that **even seclusion** with a gentile woman is prohibited. The Gemara raises an objection: **Seclusion** was **also** prohibited earlier, as **the court of** King **David decreed** that with regard to this matter.

The prohibition of *yichud* with a Jewish woman, by contrast, was decreed by *beit dino shel David*. *Rav Yehuda* teaches that at the time of the rape of Tamar by Amnon, David's court enacted the decree against *yichud*. That decree, however, addressed seclusion between a Jewish man and a Jewish woman — *yichud de-bat Yisrael* — prompted by the specific violation that gave rise to the Tamar tragedy.

### Shammai/Hillel: Yichud E…

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּאָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: בְּאוֹתָה שָׁעָה גָּזְרוּ עַל יִיחוּד. אָמְרִי: הָתָם יִיחוּד דְּבַת יִשְׂרָאֵל, אֲבָל יִיחוּד דְּגוֹיָה — לָא, וַאֲתוֹ אִינְהוּ גְּזַרוּ אֲפִילּוּ אַיִּיחוּד דְּגוֹיָה.
>
> **Translation:** **As Rav Yehuda says: At that time,** after the incident involving Amnon and Tamar (see II Samuel 13:1–19), **they decreed with regard to seclusion.** The Sages **said** in response to the objection: **There,** in David's court, **seclusion with a Jewish woman** was prohibited, **but seclusion with a gentile woman** was **not** prohibited. **And** the students of Shammai and Hillel **came** and **decreed** a prohibition **even with regard to seclusion with a gentile woman.**

the students of *Beit Shammai* and *Beit Hillel* later extended the *yichud* prohibition to cover seclusion with a non-Jewish woman as well. David's court had not addressed this scenario, presumably because the concern at that stage was focused on violations within the Jewish community.

## Yichud Status & Drashah

### Yichud De'oraisa Source

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יִיחוּד דְּבַת יִשְׂרָאֵל דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא הִיא! דְּאָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן יְהוֹצָדָק: רֶמֶז לְיִיחוּד מִן הַתּוֹרָה מִנַּיִן?
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises yet another difficulty: **Seclusion with a Jewish woman is** prohibited **by Torah law, as Rabbi Yoḥanan says in the name of Rabbi Shimon ben Yehotzadak: Where is there an allusion in the Torah to** the prohibition against **seclusion?**

With the prohibition of *yichud* with a Jewish woman now attributed to David's *beit din*, a question arises: is *yichud de-oraita*? The *gemara* suggests it is, or at minimum has a *de-oraita* basis — though the source is characterized only as a *remez*, a hint from the Torah rather than an explicit verse.

### Deuteronomy Remez

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״כִּי יְסִיתְךָ אָחִיךָ בֶן אִמֶּךָ״, וְכִי בֶּן אֵם מֵסִית, בֶּן אָב אֵינוֹ מֵסִית? אֶלָּא, בֵּן מִתְיַיחֵד עִם אִמּוֹ, וְאֵין אַחֵר מִתְיַיחֵד עִם כׇּל עֲרָיוֹת שֶׁבַּתּוֹרָה!
>
> **Translation:** **As it is stated: "If your brother, the son of your mother, entices you"** (Deuteronomy 13:7). **And does only** a half brother who is **the son of a mother entice** one to sin, whereas **the son of a father does not entice? Rather,** there is a greater concern that a maternal half brother might entice one to sin, as **a son secludes himself with his mother, and no other may seclude himself with any** of **those with whom relations are forbidden by the Torah.**

*Rabbi Yochanan* in the name of *Rabbi Shimon ben Yehotzadak* asks: where in the Torah is there a hint to the prohibition of *yichud*? The verse cited is from the section dealing with *avodah zarah*: "if your brother, the son of your mother, entices you." The question is posed: does only a maternal brother entice, but not a paternal brother? The *gemara* explains: a son may be secluded with his mother, because that relationship is not subject to the *yichud* concern.

### Son-Mother Exception

but no one else may be secluded with any of the *arayot* listed in the Torah. The verse specifies *ben imecha*, the son of your mother, because that is the one familial dyad for which *yichud* is permitted — a son and his mother. All other *arayot* are subject to the *yichud* prohibition. This *remez* from the Torah constitutes the basis for treating *yichud* with a Jewish woman as a *de-oraita* concern, or at least as something with a strong scriptural anchor, even though the formal enactment is attributed to David's court.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יִיחוּד דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא דְּאֵשֶׁת אִישׁ, וַאֲתָא דָּוִד וּגְזַר אֲפִילּוּ אַיִּיחוּד דִּפְנוּיָה, וַאֲתוֹ תַּלְמִידֵי בֵּית שַׁמַּאי וּבֵית הִלֵּל גְּזוּר אֲפִילּוּ אַיִּיחוּד דְּגוֹיָה.
>
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains: The prohibition against **seclusion** prescribed **by Torah law** applies specifically to **a married woman, and David came and decreed** a prohibition **even with regard to seclusion with an unmarried woman. And** later **the students of Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel came** and **decreed even with regard to seclusion with a gentile woman.**
