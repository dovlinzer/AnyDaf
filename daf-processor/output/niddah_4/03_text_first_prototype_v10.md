## Box & Rodent Case



### Box Setup

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** (שׁוּלַיִם, בְּדוּקִין, מְכוּסִּין בְּזָוִית — סִימָן). אִיתְּמַר: קוּפָּה שֶׁנִּשְׁתַּמְּשׁוּ בָּהּ טְהָרוֹת בְּזָוִית זוֹ, וְנִמְצָא שֶׁרֶץ בְּזָוִית אַחֶרֶת — חִזְקִיָּה אָמַר: טְהָרוֹת הָרִאשׁוֹנוֹת טְהוֹרוֹת, רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: טְהָרוֹת הָרִאשׁוֹנוֹת טְמֵאוֹת. וְהָא (בֵּית) שַׁמַּאי וְהִלֵּל מוֹדוּ בְּקוּפָּה דִּטְהָרוֹת הָרִאשׁוֹנוֹת טְמֵאוֹת!
> **Translation:** The Gemara provides **a mnemonic** for the ensuing discussions: **A base; examined; covered; in a corner.** It **was stated:** If one has **a basket** that **was used** as a container for holding **ritually pure** produce, and those items were placed **in** one **corner** of the basket, **and** subsequently the carcass of **a creeping animal was found in a different corner** of the basket, **Ḥizkiyya says:** That produce **initially** considered **ritually pure** is still **pure. Rabbi Yoḥanan says:** The produce **initially** considered **ritually pure** is now retroactively deemed **impure.** The Gemara asks: **But** how can Ḥizkiyya rule that it is pure? After all, don’t **Shammai and Hillel agree in** the case of the **basket that** the produce **initially** considered **pure** is now retroactively **impure?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי מוֹדוּ שַׁמַּאי וְהִלֵּל בְּקוּפָּה שֶׁיֵּשׁ לָהּ שׁוּלַיִם, כִּי פְּלִיגִי חִזְקִיָּה וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בְּקוּפָּה שֶׁאֵין לָהּ שׁוּלַיִם.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: **When** do **Shammai and Hillel agree** that all the produce is retroactively impure? **With regard to a basket that has a base.** In such a case, the carcass of the creeping animal might have previously been in the basket and it went unnoticed when one removed the pure produce items. **When Ḥizkiyya and Rabbi Yoḥanan disagree,** it is **with regard to a basket that has no base,** i.e., both ends were open and the basket was used while it was lying on its side. In that situation, the contents of the basket are emptied by raising one end, which causes all items inside to fall out. Consequently, any creeping animal that was found subsequently must have entered the basket only after that produce was removed, which is why Ḥizkiyya rules that the produce remains pure.

The Gemara on the bottom of 3b presents an Amoraic dispute that echoes a case already introduced in the discussion of Hillel and Shammai. The case involves a *kuppa* — a box or basket — that was used to store *tahoros*, ritually pure food, in one section or corner. Subsequently, after the contents were removed or the box emptied, a dead *sheretz* (rodent) was discovered in a different corner. The central question is whether we must be concerned that this *sheretz* was already present when the *tahoros* were being handled, retroactively rendering them *tamei*, or whether we may assume the *sheretz* arrived only afterward.

### Hillel & Shammai

The Gemara first establishes a point of agreement: *beshammai veHillel modu bekuppa sheyesh lah shulayim* — both Shammai and Hillel concur that when the box has a floor (*shulayim*), we must be concerned that the *sheretz* was there earlier and that the prior *tahoros* are retroactively *tamei*. This was, in fact, the very case Hillel cited when he pressed Shammai during their famous exchange. Shammai had accepted the argument in principle — a *sheretz* found in a floored box may well have been resting there unnoticed throughout — but distinguished it from the case of the woman by noting that a woman has no such "floor" to retain the blood. So Shammai and Hillel agreed on the box with a floor; what remains is to understand where Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan enter the picture.

### Chizkiyah vs. Yochanan

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֵין לָהּ שׁוּלַיִם, מַאי טַעְמָא דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן? אֵין לָהּ שׁוּלַיִם, וְיֵשׁ לָהּ אֳוגָנִים.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: If this is referring to a case where the basket **has no base, what is the reasoning of Rabbi Yoḥanan** for ruling that the produce is ritually impure? The Gemara answers: The basket **has no base but it** does **have** an inward-curved **rim.** Therefore, it is possible that the carcass of a small creeping animal might have been held back by the rim and remained inside the basket even after it was turned over. Rabbi Yoḥanan rules that the produce is retroactively impure, due to the possibility that the creeping animal might have been in the basket previously together with the initially pure produce.

*Ki pligi Chizkiya veRebbe Yochanan bekuppa she'ein lah shulayim* — their dispute concerns a box without a floor. The practical implication of such a configuration is that in order to retrieve the contents, one would have to empty or pour everything out completely. In such a case, the logic runs: had the *sheretz* been present all along, it would have come out when the box was fully emptied. Since it was not seen then, one could reasonably conclude it only arrived later. Chizkiyah therefore rules that the prior *tahoros* are *tahor* — we need not be concerned retroactively.

The Gemara immediately asks: *ein lah shulayim mai ta'amei deRebbe Yochanan?* If there is no floor and everything necessarily empties out, why would Rabbi Yochanan still be concerned that the *sheretz* was there earlier? The answer: *ein lah shulayim veyesh lah oznayim* — the box lacks a floor but possesses a lip (*oznayim*), a rim curled inward. It is therefore possible that when the contents were poured out, the *sheretz* became lodged in that lip, unnoticed, and later fell back to the bottom of the box when it was righted. Rabbi Yochanan holds that this possibility is sufficient to generate retroactive concern; Chizkiyah disagrees.

### Barrel & Buckets

The Gemara now cites a *baraita* that makes this scenario considerably clearer. *Ha-medaleh asar dli-im mayim be-zeh achar zeh* — one who draws water from a well using successive buckets, each poured into a large barrel, ten times in sequence. When filling bucket number ten, a dead *sheretz* is discovered inside that bucket. The bucket itself is obviously *tamei*. But what of the water already collected in the barrel? *Ve-kulan tehorin* — all of it is *tahor*, because the logic is straightforward: had the *sheretz* been present in an earlier bucket, it would have been poured into the barrel along with the water. The fact that it is found still sitting in the bucket demonstrates it could not have been there during earlier pours.

### Reish Lakish

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָתַנְיָא: הַמַּדְלֶה עֲשָׂרָה דְּלָיִים מַיִם בְּזֶה אַחַר זֶה, וְנִמְצָא שֶׁרֶץ בְּאֶחָד מֵהֶן — הוּא טָמֵא, וְכוּלָּן טְהוֹרִין. וְאָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ מִשּׁוּם רַבִּי יַנַּאי: לֹא שָׁנוּ אֶלָּא שֶׁאֵין לָהּ אֳוגָנִים, אֲבָל יֵשׁ לָהּ אֳוגָנִים — כּוּלָּן טְמֵאִין.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **But isn’t it taught** in a *baraita*: With regard to **one who draws ten pails of water one after another,** i.e., he draws out water in one pail and from it he fills another ten pails with water, **and** the carcass of **a creeping animal is found in one of them, that** pail of water **is ritually impure but all** the rest **of them** are **pure. And Reish Lakish says in the name of Rabbi Yannai:** This *halakha* was **taught only** in a case **where** the pail **has no** inward-curved **rim.** Consequently, its contents are entirely emptied each time it is overturned, which means that the creeping animal must have entered only this pail. **But** if the pail **has a rim, all of** the water that had been drawn is **impure,** as the creeping animal might have entered the pail earlier and the rim held it back each time the pail was overturned.

Reish Lakish, citing Rabbi Yannai, introduces a qualification: *lo shanu ela she-ein lo oznayim* — the ruling that everything is *tahor* applies only if the bucket lacks a lip. *Aval yesh lo oznayim kulan tme'in* — but if the bucket has an inward-curling lip, then one must be concerned that all the water drawn from previous buckets is *tamei*, since the *sheretz* may have been caught in the lip all along and not poured into the barrel each time.

This naturally raises a tension: Chizkiyah, in the case of the box with a lip, ruled *tahor*, whereas Rabbi Yannai (as cited by Reish Lakish) in the case of a bucket with a lip rules *tamei*. Perhaps, the Gemara suggests, they simply disagree.

### Water vs. Fruit

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֵימָא חִזְקִיָּה לֵית לֵיהּ דְּרַבִּי יַנַּאי? מַיָּא שָׁרְקִי, פֵּירֵי לָא שָׁרְקִי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains the difficulty: **Shall we say** that **Ḥizkiyya,** who is not concerned that the rim of the basket might have held back the creeping animal, does **not agree** with the opinion **of Rabbi Yannai?** The Gemara answers that there is a difference between the two cases: **Water is slippery,** whereas **produce is not slippery.** In order to pour water out of the pail it is not necessary to turn it over completely. Therefore, the rim might hold back a creeping animal. By contrast, a basket must have one end fully raised in order to empty out all the produce it contains. In such a case Rabbi Yannai would agree that nothing could have been held back, and therefore the emptied produce remains pure.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִי נָמֵי: מַיָּא לָא קָפֵיד עֲלַיְיהוּ, פֵּירֵי קָפֵיד עֲלַיְיהוּ.
> **Translation:** Alternatively, there is a different reason why one does not overturn a pail of water entirely: In the case of **water, one is not particular about it,** i.e., he does not mind if some is left over. Therefore he does not overturn the pail completely. By contrast, in the case of **produce one is particular about it,** so he raises one end of the basket entirely to ensure that every last piece of produce is emptied. Consequently, in the case of produce Rabbi Yannai would agree that even if the basket has a rim, all the produce remains pure.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי בָּעֵית אֵימָא: כִּי מוֹדוּ שַׁמַּאי וְהִלֵּל בְּקוּפָּה שֶׁאֵינָהּ בְּדוּקָה,
> **Translation:** As an alternative to its previous resolution that Ḥizkiyya is discussing the case of a rimmed receptacle, the Gemara suggests another resolution of the apparent contradiction between Ḥizkiyya’s ruling and the opinions of Hillel and Shammai. **And if you wish, say: When** do **Shammai and Hillel agree? In** the case of **a basket that was not examined.** Since he did not examine the basket when he placed the pure produce there, a creeping animal might have already been inside. Consequently, they agree that the produce does not retain its presumptive state of purity but is considered retroactively impure.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כִּי פְּלִיגִי חִזְקִיָּה וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בְּקוּפָּה בְּדוּקָה, מָר סָבַר: הָא בַּדְקַהּ, וּמָר סָבַר: אֵימוֹר עִם סִילּוּק יָדוֹ נָפַל.
> **Translation:** **When Ḥizkiyya and Rabbi Yoḥanan disagree,** it is **with regard to a basket** that was **examined.** One **Sage,** Ḥizkiyya, **holds** that **since** it **was examined** before the produce was placed inside and was found to be clean of creeping animals, it is reasonable to assume that the creeping animal entered only after the ritually pure produce was removed. **And** one **Sage,** Rabbi Yoḥanan, **holds** that one can **say** that it is possible that **when he removed his hand** after feeling around to examine the basket, the creeping animal **fell** in.

The Gemara attempts to distinguish the two cases rather than leave them in simple disagreement. *Maya sharki peiri lo sharki* — water slides; solid contents do not. Water flows out with minimal tilting; a quick pour is sufficient to empty a bucket of water and a *sheretz* lodged in the lip might plausibly remain. By contrast, emptying a box of grain, flour, or fruit requires vigorous shaking and a far more thorough inversion, making it far less likely that something caught in a lip would remain behind. Thus Chizkiyah, dealing with solid contents, is not concerned that the *sheretz* was caught in the lip, whereas Rabbi Yannai's ruling about water is entirely consistent.

An alternative explanation is offered as well: *maya lo kapdi aleihu peiri kapdi aleihu* — with water, one is not meticulous about getting out every last drop, so one might well leave a small amount behind, and with it a *sheretz* that happened to be there. With solid food, however, one is careful to extract everything and shakes the container accordingly, making it highly unlikely anything was left behind. The two explanations complement one another and successfully reconcile Chizkiyah's ruling with Rabbi Yannai's.

Having reconciled those two cases, the Gemara now turns to distinguishing when Hillel and Shammai agree from when Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan debate. The critical variable, as the Gemara will spell out in full, is whether the box was inspected before use — since the presence or absence of an established *status quo* determines whether there is any baseline of *taharah* to reason from in the first place.

## Box Applied to Woman



### Unchecked Box

Having analyzed the box-and-rodent case in its various dimensions, the Gemara turns to another distinction that connects directly to the case of the woman. *Keivan d'shchichi bah damim k'she'einah bedukah damya* — because menstruation is prevalent in women, her status is comparable to that of a box that has not been checked before use. This comparison is made in the context of establishing when Hillel and Shammai agree versus when Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan debate. According to one answer offered by the Gemara, Hillel and Shammai agree that the prior *tahoros* are *tamei* specifically when the box was not inspected before use — for in that case there was never a confirmed *status quo* of being free from *sheretz*. Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan debate the case when the box *was* checked (*bekuppah bedukah*) prior to use.

### Chazakah Liable Change

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא דּוּמְיָא דְּאִשָּׁה קָתָנֵי, וְאִשָּׁה בְּדוּקָה הִיא! כֵּיוָן דִּשְׁכִיחִי בָּהּ דָּמִים — כְּשֶׁאֵינָהּ בְּדוּקָה דָּמְיָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But isn’t** the case of a basket **taught** as being **similar to** the case of a menstruating **woman?** Hillel had cited the case of the basket as a difficulty with regard to Shammai’s opinion in the case of a menstruating woman. **And** since **a woman is** considered fully **examined,** since she examines herself with examination cloths twice a day, the other case must also be referring to a basket that had been examined. The Gemara answers: **Since blood is commonly found** flowing from **her,** as women regularly experience menstrual flows, it is **considered as** though she were **not examined.**

This brings us back to the woman, and the Gemara's formulation is striking. When Hillel and Shammai compared their box case to the case of a woman who discovered blood, the Gemara asks: *v'ha'ishah bedukah hi* — is a woman not like the checked box? She was known to be *tehorah* at some prior point; she has an established *status quo*. So why should she be treated like an unchecked box?

The answer: *keivan d'shchichi bah damim k'she'einah bedukah damya* — since blood is prevalent in a woman, since menstruation is a regular, recurring reality, the prior *status quo* of *taharah* is rendered conceptually unstable. In more formal terms, hers is a *chazakah ha'asuyah l'hishtanos* — a *chazakah*, a status quo, that is by its very nature in the process of changing, likely to shift at any time. A *chazakah* only reliably carries forward into the future and backwards to explain the past when the status it reflects is a stable one — when, in other words, there is no particular reason to expect it to change. The woman's status does not enjoy that stability, and therefore even though she is in a sense "checked," her case is more akin to the unchecked box for purposes of this analysis.

It should be noted, however, that the analogy is admittedly imprecise. Knowing a status is stable and confirmed is not exactly the same thing as never having confirmed the status at all — these are distinct epistemic situations. What the Gemara is claiming is that they are at least roughly analogous in the relevant halachic sense, not that they are identical. One might also observe that this characterization of the woman's status as perpetually liable to change is itself not uniformly accurate across the entire monthly cycle — there are periods during which her status is, if anything, moving in the opposite direction. The Gemara does not address this, and the point stands as a genuine difficulty in the comparison.

It is worth pausing here to note that this analysis operates not on the basis of raw probability — *rov* is not mentioned — but on the basis of formal rules about the reliability of a *chazakah*. A standard *chazakah* allows one to say: "What is true today was likely true yesterday, and the day before." A *chazakah ha'asuyah l'hishtanos* cannot sustain that inference with the same force. And this remains a purely formal principle, analogous in a certain sense to what philosophers call the problem of induction — the inference that the future will resemble the past has no strictly logical foundation, yet it is the basis on which we conduct our lives, and the *chazakah* functions in precisely that manner as a halachic instrument.

### Covered Box

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: כִּי מוֹדוּ שַׁמַּאי וְהִלֵּל בְּקוּפָּה שֶׁאֵינָהּ מְכוּסָּה, כִּי פְּלִיגִי חִזְקִיָּה וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בְּקוּפָּה מְכוּסָּה. מְכוּסָּה — הֵיכִי נְפַל? כְּגוֹן שֶׁתַּשְׁמִישָׁהּ עַל יְדֵי כִּסּוּי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests another resolution of the apparent contradiction between the ruling of Ḥizkiyya and the opinions of Hillel and Shammai. **And if you wish, say: When Shammai and Hillel agree,** it is **with regard to a basket that is not covered. When** do **Ḥizkiyya and Rabbi Yoḥanan disagree? With regard to a basket** that is **covered.** The Gemara asks: If the basket **is covered, how did** the creeping animal **fall** inside? The Gemara answers: **For example, if** the basket **is used by** removing its **lid.** Ḥizkiyya holds that the creeping animal must have fallen in after the produce was removed, because as long as the produce was inside one would be careful not to allow anything else inside. Rabbi Yoḥanan is concerned that perhaps while the basket was uncovered a creeping animal could have fallen inside without one noticing.

The Gemara offers yet another way of distinguishing the case where Hillel and Shammai agree from the case where Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan debate. *Ki modu Shammai veHillel bekuppa she'einah mechusah* — Hillel and Shammai agree that the prior *tahoros* are *tamei* when the box was not covered, meaning there was no active protection against a *sheretz* entering. *Pligi Chizkiya veRebbe Yochanan bekuppa mechusah* — Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan debate the case of a box that was used with a cover; it is not sealed absolutely at all times, but when not actively in use, it is kept closed.

This covered box represents the stronger end of the *chazakah* spectrum. Rather than a mere passive *status quo* — "as far as we know, no *sheretz* has entered" — the covered box has an active protection operating to preserve its *tahor* status. There is something positively working to maintain the state of affairs, not merely an absence of known disruption. And therefore in that case, at minimum, one might say the prior *tahoros* are *tahor*, or at least that there is room to debate. In the uncovered box, there is no such protection, and both Hillel and Shammai concede the concern is well-founded.

### Woman Covered Concept

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא דּוּמְיָא דְאִשָּׁה קָתָנֵי, וְאִשָּׁה מְכוּסָּה הִיא, כֵּיוָן דִּשְׁכִיחִי בַּהּ דָּמִים — כְּשֶׁאֵין מְכוּסָּה דָּמְיָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: But **isn’t** the case of a basket **taught** as being **similar to** the case of a menstruating **woman? And** just as a woman **is considered covered,** since no outside blood can enter her, so too in the case of a basket, it must be one where it is constantly covered. The Gemara explains: **Since blood is commonly found** flowing from **her,** as women regularly experience menstrual flows, it is **considered as** though she is **not** always **covered.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא: כִּי מוֹדוּ שַׁמַּאי וְהִלֵּל בְּזָוִית קוּפָּה, כִּי פְּלִיגִי חִזְקִיָּה וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בְּזָוִית בַּיִת, וְהָא ״קוּפָּה״ קָאָמַר!
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests another resolution. **And if you wish, say: When** do **Shammai and Hillel agree?** In a case where the produce was stored **in** the **corner** of **a basket.** By contrast, **when Ḥizkiyya and Rabbi Yoḥanan disagree,** it is in a case where the produce was stored **in** the **corner** of **a house.** The Gemara expresses puzzlement at this suggestion: **But** the Gemara on 3b explicitly **states** that they are referring to a case of **a basket.**

The Gemara now returns to the comparison: *v'hadumya d'isha katani* — but Hillel and Shammai explicitly compared their case to the woman, implying the woman corresponds to the uncovered box. Yet the Gemara observes: *d'isha mechusah hi* — a woman is "covered."

Rashi interprets this as a physical observation: just as a *sheretz* cannot enter a box from outside if the box is covered, nothing foreign enters the woman's body. But Tosafot finds this reading untenable, since a woman's *tumah* arises from her own blood, not from any external contamination. Tosafot therefore explains "covered" in terms of the position of Shammai himself: the blood becomes *tamei* only once it crosses the cervix into the vaginal canal, and the cervix is in a sense "closed," functioning like a cover. More broadly, this maps onto Shammai's earlier argument that a woman senses when she is beginning to menstruate, and that sensory awareness serves as a kind of protection of her prior status — analogous to the cover that prevents a *sheretz* from entering undetected.

If so, Hillel, who does not accept Shammai's experiential argument, would still agree that the box case is *tamei*, and that the same applies to the woman — and that the "covered" quality only appears to protect her status without actually doing so. Why? *Keivan d'shchichi bah damim ke'eino mechuso damya* — because menstruation is so common, the theoretical "coverage" is counterbalanced and overridden by the statistical reality of frequent bleeding. The idea that she will sense the onset provides some analogy to a covered box, but the prevalence of menstruation neutralizes that advantage, rendering her status effectively equivalent to an uncovered box — and in an uncovered box, both Hillel and Shammai agree that the prior *tahoros* are *tamei*.

### Woman Objectified

Finally, the Gemara consolidates the comparison by suggesting that a woman is *k'kuppa she'yesh lah sheretz bifnim* — like a box with a dead *sheretz* inside of it. This is a point worth naming directly. The entire sustained discussion of this daf has involved objectifying the case of the woman — treating her as though she were a *mikvah* with a leak, a barrel of wine turning to vinegar, or a box containing a *sheretz*. These comparisons are not made with any pejorative intent as a matter of Talmudic method; they arise naturally from the process of abstraction, of identifying the formal halachic structure underlying a case and then comparing it to other cases with the same structure. Nevertheless, once those comparisons are made, the associations exist and carry their own weight, and that remains a genuine challenge that should be acknowledged.

## Place-to-Place Transfer



### Box Moved Rooms

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכִי קָאָמַר: קוּפָּה שֶׁנִּשְׁתַּמְּשׁוּ בָּהּ טְהָרוֹת בְּזָוִית בַּיִת זוֹ, וְטִלְטְלוּהָ בְּזָוִית אַחֶרֶת, וְנִמְצָא שֶׁרֶץ בְּזָוִית אַחֶרֶת. חִזְקִיָּה סָבַר: לָא מַחְזְקִינַן טוּמְאָה מִמָּקוֹם לְמָקוֹם, וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן סָבַר: מַחְזְקִינַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains that **this** is what the Gemara on 3b **is saying:** If one has **a basket that was used** as a container for **ritually pure** produce **in this corner** of the **house, and** after the produce was removed **it was** subsequently **carried to another corner, and** the carcass of **a creeping animal was found** in the basket while it was **in** that **other corner, Ḥizkiyya holds:** The produce remains ritually pure, as **we do not presume** that **ritual impurity** moved **from place to place.** In other words, the impure creeping animal is not assumed to have moved from the first corner where the produce was kept. Instead, it fell inside while the basket was in the second corner, and therefore the produce that it previously contained remains pure. **And Rabbi Yoḥanan holds:** The produce is retroactively considered impure, as **we** do **presume** that ritual impurity, i.e., the carcass of the creeping animal in the basket, moved from place to place.

The Gemara now pivots to a different variant of the box-and-*sheretz* case. *Kuppa she'nishtamshu bah taharos be'zavit bayit zu* — a box was being used to handle *tahoros* in one corner of a house — say, the living room. *Ve'tilteluha be'zavit acheres* — it was then moved to a different corner, or to another room entirely, the bedroom. *Ve'nimtza sheretz be'zavit acheres* — and now, with the box sitting in the bedroom, a dead *sheretz* is discovered inside it. The question is whether the fact that we find the *sheretz* in the box now, in the bedroom, gives rise to retroactive concern about what happened earlier when the box was in the living room with the *tahoros*.

### Chizkiyah No Concern

Chizkiyah rules: *lo machzikinan tuma mi'makom le'makom* — we do not establish, or apply, the status of *tumah* from one location to another. Since the *sheretz* is found in the box now in the bedroom, we cannot project that status backward to when the box was in the living room. The prior use of the *tahoros* remains *tahor*.

### Yochanan Applies

Rabbi Yochanan rules: *machzikinan* — we do apply the status from one place to another. The fact that we find the *sheretz* in the box now is sufficient cause to be concerned that it was present earlier, regardless of which room the box happened to be in at the time. The physical relocation of the box does not break the inferential link between the current status and the prior one.

It is worth emphasizing the formal character of this dispute. It is not purely a matter of probability — one might construct scenarios in which rodents are more prevalent in the bedroom than the living room, or vice versa, and that would affect the likelihood of the *sheretz* having been present in either location. But the dispute of Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan is not framed in those terms. It is a formal question: can the current status — *sheretz* in the box, in the bedroom — be applied retroactively to characterize what occurred in a different place? That is a rule of status, not a statistical inference.

### Dead Body Baraita

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִי מַחְזְקִינַן? וְהָתְנַן: נָגַע בְּאֶחָד בַּלַּיְלָה, וְאֵינוֹ יוֹדֵעַ אִם חַי אִם מֵת, וּלְמָחָר הִשְׁכִּים וּמְצָאוֹ מֵת — רַבִּי מֵאִיר מְטַהֵר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks a question with regard to the opinion of Rabbi Yoḥanan: **And do we presume** that ritual impurity moved from place to place? **But didn’t we learn** in a mishna (*Teharot* 5:7): If someone **touched one** other person **at night, and he does not know whether** the person he touched was **alive or dead, and on the following day he arose and found him dead,** and he is uncertain whether or not he contracted ritual impurity from contact with a corpse, **Rabbi Meir deems him** ritually **pure.** It is assumed that the deceased was still alive until the point that it is known with certainty that he was dead.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַחֲכָמִים מְטַמְּאִין, שֶׁכׇּל הַטֻּמְאוֹת כִּשְׁעַת מְצִיאָתָן.
> **Translation:** **And the Rabbis deem him** ritually **impure, as** it is presumed that **all** ritually **impure** items had already been in the same state as they were **at the time they were discovered.** Just as the deceased was found dead in the morning, so too, it is presumed that he was dead when he was touched in the middle of the night.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתָנֵי עֲלַהּ: כִּשְׁעַת מְצִיאָתָן, וּבִמְקוֹם מְצִיאָתָן!
> **Translation:** The Gemara concludes its question: **And it is taught with regard to** this mishna: It is presumed that ritually impure items had been in the same state as they were **at the time they were discovered, but** only **in the place** in which **they were discovered.** In other words, if the corpse had been found in a different spot than he was at night, it is not presumed that he was already dead in the first spot, and the man who touched him remains ritually pure. If so, how can Rabbi Yoḥanan maintain that we presume impurity moved from place to place?

The Gemara challenges Rabbi Yochanan's position from a *Mishnah*. *Taharos nig'a be'echad ba'layla* — you touched a person at night, not knowing whether the person was alive or dead. *Ve'la'machar hishkim u'metza'o mes* — the next morning you wake up and find the person is a corpse. Were you *tamei* last night when you touched him? Rebbi Meir rules *tahor*; the *Chachamim* rule *tamei*.

The *Chachamim*'s reasoning: *shekol hatumaot k'sha'at metzi'atan* — all matters of *tumah* are determined by the status at the time of discovery. The person is found dead now; therefore we assume he was dead last night when you touched him. And attached to this ruling is an important qualification from a *baraita*: *k'sha'at metzi'atan u'vimkom metzi'atan* — not only by the condition at which they are found, but by the *place* at which they are found. If the corpse was in a different location from where the nighttime contact occurred, we would not project the *tumah* backward to the place of contact.

This *baraita* appears to directly support Chizkiyah's position — we do not apply *tumah* from one location to another — and to challenge Rabbi Yochanan. The principle *ein machzikin tumah mimakom l'makom* seems well established.

### Doubtful Status

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי תֵּימָא: הָנֵי מִילֵּי לִשְׂרוֹף, אֲבָל לִתְלוֹת — תָּלֵינַן? וּמִי תָּלֵינַן?!
> **Translation:** **And if you would say** in response: **This statement,** that impurity is presumed only in the place in which it was discovered, applies specifically with regard to definite impure status, i.e., **to burn** *teruma*, **but** with regard to uncertain impurity, i.e., **to suspend** the status of *teruma*, **we** do in fact **suspend** its status and it may be neither burned nor eaten; is this distinction correct? **Do we** in fact **suspend** the status of ritually pure items in such a case, due to the concern that the dead man whom this individual touched might have already been dead in the first location?

The Gemara attempts a reconciliation: *v'chi teima hanei milei l'isrof* — perhaps the *baraita* means only that we would not declare the *tahoros* definitively *tamei* in a way that requires them to be burned (*l'isrof*), but *aval litlot talinan* — we would still suspend them in doubt (*litlot*). The distinction in halachic terminology between *l'isrof* and *litlot* is crucial: *tahoros* that are definitely *tamei* are burned; *tahoros* in doubtful status cannot be eaten (for they might be *tamei*) but cannot be burned either (for they might be *tahor*), and must be set aside until they decompose naturally. Perhaps Rabbi Yochanan only meant that cross-place concern raises a *doubt* — placing the *tahoros* in suspension — while the *baraita* means only that we would not declare them definitively *tamei* in a manner requiring burning.

## Needle Principle



### Moldy Needle

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָתְנַן: מַחַט שֶׁנִּמְצֵאת מְלֵאָה חֲלוּדָּה אוֹ שְׁבוּרָה — טְהוֹרָה, שֶׁכׇּל הַטֻּמְאוֹת כִּשְׁעַת מְצִיאָתָן. וְאַמַּאי? לֵימָא: הַאי מֵעִיקָּרָא מַחַט מְעַלַּיְיתָא הִיא, וְהַשְׁתָּא הוּא דְּהֶעֱלָה חֲלוּדָּה.
> **Translation:** **But didn’t we learn** in a mishna (*Teharot* 3:5): With regard to a previously impure **needle that is found** on top of *teruma* and it is **full of rust or broken,** and therefore no longer contracts or transmit ritual impurity, the *teruma* remains **pure, as** it is presumed that in **all** cases of **impurity,** the items in question had already been in the same state as they were **at the time they were discovered? But why** should that be the case? **Let us say** that **initially,** when it had fallen onto the *teruma*, **this needle was a proper,** non-rusty and unbroken, **needle,** capable of contracting and transmitting ritual impurity, **and it is** only **now that rust** had **formed** on it. The status of the *teruma* should at least be held in suspension. Rather, it is evident that the *teruma* is considered definitely pure and is not held in suspension due to the possibility that it might have become impure from the nail at a previous time or, presumably, in a previous place.

To explore the principle of *shekol hatumaot k'sha'at metzi'atan* further, the Gemara cites a *baraita* involving a needle. *Machat shenimtzeit meleiah chaludah* — a needle found completely covered in rust. A *kli* (vessel or implement) that is broken or rendered entirely unusable can no longer contract *tumah*, and if it was already in that state, it could not transmit *tumah* either. This needle, fully rusted through, is functionally destroyed. It is found resting on top of a pile of *terumah*. The question: must we be concerned that the needle was once intact, contracted *tumah* earlier, fell onto this grain, and only later became completely rusted? *Tehorah* — the grain is *tahor*.

The Gemara asks the obvious: *v'amai leima hai me'ikara machat ma'alyata hi v'hashta hu deha'alah chaludah* — why not say the needle was originally whole and functional and only now has become fully rusted? The answer lies in the principle: we go by the status at the time of discovery. The needle is found broken; therefore we assume it was in that state when it came into contact with the grain.

### Shekol HaTumaot

The explicit principle stated here is *shekol hatme'os k'sha'as m'tzi'asan* — all matters of *tumah* are determined in accordance with the condition in which they are found at the time of discovery. This applies in both directions: whether strict or lenient. If you find the person dead, you assume he was dead before and you are *tamei*. If you find the needle broken, you assume it was broken before and the *tahoros* are *tahor*. The current status is taken as definitive and applied backward without qualification — not as a *safek*, not as a suspended doubt, but as a settled halachic conclusion.

### Place of Discovery

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְעוֹד תְּנַן: מָצָא שֶׁרֶץ שָׂרוּף עַל גַּבֵּי הַזֵּיתִים, וְכֵן מַטְלִית הַמְהוּמְהָם — טָהוֹר, שֶׁכׇּל הַטֻּמְאוֹת כִּשְׁעַת מְצִיאָתָן!
> **Translation:** **And furthermore, we learned** in a mishna (*Teharot* 9:9): If one **found** the carcass of **a burned creeping animal on top of** a pile of **olives,** and that animal no longer transmits impurity as it is burned, **and similarly** if one finds **a tattered rag** of a *zav*, which likewise no longer transmits impurity, on top of a pile of olives, the olives are **pure.** The reason is **that** it is presumed in **all** cases of **impurity,** the items in question had already been in the same state as they were **at the time they were discovered.** Once again, this demonstrates that when this presumption is applied, the item is considered definitely pure, and is not held in suspension due to uncertainty.

The Gemara extends this principle: *u'vimkom metzi'atan* — and it applies specifically at the *place* of discovery. The Gemara draws the analogy: just as we take the current condition and apply it backward definitively — not merely doubtfully — the same should apply to the place. If the needle is found in one location with *terumah* underneath it, we apply the principle that the needle was broken at that location when it contacted that *terumah*, and the *terumah* is definitively *tahor*. The argument then runs: by analogy, in the case of the box moved from room to room, we should also say definitively *tahor*, not merely *safek*. This would undermine even the compromise position raised at the end of the previous section — that Rabbi Yochanan's view might amount only to placing the *tahoros* in *tiltul* (suspension) rather than declaring them definitely *tamei*.

The Gemara cites another case from the *Mishnah*: *matza sheretz saruf al gabei hazeisim* — a burnt *sheretz* found on top of a pile of olives. A burnt *sheretz* no longer causes *tumah*. *Tehorah* — the olives are *tahor*, by the same principle: found burnt, assumed to have been burnt when it contacted the olives. Similarly, a thoroughly worn and unusable garment — *matlit hamehumemet* — found on top of *tahoros* does not render them *tamei*: *shekol hatme'os k'sha'as m'tzi'asan*.

The Gemara then makes the concession: *k'sha'as m'tzi'asan bein l'kula bein l'chumra* — yes, by the *condition* at which something is found, we rule consistently, whether strictly or leniently, definitively in either direction. *Aval shelo bimkom metzi'atan* — but when the question involves a *different location* from where the item was found, it remains possible to say *misraf lo sarfinan, matla talinan* — we would not burn the *terumah* definitively, but we might still place it in suspension as a doubtful case. The principle of *shekol hatumaot k'sha'at metzi'atan* applies definitively to condition; the question of place-to-place may still admit of a middle position between definitive *tahor* and definitive *tamei*.

### Loaf & Garment

To probe further, the Gemara introduces a *baraita*: *kikar al gabei ha-daf* — a loaf of bread (*terumah*) placed on top of a shelf. Beneath the shelf lies a *tamei* garment — specifically, one referred to as *madaf*, the detailed nature of which we will return to shortly. *Af al pi she-im naflah i efshar ela im kein nag'ah* — even though if the loaf fell off the shelf by itself, it would almost certainly have contacted the *tamei* garment on the way down. Nevertheless: *tehorah* — the loaf is *tahor*. The loaf is found not on the garment but elsewhere; it did not come into contact with the *tamei* garment as far as we can establish.

This is a striking ruling. The most physically reasonable assumption — that the loaf fell, struck the garment, and rolled away — is set aside in favor of a *tahor* ruling. Why?

### Person Moved It

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי תֵּימָא, כִּשְׁעַת מְצִיאָתָן בֵּין לְקוּלָּא בֵּין לְחוּמְרָא, וּבִמְקוֹם מְצִיאָתָן, אֲבָל שֶׁלֹּא בִּמְקוֹם מְצִיאָתָן — מִשְׂרָף לָא שָׂרְפִינַן, מִתְלֵא תָּלֵינַן.
> **Translation:** **And if you would say** that there is a difference between these last cases, where the items did not move, and the case of the basket that moved, this distinction is not correct. The suggestion is that in the last cases the items are treated entirely as if they had always been as they were **at the time they were discovered, whether** this leads **to a leniency,** as in the cases of the needle and the rag, **and whether** it leads **to a stringency,** in the case of one who touched someone at night, when he is considered to be definitely impure, **but** this is the *halakha* only with regard to the **place** where **they were discovered,** i.e., if they did not move. **But** with regard to a location that is **not the place** where **they were discovered,** we do suspect that ritual impurity moved from place to place, and therefore although **we do not burn** the *teruma* in question, **we hold it in suspension.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָתְנַן: כִּכָּר עַל גַּבֵּי הַדַּף, וּמַדָּף טָמֵא מוּנָּח תַּחְתָּיו, אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁאִם נָפְלָה אִי אֶפְשָׁר אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן נָגְעָה — טְהוֹרָה, שֶׁאֲנִי אוֹמֵר: אָדָם טָהוֹר נִכְנַס לְשָׁם וּנְטָלָהּ,
> **Translation:** The Gemara refutes this suggestion: **But didn’t we learn** in a *baraita* (*Tosefta*, *Teharot* 4:3): There was **a loaf** resting **on top** of **a shelf, and** there was **an item of light impurity,** e.g., a garment of a *zav*, which transmits impurity to food but not to people or vessels, **lying underneath it,** and the loaf was later found on the ground. **Even though** the situation was such **that if** the loaf **fell** to the ground it would be **impossible** for it to have done anything **other than touch** the impure garment on its way down, nevertheless the loaf is **pure.** The reason is **that I say** that **a ritually pure man entered there and took it** off the shelf and placed it onto the ground without it touching the impure garment.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עַד שֶׁיֹּאמַר: ״בָּרִי לִי שֶׁלֹּא נִכְנַס אָדָם שָׁם״. וְאָמַר רַבִּי אֶלְעָזָר: לֹא נִצְרְכָה אֶלָּא לִמְקוֹם מִדְרוֹן!
> **Translation:** The *baraita* concludes: This ruling applies **unless someone says: It is clear to me that no person entered** there. **And Rabbi Elazar says:** This principle is **necessary only when** the top shelf is **an inclined surface.** In other words, even if it is very likely that the loaf rolled off the shelf and touched the garment on its way down to the ground, nevertheless it is assumed to be pure. This indicates that one does not presume that the loaf contracted impurity and then fell to the ground where it was found. Since such a possibility is not even entertained to the extent that the *teruma* is held in suspension, this demonstrates that in a case involving a movement from one place to another there is no presumption of ritual impurity at all.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָתָם כִּדְקָתָנֵי טַעְמָא,
> **Translation:** The Gemara explains that no proof can be cited from **there, as** that *baraita* explicitly **teaches** the **reason** for its ruling:

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁאֲנִי אוֹמֵר: אָדָם טָהוֹר נִכְנַס לְשָׁם וּנְטָלָהּ.
> **Translation:** The reason is not **that** one does not presume that ritual impurity moved from place to place, but because **I say** that **a ritually pure man entered there and took it** off the shelf and placed it onto the ground, avoiding the impure garment it would have hit had it fallen. When there is no such explanation, the principle that one presumes impurity moved from place to place does apply, e.g., in the case of the basket where the carcass of a creeping animal was found inside.

*She-ani omer adam tahor nichnas le-sham u-netalah* — I will say that a *tahor* person entered the area and moved the loaf before it could fall. This is the operating assumption that preserves the loaf's *tahor* status, and it drives the principle that we make whatever reasonable assumption we can to uphold the current status of the object as found.

The Gemara qualifies: *ad she-yomar bari li she-lo nichnas adam le-sham* — this only works unless the owner declares with certainty that no person entered the area. In that case, the loaf would be *tamei*, because the only remaining explanation for its displacement is that it fell on its own onto the *tamei* garment.

Tosafot notes that there may be a particularly strong reason to invoke the "a person moved it" assumption here: a *tahor* person seeing a loaf of *terumah* near a *tamei* garment would have a specific motivation to move it and prevent it from becoming *tamei*. This targeted rationale, Tosafot suggests, strengthens the inference beyond a generic "someone might have moved it."

Rabbi Elazar adds: *lo nitzrecha ela le-makom madru'ah* — this ruling is needed specifically for the case where the shelf is on an incline, where the loaf would very likely roll off on its own. Even there, we still assume a person moved it, as long as we cannot rule that out with certainty. The Gemara affirms: *ki kol ha-tamei'ot ke-sha'at metzi'atan* — we go by the way things are found. The loaf is found not on the garment; we assume it was *tahor* at the time of discovery, and we use whatever assumptions are available to support that.

The Gemara then explicitly connects this to the *mimakom le'makom* principle. The garment is *tamei* "here," beneath the shelf. The loaf is found "there," on the floor away from the garment. We do not carry the *tumah* from the garment's location and apply it to the loaf's location. *Lo machzikin tumah mimakom le'makom* is thus operative here — though the Gemara notes that this case has an additional feature: the possible intentional intervention of a person. *Adam d'bikvanta amrinan* — a person, who acts with intention and purpose, can be invoked as an explanatory factor. *Orev d'shelo bikvanta lo amrinan* — a raven, which acts randomly and without intention, cannot similarly be invoked to explain how a *sheretz* might have landed in a barrel from the outside. That distinction limits how far the "someone moved it" logic can be extended.

Returning to the main thread: the debate between Chizkiyah and Rabbi Yochanan over place-to-place application of *tumah* thus remains open. The various *baraitot* and *mishnayot* cited suggest that as a general rule we do not apply *tumah* definitively from one place to another, but whether we would place it in suspension (*litlot*) or declare it definitively *tahor* is the contested point, and the special circumstance of a possible human actor provides one way to explain why certain cases come out as definitively *tahor* without settling the broader question.

## Private Domain Doubt



### Private Domain Rule

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָכָא נָמֵי נֵימָא: עוֹרֵב אֲתָא וּשְׁדָא! אָדָם דִּבְכַוָּנָה — אָמְרִינַן, עוֹרֵב דְּשֶׁלֹּא בְּכַוָּנָה — לָא אָמְרִינַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: **Here, too, let us say** that **a raven,** which often touches creeping animals, **came and threw** the creeping animal into the basket when it was in the second corner, after the produce had been emptied from it. The Gemara rejects this claim: In the case of **a person, who** acts **with intent, we** can **say** that perhaps a person moved the loaf from the shelf onto the ground. By contrast, with regard to **a raven, which** does **not** act **with intent, we** do **not say** that perhaps it committed such a purposeful act.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִכְּדִי הַאי כִּכָּר סְפֵק טוּמְאָה בִּרְשׁוּת הַיָּחִיד הוּא, וְכֹל סְפֵק טוּמְאָה בִּרְשׁוּת הַיָּחִיד — סְפֵקָהּ טָמֵא, מִשּׁוּם דְּהָוֵי דָּבָר שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ דַּעַת לִישָּׁאֵל, וְכׇל דָּבָר שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ דַּעַת לִישָּׁאֵל, בֵּין בִּרְשׁוּת הָרַבִּים בֵּין בִּרְשׁוּת הַיָּחִיד — סְפֵקוֹ טָהוֹר.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty with regard to the ruling of the *Tosefta*: **Now** the status of **this loaf** found on the ground **is** one of **uncertain impurity** found **in a private domain, and** the guiding principle in **any** case of **uncertainty** involving **impurity in a private domain** is that the item with **uncertain** status is deemed **impure.** If so, shouldn’t the loaf be deemed impure? The Gemara answers: No, **as** this **is an entity that lacks consciousness** in order for it **to be asked, and** the guiding principle is that with regard to **any entity that lacks consciousness** in order for it **to be asked, whether** it is found **in a public domain or in a private domain,** the item with **uncertain** status is deemed **pure.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִי בָּעֵית אֵימָא: הָכָא בְּטוּמְאָה דְרַבָּנַן. דַּיְקָא נָמֵי דְּקָתָנֵי ״מַדָּף״, כְּדִכְתִיב ״עָלֶה נִדָּף״.
> **Translation:** **And if you wish, say** instead: That principle, that in any case of uncertainty involving impurity in a private domain the item with uncertain status is deemed impure, applies to a case of impurity by Torah law, whereas **here** we are dealing **with ritual impurity by rabbinic law.** The Gemara adds: The language of the *Tosefta* **is also precise, as it teaches:** There was a loaf resting on top of a shelf, and there was an item of light impurity [*madaf* ] lying underneath it. The term ***madaf*** is similar to that **which is written: “A driven leaf [*niddaf* ]”** (Leviticus 26:36), i.e., a light item. Likewise, the *Tosefta* is referring to a light, or rabbinic, impurity.

The Gemara now raises a further complication regarding the loaf of bread on the shelf. *Lama michti hai kikar safek tumah b'reshut hayachid hi* — this loaf of bread, if it fell within one's private house, presents a case of doubtful *tumah* in a *reshut hayachid* (private domain). The established rule is: *v'chol safek tumah b'reshut hayachid safko tamei* — any doubtful *tumah* in a private domain is treated as definitely *tamei*. Why, then, is the loaf ruled *tahor*?

The Gemara answers that the private-domain rule is derived from the case of the *sotah*, the woman suspected of adultery who enters seclusion with a man after having been warned. Her seclusion is the model for the *reshut hayachid*, and by analogy, any doubtful *tumah* in a private domain is declared definitively *tamei*. But the *sotah* case has a critical feature: the woman herself can be asked what happened. She is, in the language of the Gemara, *davar she-yesh bo da'as lishol* — something, or rather someone, who can be questioned. The loaf of bread and the garment cannot be questioned. *Mishum d'havei davar she-ein bo da'as lishol* — because this is a matter that cannot be interrogated, the stringent private-domain rule does not apply. *V'chol davar she-ein bo da'as lishol, bein b'reshut ha-rabbim bein b'reshut ha-yachid, safko tahor* — anything that cannot be asked, whether in a public or private domain, its doubt resolves to *tahor*.

This is a striking result: the very feature that makes the *sotah* analogy so powerful — the presence of a person who might know the truth and can be pressured to disclose it — is what limits the analogy's reach. One way to understand the function of *vadai tamei* in a private domain is not merely as a legal fiction but as an incentive structure. Declaring something definitively *tamei* creates a strong pressure on those involved to investigate and establish the facts. Where there is someone with *da'as* — awareness, intention, the capacity to be questioned — that pressure is meaningful. Where there is no one to ask, no one to prod, declaring *vadai tamei* accomplishes nothing practically, and so the Gemara does not do so. Whether the *yesh bo da'as lishol* criterion is itself derived from the *sotah* or is a more fundamental independent factor is itself a matter of dispute among the *Rishonim*, and Tosafot raises the possibility that it is the stronger of the two criteria.

The Gemara then adds a further, alternative ground for leniency: *ve-ei ba'it eima, hacha b'tumah d'rabbanan* — if you prefer, here the *tumah* involved is only rabbinic in character. The garment described in the *baraita* is referred to as a *madaf*, a term the Gemara connects to the word *nidaf* — something fluttering or insubstantial — suggesting it is a garment with only a weak or derivative *tumah* status. Garments belonging to ordinary people who are not careful about eating even non-sacred food (*chullin*) in a state of *taharah* are considered *tamei* by rabbinic decree for those who do maintain that standard; and the garments of those who eat *chullin b'taharah* are considered *tamei* by rabbinic decree for *kohanim* who eat *terumah*. These hierarchies of stringency are entirely rabbinic, and where the underlying *tumah* is itself only rabbinic, the Gemara is considerably more lenient. This provides an independent basis for ruling the loaf *tahor* without needing to fully resolve the private-domain question on its own terms.

---

## Three Positions



### Shammai

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: לֹא כְּדִבְרֵי זֶה כּוּ׳. תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: לֹא כְּדִבְרֵי זֶה וְלֹא כְּדִבְרֵי זֶה, לֹא כְּדִבְרֵי שַׁמַּאי — שֶׁלֹּא עָשָׂה סְיָיג לִדְבָרָיו, וְלֹא כְּדִבְרֵי הִלֵּל — שֶׁהִפְרִיז עַל מִדּוֹתָיו.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And the Rabbis say:** The *halakha* is **neither in accordance with the statement of this** *tanna* nor in accordance with the statement of that *tanna*. **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: **And the Rabbis say:** The *halakha* is **neither in accordance with the statement of this** *tanna* **nor in accordance with** the statement of **that** *tanna*. It is **not accordance with the statement of Shammai,** who rules that her time is sufficient and she does not need to be concerned that her menstrual flow started earlier, **as he did not enact** any **safeguard for his statement. And** is it **not in accordance with the statement of Hillel,** who rules that she assumes ritual impurity status retroactive to the time of her most recent examination, **as** he **went beyond [*hifriz*] his bounds** with his safeguard.

Before completing the analysis of the private domain exception, it is worth noting that all of this extensive discussion of boxes, needles, garments, and places has been running parallel to the central question of the tractate: the retroactive *tumah* status of the *niddah*. The three positions — those of Shammai, Hillel, and the *Chachamim* — now receive explicit statement. Shammai holds that a woman is *temeiah* only from the moment she discovers the blood. She cannot be assumed to have been *temeiah* beforehand; the discovery itself marks the onset of her *tumah*.

---

## Private Domain Doubt (II)



### Sotah Exception

The Gemara answers that the principle of doubtful *tumah* in a private domain being *tamei* is derived from the *sotah* case — the suspected adulteress who enters seclusion with a man after being warned. That seclusion is analogized to a *reshut hayachid*, and the *halacha* that she is considered forbidden to her husband in a definitive rather than merely doubtful sense is the model for the broader rule. But the *sotah* case has a critical feature: *yesh lah da'at lishol* — she is a person capable of being asked. She knows what happened and can be questioned.

The logic underlying the rule that doubtful *tumah* in a private domain is *tamei* thus carries with it an implicit condition: it applies only where there are people involved who are capable of being questioned — *yesh bo da'at lishol*. The ruling of definite *tumah* functions, in a sense, as a halachic instrument to press for disclosure and resolution.

---

## Three Positions (II)



### Hillel

Hillel's position stands at the opposite extreme from Shammai. A woman is retroactively *temeiah* all the way back to the last time she performed a bodily examination (*bedikah*). However long ago that was — even days — all *tahoros* she handled in the intervening period are *tamei*. *Velo k'divrei Hillel shehifriz al midotav* — the Gemara characterizes Hillel as having gone to excess, since this could push the retroactive *tumah* back a week or more.

---

## Private Domain Doub (III)



### No Da'at

The loaf of bread and the worn garment beneath it are objects. They cannot be interrogated. There is no person involved whose testimony or self-knowledge might resolve the doubt. *Mishum dehavi davar she'ein bo da'at lishol* — because this is something that cannot be asked, the rule of *safek tumah b'reshut hayachid* simply does not apply. *Vechol davar she'ein bo da'at lishol bein bireshut harabbim bein bireshut hayachid safko tahor* — anything incapable of being asked, whether in a public domain or a private one, its doubtful status is *tahor*.

---

## Three Positions (III)



### Sages Middle

The *Chachamim* stake out the middle position: *ela me'et l'et mematet al yad mipkidah l'fkidah, u'mipkidah l'fkidah mematet al yad me'et l'et* — the retroactive *tumah* extends back the lesser of two limits: 24 hours (*me'et l'et*) or the last time the woman performed a *bedikah* and found herself *tehorah* (*mipkidah l'fkidah*). Each limit constrains the other.

---

## Private Domain Doubt (IV)



### Rabbinic Tumaot

An alternative explanation is offered for why the loaf of bread in the private domain is *tahor* despite the general rule. *Hacha bitumah derabbanan* — the entire case may involve only rabbinic *tumah*, not biblical. The garment referred to in the *baraita* as *madaf* — the Gemara derives this from the verse *aleh nidaf*, a fluttering or flimsy leaf — refers to a garment that is treated as *tamei* only rabbinically. One example: garments of *amei ha'aretz*, those not careful about *taharah* even for *chullin*, are treated as *tamei* for those who eat their *chullin b'taharah*, and those who eat *chullin b'taharah* have their garments treated as *tamei* for *kohanim* eating *terumah*. These are entirely rabbinic hierarchies of *tumah*. Since the underlying *tumah* in this case is only rabbinic, the ruling is correspondingly lenient.

---

## Three Positions (IV)



### 24 Hour Limit

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא, מֵעֵת לְעֵת מְמַעֶטֶת עַל יָד מִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה, וּמִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה מְמַעֶטֶת עַל יָד מֵעֵת לְעֵת.
> **Translation:** The Rabbis continue: **Rather,** a twenty-four-hour **period reduces** the time **from examination to examination,** i.e., if her most recent self-examination took place more than twenty-four hours earlier, she need concern herself with ritual impurity only for the twenty-four-hour period prior to discerning the blood. **And from examination to examination reduces** the time **from** a twenty-four-hour **period,** i.e., if she examined herself in the course of the previous day and discovered no blood, she was definitely pure prior to the examination.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מֵעֵת לְעֵת מְמַעֶטֶת עַל יָד מִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה, כֵּיצַד? בָּדְקָה עַצְמָהּ בְּאֶחָד בַּשַּׁבָּת וּמָצָאת טְהוֹרָה, וְיָשְׁבָה שֵׁנִי וּשְׁלִישִׁי וְלֹא בָּדְקָה, וְלִרְבִיעִי בָּדְקָה וּמָצְאָה טְמֵאָה — אֵין אוֹמְרִים תְּטַמֵּא מִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה, אֶלָּא מֵעֵת לְעֵת.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* elaborates: A twenty-four-hour **period reduces** the time **from examination to examination, how so?** A woman **examined herself on Sunday and found** that she was **ritually pure, and** then **she sat** through **Monday and Tuesday and did not examine** herself. **And** then **on Wednesday she examined herself and found** that she was **impure.** In such a case **we do not say** that **she should be impure** retroactively **from** the time of this **examination** extending back **until** the time of her most recent **examination. Rather,** she is impure retroactively **for** a twenty-four-hour **period.**

*Badka atzma b'erev Shabbos u'matzah tehorah* — she examined herself on Sunday evening and found herself *tehorah*. *Yashva sheini v'shlishi v'lo badka* — Monday and Tuesday she did not examine herself. *U'birvii badka u'matzah tmeiah* — on Wednesday she examined herself and found that she was a *niddah*. In this case, *ein omrim titamei mipkidah l'fkidah* — we do not say she is *temeiah* all the way back to the last time she checked (Sunday). *Ela me'et l'et* — only 24 hours back from the discovery on Wednesday.

### Last Check Limit

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה מְמַעֶטֶת עַל יָד מֵעֵת לְעֵת, כֵּיצַד? בָּדְקָה עַצְמָהּ בְּשָׁעָה רִאשׁוֹנָה וּמָצָאת טְהוֹרָה, וְיָשְׁבָה לָהּ שְׁנִיָּה וּשְׁלִישִׁית וְלֹא בָּדְקָה, וְלִרְבִיעִית בָּדְקָה וּמָצְאָה טְמֵאָה — אֵין אוֹמְרִים תְּטַמֵּא מֵעֵת לְעֵת, אֶלָּא מִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה.
> **Translation:** The *baraita* continues: And **from examination to examination reduces** the time **from** a twenty-four-hour **period, how so?** A woman **examined herself** in **the first hour** of a day **and found** that she was **ritually pure, and** then **she sat** through the **second and third** hours of the day **and did not examine** herself. **And** then **at the fourth** hour **she examined herself and found** that she was **impure.** In such a case **we do not say** that **she should be impure** retroactively **for** a twenty-four-hour **period. Rather,** she is impure retroactively **from** the time of this **examination** extending back **until** the time of her most recent **examination,** three hours earlier.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** פְּשִׁיטָא! כֵּיוָן דְּבָדְקָה עַצְמָהּ בְּשָׁעָה רִאשׁוֹנָה וּמָצָאת טְהוֹרָה, לָא מְטַמֵּינַן לַהּ מֵעֵת לְעֵת. אַיְּידֵי דִּתְנָא מֵעֵת לְעֵת מְמַעֶטֶת עַל יָד מִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה, תְּנָא נָמֵי מִפְּקִידָה לִפְקִידָה מְמַעֶטֶת עַל יָד מֵעֵת לְעֵת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara raises a difficulty: This *halakha* is **obvious. Since she examined herself at the first hour and found** that she was **pure,** there is **no** reason **to render her impure** retroactively **for** a twenty-four-hour **period.** Why does the *baraita* state such an obvious *halakha*? The Gemara answers: Since the *baraita* taught that according to the Rabbis a twenty-four-hour **period reduces** the time **from examination to examination,** it **also taught** the parallel case, that **from examination to examination reduces** the time **from** a twenty-four-hour **period,** despite the fact that this *halakha* is obvious.

*Badka atzma b'shaah rishonah u'matzah tehorah* — she examined herself at the first hour (approximately 6:00 AM) and found herself *tehorah*. She did not examine herself at the second or third hour. *L'revi'it badka u'matzah tmeiah* — at the fourth hour (9:00 AM) she examined herself and found she was *temeiah*. In this case, *ein omrim titamei me'et l'et* — we do not go back 24 hours, because *ela mipkidah l'fkidah* — the last time she checked, only three hours earlier, sets the outer limit of retroactive *tumah*.

### Both Limits Need

The Gemara notes that one of these two examples appears self-evident. *Pshita* — in the case where she checked at 6:00 AM and found *tumah* at 9:00 AM, obviously we do not go back 24 hours; there is a confirmed *bedikah* showing she was *tehorah* just three hours earlier. Is it necessary to state this?

The Gemara answers: *ayadi d'tana me'et l'et mematet al yad mipkidah l'fkidah* — because it was necessary to state the first rule (that 24 hours limits the *mipkidah l'fkidah* in the case where she went days without checking), the *tanna* also stated the complementary rule, that when she checked recently, the *bedikah* limits the 24-hour window, even though that second case might have been derivable from the first. The two limits are stated in tandem because each is needed to define the other: 24 hours limits how far back a *mipkidah l'fkidah* can reach, and a *mipkidah l'fkidah* limits how far back the 24-hour window extends. Together they define the *Chachamim*'s middle position.
