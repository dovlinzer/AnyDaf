## Measures & Custom



### Mishna Overview



The *mishna* at the center of today's daf addresses a deceptively broad topic: what it means to deal honestly with weights and measures. Honesty in this domain is not limited to ensuring that one's measuring vessels are physically accurate. It also encompasses a set of related obligations — cleaning out the measuring containers and wiping down the scales, and attending carefully to the very act of weighing or filling: whether one adds a small amount extra in the buyer's favor, whether the scale tips slightly toward the buyer, and whether a volume measure is filled level or heaped. All of these practices bear on the question of whether a buyer receives a fair measure.

### Large vs Small

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָקוֹם שֶׁנָּהֲגוּ לָמוֹד בְּדַקָּה – לֹא יָמוֹד בְּגַסָּה. בְּגַסָּה – לֹא יָמוֹד בְּדַקָּה. לִמְחוֹק – לֹא יִגְדּוֹשׁ. לִגְדּוֹשׁ – לֹא יִמְחוֹק.
> **Translation:** The mishna continues to discuss the correct method of weighing: In **a place where they were accustomed to measure** merchandise in several stages **with a small** measuring vessel, **one may not measure** all the items at once **with** a single **large** measuring vessel. In a place where they measure **with** one **large** measuring vessel, **one may not measure with** several **small** measuring vessels. In a place where the custom is **to level** the top of the measuring vessel to remove substances heaped above its edges, **one may not heap** it, and where the custom is **to heap** it, **one may not level** it.

The *mishna* establishes: *makom shenahagu lamod begasa al yimod bedaka* — in a place where the custom is to use a large measure when selling, one may not switch to using a small measure, and vice versa. The reasoning behind this rule is instructive. Since perfect precision is essentially unattainable in any act of measurement, there will always be some slight margin of error. The expectation, and the norm of honest dealing, is that this margin should consistently favor the buyer. When multiple small measures are used, that slight error occurs repeatedly — each individual measuring carries a small surplus — and the cumulative effect benefits the buyer more than a single large measurement would. This is why switching to a large measure in a place where small measures are the norm would disadvantage the buyer.

Equally striking, however, is the *mishna*'s concern in the reverse direction: one may not switch to small measures in a place where large measures are standard, even if this would benefit the buyer. The *mishna* clearly protects the seller's legitimate interests as well, not merely the buyer's.

### Lifnim Mishurat

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גְּמָ׳ מְנָהָנֵי מִילֵּי? אָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״אֶבֶן שְׁלֵמָה וָצֶדֶק״ – צַדֵּק מִשֶּׁלְּךָ וְתֵן לוֹ. אִי הָכִי, אֵימָא סֵיפָא: הָיָה שׁוֹקֵל לוֹ עַיִן בְּעַיִן – נוֹתֵן לוֹ גֵּירוּמִין. וְאִי הַכְרָעָה דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא, הֵיכִי יָהֵיב לֵיהּ עַיִן בְּעַיִן?
> **Translation:** GEMARA: The Gemara asks: **From where are these matters,** that the seller must initially let the scales tilt an extra handbreadth, **derived? Reish Lakish said:** The source is **that the verse states** that one should have: **“A perfect and just [*tzedek*] weight”** (Deuteronomy 25:15), which is interpreted as an instruction to the seller: **Be righteous [*tzaddek*] with that which is yours and give it to** the buyer. The Gemara asks: **If that is so, say the latter clause:** If the seller **weighed for him exactly, he gives** the buyer **additional amounts. But if letting** the scales **tilt** is obligatory **by Torah law, how can he** originally **give him** by weighing **exactly?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא רֵישָׁא בְּמָקוֹם שֶׁנָּהֲגוּ; וְאִי אִיתְּמַר דְּרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ – אַסֵּיפָא אִיתְּמַר: הָיָה שׁוֹקֵל לוֹ עַיִן בְּעַיִן – נוֹתֵן לוֹ גֵּירוּמִין. מְנָהָנֵי מִילֵּי? אָמַר רֵישׁ לָקִישׁ, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: ״וָצֶדֶק״ – צַדֵּק מִשֶּׁלְּךָ וְתֵן לוֹ. וְכַמָּה גֵּירוּמִין? אָמַר רַבִּי אַבָּא בַּר מֶמֶל אָמַר רַב: אֶחָד מֵעֲשָׂרָה בַּלִּיטְרָא בַּלַּח – לַעֲשָׂרָה לִיטְרִין.
> **Translation:** **Rather,** it is not obligatory to let the scales tilt, and **the first clause** is referring **to a place where they are accustomed** to let the scales tilt an extra handbreadth. **And if** the statement **of Reish Lakish was stated,** it **was stated with regard to the latter clause:** If the seller **weighed for him exactly, he gives** the buyer **additional amounts. From where is this matter** derived? **Reish Lakish said** that this is **as the verse states: “And just [*tzedek*],”** which indicates: **Be righteous [*tzaddek*] with that which is yours and give it to** the buyer. The Gemara asks: **And how much** are the **additional amounts** that are given? **Rabbi Abba bar Memel says** that **Rav says: In** the case of **liquids, one-tenth of a *litra* for** every **ten *litra*,** i.e., one-hundredth.

Even in a place where the measuring practice is a level, flat fill, and even if the norms of that place are strictly followed, there remains a separate obligation to give something extra beyond the precise measure — a *lifnim mishurat hadin* consideration. This is distinct from the question of how the measuring itself is conducted. The question of whether the act of measuring is done in a balanced or slightly generous manner is one issue; the question of throwing in an additional unit as a gesture of honest generosity is a separate matter entirely.

### Smooth vs Heap


A *baraita* is then cited: *minayin she'ein mokechin bemakom shegodshin ve'ein godshin bemakom shemokechin* — how do we know that one may not smooth the top of a volume measure in a place where the custom is to heap, and may not heap in a place where the custom is to smooth? The source is the *pasuk*: *eifah shleimah vetzedek yihyeh lach* — "a complete and just measure shall be yours." "Complete," the *baraita* understands, means complete in accordance with the norms of the place. A heaped measure in a smoothing locale is not a complete measure; it exceeds the norm. A smoothed measure in a heaping locale falls short of it.

Why should this be a problem when both parties agree to it? The *Rashbam* explains that the concern is a social one. Even if buyer and seller privately arrange to deviate from the local norm, other people see the transaction visually but do not know its terms. They observe the flat measure and assume it is the going practice — and this disrupts the established norms of the marketplace, ultimately harming either buyers or sellers who thereafter expect the wrong standard.

### Eifah Shleima

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶחָד לַעֲשָׂרָה בַּלַּח, וְאֶחָד לְעֶשְׂרִים בְּיָבֵשׁ וְכוּ׳. אִיבַּעְיָא לְהוּ: הֵיכִי קָאָמַר? אֶחָד מֵעֲשָׂרָה בַּלַּח – לַעֲשָׂרָה דְלַח, וְאֶחָד מֵעֶשְׂרִים בַּיָּבֵשׁ – לְעֶשְׂרִים דְּיָבֵשׁ; אוֹ דִלְמָא, אֶחָד מֵעֲשָׂרָה – לַעֲשָׂרָה דְּלַח וּלְעֶשְׂרִים דְּיָבֵשׁ? תֵּיקוּ.
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches that the seller adds **one-tenth in** the case of **liquids, and one-twentieth for dry** goods. **A dilemma was raised before** the Sages: With regard to **what** case **is** the *tanna* of the mishna **speaking?** Does he mean **one-tenth in** the case of **liquids for** every **ten** units **of liquid, and** similarly **one-twentieth in** the case of **dry** goods **for** every **twenty** units **of dry** goods, i.e., one four-hundredth? **Or perhaps,** he means **one-tenth for** every **ten** units **of liquid, and** similarly one-tenth **for** every **twenty** units **of dry** goods, i.e., one two-hundredth? The Gemara states that the dilemma **shall stand** unresolved.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי לֵוִי: קָשֶׁה עוֹנְשָׁן שֶׁל מִדּוֹת יוֹתֵר מֵעוֹנְשָׁן שֶׁל עֲרָיוֹת, שֶׁזֶּה נֶאֱמַר בָּהֶן ״אֵל״, וְזֶה נֶאֱמַר בָּהֶן ״אֵלֶּה״. וּמַאי מַשְׁמַע דְּהַאי ״אֵל״ – קָשֶׁה הוּא? דִּכְתִיב: ״וְאֶת אֵילֵי הָאָרֶץ לָקָח״.
> **Translation:** § **Rabbi Levi says: The punishment for** using false **measures is more severe than the punishment for** transgressing the prohibition of **forbidden sexual relations. As** in **that** case, forbidden relations, it **is stated with regard to them** a shortened term for the word “these”: **“*El*,”** in the verse: “For all these [*el* ] abominations” (Leviticus 18:27). **And** in **this** case, false measures, it **is stated** an expanded term for the word “these”: **“*Elleh*,”** in the verse: “For all that do these [*elleh*] things, even all that do unrighteously, are an abomination unto the Lord your God” (Deuteronomy 25:16). **And from where may it be inferred that this** expression **“*el*”** indicates that the prohibition **is severe,** based on which it is understood that the form this word takes indicates a level of severity? **As it is written: “And the mighty [*eilei*] of the land he took away”** (Ezekiel 17:13).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** גַּבֵּי עֲרָיוֹת נָמֵי, הָכְתִיב ״אֵלֶּה״! הָהוּא לְמַעוֹטֵי מִדּוֹת מִכָּרֵת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But with regard to forbidden relations isn’t it also written:** “For whosoever shall do any of **these [*elleh*]** abominations” (Leviticus 18:29)? If so, why is the punishment for using false measures considered harsher? The Gemara answers: **That** expression of “*elleh*” (Leviticus 18:29) in the context of forbidden relations does not serve to emphasize its severity. Rather, it serves **to exclude** one who uses deception in **measures from** the penalty of **excision from the World-to-Come [*karet*].**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֶלָּא מַאי עוּדְפַּיְיהוּ? דְּהָתָם אֶפְשָׁר בִּתְשׁוּבָה, וְהָכָא לָא אֶפְשָׁר בִּתְשׁוּבָה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **But** if the punishment is in fact less severe, **what is the advantage,** i.e., the greater severity, in the case of false measures? The Gemara answers **that there,** in the case of one who engages in forbidden relations, **he has the possibility of repentance. But here,** in the case of one who uses false measures, **there is no possibility of repentance** because he has no way of knowing whom he cheated, and is therefore unable to return the stolen money.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי לֵוִי: קָשֶׁה גֶּזֶל הֶדְיוֹט יוֹתֵר מִגֶּזֶל גָּבוֹהַּ, שֶׁזֶּה הִקְדִּים ״חֵטְא״ לִ״מְּעִילָה״, וְזֶה הִקְדִּים ״מְעִילָה״ לְ״חֵטְא״.
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Levi says: Robbing an ordinary** person **is more severe than robbing the Most High,** i.e., taking consecrated property. **As** with regard to **this** regular robber, the verse states **“sin” before “*me’ila*”:** “If any one sin, and commit a trespass [*me’ila*] against the Lord, and deal falsely with his neighbor in a matter of deposit, or of pledge, or of robbery, or have oppressed his neighbor” (Leviticus 5:21). **And** with regard to **that** one who misuses consecrated items, the verse states ***me’ila* before sin:** “If any one engages in misuse [*timol ma’al*] and sins unwittingly” (Leviticus 5:15).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאָמַר רַבִּי לֵוִי: בּוֹא וּרְאֵה, שֶׁלֹּא כְּמִדַּת הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא מִדַּת בָּשָׂר וָדָם הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא – בֵּרַךְ יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּעֶשְׂרִים וּשְׁתַּיִם, וְקִלְּלָן בִּשְׁמֹנֶה. בֵּרְכָן בְּעֶשְׂרִים וּשְׁתַּיִם – מֵ״אִם בְּחֻקֹּתַי״ עַד ״קוֹמְמִיּוּת״;
> **Translation:** **And Rabbi Levi says: Come and see that the attribute of flesh and blood is unlike the attribute of the Holy One, Blessed be He. The Holy One, Blessed be He, blessed the Jewish people with twenty-two, and cursed them with** only **eight.** Rabbi Levi explains: **He blessed them with** the **twenty-two** letters of the Hebrew alphabet, **from** the first letter, *alef*, that begins the verse: **“If [*im*] you** walk **in My statutes”** (Leviticus 26:3), **until “upright [*komemiyyut*]”** (Leviticus 26:13), which ends with the letter *tav*, the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְקִלְּלָן בִּשְׁמוֹנָה – מִ״וְּאִם בְּחֻקֹּתַי תִּמְאָסוּ״ עַד ״וְאֶת חֻקֹּתַי גָּעֲלָה נַפְשָׁם״.
> **Translation:** **And He cursed them with eight** letters, **from** the letter *vav* that begins the verse: **“And if [*ve’im*] you shall reject My statutes”** (Leviticus 26:15), **until: “And My statutes were abhorred by their soul [*nafsham*]”** (Leviticus 26:43), which ends with the letter *mem*. There are eight letters in the Hebrew alphabet from the letter *vav* to the letter *mem*, inclusive.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִילּוּ מֹשֶׁה רַבֵּינוּ – בֵּרְכָן בִּשְׁמוֹנֶה, וְקִלְּלָן בְּעֶשְׂרִים וּשְׁתַּיִם. בֵּרְכָן בִּשְׁמוֹנֶה –
> **Translation:** **And yet Moses, our teacher,** who is flesh and blood, **blessed them with eight** letters, **and cursed them with twenty-two. He blessed them with eight** letters,

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִ״וְּהָיָה אִם שָׁמוֹעַ תִּשְׁמַע״, וְעַד ״לְעׇבְדָם״; וְקִלְּלָן בְּעֶשְׂרִים וּשְׁתַּיִם – מִ״וְּהָיָה אִם לֹא תִשְׁמַע״ עַד ״וְאֵין קֹנֶה״.
> **Translation:** **from** the letter *vav* that begins the verse: **“And it shall come to pass [*vehaya*], if you shall hearken diligently”** (Deuteronomy 28:1), **until: “To serve them [*le’ovdam*]”** (Deuteronomy 28:14), which ends with the letter *mem*. **And he cursed them with twenty-two** letters, **from** the letter *vav* that begins the verse: **“But it shall come to pass [*vehaya*], if you will not hearken** to the voice of the Lord” (Deuteronomy 28:15), **until: “And no man shall buy [*koneh*]”** (Deuteronomy 28:68). This verse ends with a letter *heh*, which comes just before *vav* in the alphabet. Starting with a *vav* and ending with a *heh* encompasses the twenty-two letters of the alphabet.

The *baraita* continues: *uminayin she'im amar hareni mokech bemakom shegodshin ulephachot lo min hadamim* — how do we know that even if the seller says "I will smooth it rather than heap it, but I will charge a reduced price to compensate," one may not follow this approach? The *Rashbam* explains that the additional word in the *pasuk* — *vetzedek*, "and just" — prohibits even this arrangement. The reason is the same: *de'ika dechazi bimdidah velo yada lo bifchas velo betosefes* — observers see the manner of measuring but have no way of knowing whether a corresponding price adjustment was made. This visual discrepancy leads to disruption of honest practice throughout the marketplace. One student added a sharper formulation: this amounts to *rama'ut* — it actively invites deception, since other sellers, seeing the deviation, may adopt it without the compensating price adjustment. The *Rashbam* includes this concern implicitly, but the full force of the point is that the visual impression creates the opening for fraud.

It is worth noting that this concern is specific to the laws of *middos* — measures and weights. The question naturally arises whether a similar logic would apply to *kinyanim*, acts of acquisition, where parties might privately agree to some non-standard arrangement. But that is a different matter entirely. A private *kinyan* between two individuals cannot simply be invented, and the kind of trade-based customary *kinyan* that is recognized in halacha — the sort we have been discussing in a different context — rests on communal convention, not unilateral agreement. The concern with *middos* is precisely that the visual act is public even when the agreement is private.

*Tosafot* offers a somewhat different explanation of the underlying prohibition, which we will return to below.

A note from the *mishna* suggests that the difference between a heaped and a level measure is substantial — as much as a third more for the heap. This is a striking figure. If the deviation is that large, it would obviously need to be reflected in pricing; one cannot simply pretend the difference is negligible. Similarly, the *mishna* mentions later that the scale should tip a full *tefach* in the buyer's favor when weighing — which also seems like an enormous amount, one that would necessarily affect the pricing structure. These details suggest that whatever these measures mean in practice, they were understood as embedded in a larger system of pricing conventions, not as isolated acts of generosity.

### Even Shleima

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מָקוֹם שֶׁנָּהֲגוּ לָמוֹד בְּגַסָּה כּוּ׳. (סִימָן: אֵין מְעַיְּינִין, וְאֵין גּוֹדְשִׁין, בַּאֲגַרְדְּמִין, וּבְלִיטְרָא, שְׁלֹשָׁה, וָעֶשֶׂר, נֶפֶשׁ, מִשְׁקָלוֹת, מִמֶּחָק, עָבֶה, לֹא תַעֲשׂוּ, לֹא יַעֲשֶׂה.)
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches that **in a place where they were accustomed to measure with** one **large** measuring vessel, one may not measure with several small measuring vessels. In a place where the custom is to level the top of the measuring vessel to remove substances heaped above its edges, one may not heap it; and where they are accustomed to heap it, one may not level it. The Gemara provides **a mnemonic** for the ensuing *halakhot*: **One may not balance with precision; and one may not heap** it; **with market inspectors [*ba’agardamin*]; and with a *litra*; three; and ten; fulcrum; weights; a thick leveler; you may not do; one may not do.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁאֵין מוֹחֲקִין בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁגּוֹדְשִׁין, וְאֵין גּוֹדְשִׁין בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁמּוֹחֲקִין? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״אֵיפָה שְׁלֵמָה״.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught: From where** is it derived **that one may not level** a measuring vessel **in a place where they** are accustomed to **heap** it **and** that **one may not heap** it **in a place where they** are accustomed to **level** it? **The verse states: “A perfect weight”** (Deuteronomy 25:15), which indicates that one must use whatever is considered a perfect measuring vessel in that locale, as this ensures that there is no deception or trickery.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִנַּיִן שֶׁאִם אָמַר: הֲרֵינִי מוֹחֵק בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁגּוֹדְשִׁין – וְלִפְחוֹת לוֹ מִן הַדָּמִים, וַהֲרֵינִי גּוֹדֵשׁ בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁמּוֹחֲקִין – וּלְהוֹסִיף לוֹ עַל הַדָּמִים; שֶׁאֵין שׁוֹמְעִין לוֹ? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״אֵיפָה שְׁלֵמָה וָצֶדֶק יִהְיֶה לָּךְ״.
> **Translation:** **And from where** is it derived **that if** the seller **said in a place where they heap: I am hereby leveling and** am willing in exchange **to reduce the price for** you, or said, **in a place where they level: I am hereby heaping and** am willing **to increase the price for** you, **that** the court **does not listen to him? The verse states: “A perfect and just weight you shall have”** (Deuteronomy 25:15). The addition of the term “just,” beyond the requirement that the weight be “perfect,” teaches that one must keep his weights in a just manner, i.e., that employed by the people of that locale.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מִנַּיִן שֶׁאֵין מְעַיְּינִין בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁמַּכְרִיעִין, וְאֵין מַכְרִיעִין בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁמְּעַיְּינִין? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״אֶבֶן שְׁלֵמָה״.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught: From where** is it derived **that one may not** balance a scale so that it will **balance with precision [*me’ayyenin*] in a place where they** are accustomed to **let** the scales **tilt** a handbreadth for the benefit of the buyer, **and** that **one may not let** the scales **tilt** a handbreadth **in a place where they** are accustomed to **balance with precision? The verse states: “A perfect weight”** (Deuteronomy 25:15), which teaches that the weight must be considered perfect in that locale.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמִנַּיִן שֶׁאִם אָמַר: הֲרֵינִי מְעַיֵּין בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁמַּכְרִיעִין – וְלִפְחוֹת לוֹ מִן הַדָּמִים, וַהֲרֵינִי מַכְרִיעַ בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁמְּעַיְּינִין – וּלְהוֹסִיף לוֹ עַל הַדָּמִים; שֶׁאֵין שׁוֹמְעִין לוֹ? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״אֶבֶן שְׁלֵמָה וָצֶדֶק״.
> **Translation:** **And from where** is it derived **that if** a seller **said, in a place where** it is the custom to **let** the scales **tilt** a handbreadth: **I am hereby balancing with precision and** am willing **to reduce the price for** the buyer; **or, in a place where** it is the custom to **balance with precision: I am hereby letting** the scales **tilt** a handbreadth **and** am willing **to increase the price for** the buyer, **that** the court **does not listen to him? The verse states: “A perfect and just weight”** (Deuteronomy 25:15), which teaches that the just way of acting is to weigh in accordance with the practice in that locale.

The *baraita* then extends the identical analysis to weight measures. *Minayin she'ein me'aynin bimkom shemachri'in* — how do we know that one may not weigh to the exact balance (*me'aynin*, literally, "eye-level") in a place where the norm is to tip the scale (*machri'in*) in the buyer's favor? And the reverse: one may not tip the scale where the norm is to weigh precisely. The source is *even shleimah vatzedek yihyeh lach* — "a complete and just weight shall be yours." The word *even* (weight measure) parallels *eifah* (volume measure) exactly, and the same analysis applies. Exact balance, in a tipping-custom locale, defrauds the buyer. Tipping, in an exact-balance locale, defrauds the seller. And the same prohibition against compensating through adjusted pricing applies here as well, with *even shleimah vatzedek* as its source.

It is worth pausing on the linguistic observation that *machri'ah* — "to tip the scales" — is the same word used in halachic discourse for "to decide between competing opinions," because tipping in one direction is itself the act of deciding. The metaphor is built into the vocabulary.

## Midrash & Officials



### Rav Yehuda

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה מִסּוּרָא: ״לֹא יִהְיֶה לְךָ בְּבֵיתְךָ״ – מָה טַעַם? מִשּׁוּם ״אֵיפָה וְאֵיפָה״.
> **Translation:** **Rav Yehuda of Sura says** a homiletic interpretation of the phrase: “You shall not have in your house diverse measures” (Deuteronomy 25:14): **“You shall not have in your house”** means that you will become a pauper, who has nothing in his house. **What is the reason** for this? It is **due to** the fact that you used **diverse measures,** buying with a large vessel and selling with a small one.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** ״לֹא יִהְיֶה לְךָ בְּכִיסְךָ״ – מָה טַעַם? מִשּׁוּם ״אֶבֶן וָאָבֶן״. אֲבָל ״אֶבֶן שְׁלֵמָה וָצֶדֶק יִהְיֶה לָּךְ, אֵיפָה שְׁלֵמָה וָצֶדֶק יִהְיֶה לָּךְ״.
> **Translation:** The phrase: “You shall not have in your purse diverse weights” (Deuteronomy 25:13), is interpreted in a similar fashion: **“You shall not have in your purse,”** i.e., you will become a pauper, who has nothing in his purse. **What is the reason** for this? It is **due to** the fact that you used **diverse weights. But** if you possess only **“a perfect and just weight,”** then **“you shall have”** wealth. Likewise, if you possess only **“a perfect and just measure,”** then **“you shall have”** wealth.

The *gemara* then presents a remarkable *midrashic* reading of the *pesukim* in Devarim 25, attributed to Rav Yehuda Mi-Sura. The verse *lo yihyeh lecha b'veitecha eifah va'eifah* — "you shall not have in your house two measures" — is read as a prediction of poverty: *lo yihyeh lecha b'veitecha*, "you will have nothing in your house." Why will this person end up with nothing? *Mishum eifah va'eifah* — because he maintains two sets of measures in his house: the larger measure with which he buys and the smaller measure with which he sells. This is the classic "two sets of books" — one for acquisition and one for sale, each manipulated in the seller's favor.

The reading extends further: *lo yihyeh lecha b'chisecha* — "you will have nothing in your pocket" — because of *even va'even*, maintaining two sets of weights. But by contrast, *even shleimah vatzedek yihyeh lach* — if you maintain a just weight, *yihyeh lach*, you will have wealth. *Eifah shleimah vatzedek yihyeh lach* — and if you maintain a just volume measure, you too will be rewarded with prosperity.

This is a powerful *midrashic* inversion: the very phrase the deceiver hopes will describe his gains — *yihyeh lach* — becomes the description of the honest dealer's reward, while the cheater ends up with *lo yihyeh lach* precisely because of the deception he practiced.

The *gemara* then takes the final *pasuk*, *eifah shleimah vatzedek yihyeh lach*, and notes that the use of the singular pronoun *lach* (rather than *lachem*) is instructive in a surprising direction. Normally one might think the singular *lach* addresses the individual and the plural *lachem* addresses the community. But the *midrash halacha* often reads the singular as a communal address — the nation as a unified entity — while the plural distributes obligation to each individual. The parallel from *Sefirat HaOmer* is instructive: *u'sefartem lachem* imposes an individual obligation on each person to count, while *v'safarta lecha* regarding *yovel* is a communal obligation fulfilled through the *Beit Din*. So too here: *eifah shleimah vatzedek yihyeh lach* addresses the community as a whole, teaching that the community bears collective responsibility to ensure the integrity of weights and measures.

### Agardomin

This communal reading leads directly to the practical ruling: *melamed she'ma'amidim agardemin l'middot* — the community must appoint *agardomin* (market inspectors, from a Greek term meaning officer in charge of the market) to oversee weights and measures. These officials go from stall to stall, testing whether the measures are accurate — the ancient equivalent of the government inspectors who once visited butcher shops incognito to weigh a pound of meat after purchase. That this kind of oversight is genuinely necessary is illustrated by a more contemporary parallel: studies of fish labeling, including one that began as a high school science fair project involving DNA testing, have found that something like thirty percent of fish sold in stores are intentionally mislabeled — cheaper species sold under the name of more expensive ones. The question of how much the fear of being caught can be relied upon to keep sellers honest is itself a recurring one in *halacha*, particularly in the context of *kashrut*, and the empirical answer appears to be: not enough. City officials are needed.

However, *v'ein ma'amidim agardemin l'sha'arim* — one does not appoint such officials to regulate prices. The *Rashbam* explains: free-market competition is sufficient to bring prices to their natural level. If one vendor overcharges, a competitor will undercut him and attract the business. This principle implicitly assumes a competitive marketplace without collusion, but it is a strikingly early articulation of the idea that market prices regulate themselves while the integrity of the measuring instruments does not.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״(לֹא) יִהְיֶה לְךָ״ – מְלַמֵּד שֶׁמַּעֲמִידִין אֲגַרְדְּמִין לַמִּדּוֹת, וְאֵין מַעֲמִידִין אֲגַרְדְּמִין לַשְּׁעָרִים. דְּבֵי נְשִׂיאָה אוֹקִימוּ אֲגַרְדְּמִין בֵּין לַמִּדּוֹת בֵּין לַשְּׁעָרִים, אֲמַר לֵיהּ שְׁמוּאֵל לְקַרְנָא: פּוֹק תְּנִי לְהוּ: מַעֲמִידִין אֲגַרְדְּמִין לַמִּדּוֹת, וְאֵין מַעֲמִידִין אֲגַרְדְּמִין לַשְּׁעָרִים.
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught** that the phrase: **“You shall not have,” teaches that** the court **appoints market inspectors to** supervise the accuracy of **measures.** The Gemara infers: **But** the court **does not appoint market inspectors for** supervising market **prices.** The Gemara relates: **The house of the *Nasi* appointed market inspectors for** supervising **both measures and prices. Shmuel said to** his student, the Sage **Karna: Go out** and **teach them** that **one appoints market inspectors for** supervising **measures but one does not appoint market inspectors for prices.**

### Nasi Disagreement


The house of the *Nasi* in Bavel, however, took a different position: *debe Nesia ukmi agordemi bein le-middot bein le-shearim* — they appointed officials for both weights and measures *and* for prices. Shmuel, upon learning of this, sent a pointed message through his colleague Karna: *puk tani le-hu ma'amidin agordemi le-middot ve-ein ma'amidim agordemi le-shearim* — go teach the house of the *Nasi* that the *halacha* is that officials are appointed for measures but not for prices.

That Shmuel dispatched Karna to deliver this correction to the *Nasi*'s household is itself noteworthy. The *gemara* then reports that when Karna arrived, he instead taught *ma'amidin agordemi bein le-middot bein le-shearim* — the position of the *Nasi*'s house, which he had been sent to correct. Shmuel's response was sharp: *ma shimcha?* — "What is your name?" *Karna* — "Horn?" *Tepuk lei karna bein einai* — "A horn should sprout between your eyes." And, the *gemara* notes, *nafka lei karna bein einai* — it did. The *Rashbam* mentions a parallel account in *Gemara Shabbat* in which Shmuel was angered at Karna for a different reason; perhaps both incidents contributed to the result.

### Rav Yitzchak

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** נְפַק דְּרַשׁ לְהוּ: מַעֲמִידִין אֲגַרְדְּמִין בֵּין לַמִּדּוֹת בֵּין לַשְּׁעָרִים. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: מָה שְׁמָךְ? קַרְנָא. תִּיפּוֹק לֵיהּ קַרְנָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ. נְפַקָא לֵיהּ קַרְנָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ. וְאִיהוּ כְּמַאן סָבַר? כִּי הָא דְּאָמַר רָמֵי בַּר חָמָא אָמַר רַבִּי יִצְחָק: מַעֲמִידִין אֲגַרְדְּמִין בֵּין לַמִּדּוֹת בֵּין לַשְּׁעָרִים, מִפְּנֵי הָרַמָּאִין.
> **Translation:** Karna **went out** and **taught them** that **one appoints market inspectors for** supervising **both measures and prices.** Shmuel, hearing what he had done, **said to him: What is your name?** He replied: **Karna.** Shmuel said: **Let a horn [*karna*] emerge in his eye. A horn,** i.e., a growth of flesh, **emerged in his eye.** The Gemara asks: **And** Karna, **in accordance with whose** opinion **did he hold,** which led him to disregard his teacher’s statement? He held **in accordance with that which Rami bar Ḥama says** that **Rabbi Yitzḥak says: One appoints market inspectors for** supervising **both measures and prices, due to swindlers,** to prevent people from using smaller measures or from selling at a steep price while falsely claiming that they are selling superior-quality merchandise.

The *gemara* then asks: when Karna taught the position of the *Nasi*'s house, whose opinion was he relying on? It would be unthinkable that he would teach an entirely unsupported view even under social pressure. The answer: *ki ha de'amar Rami bar Chama amar Rav Yitzchak ma'amidin agordemi bein le-middot bein le-shearim mipnei ha-ramaim* — there is indeed a tradition that officials are appointed even for prices, because of the *ramaim*, the deceivers. The *Rashbam* explains that this is not merely about setting prices, but about broader forms of market deception — selling lower-grade goods as higher-grade, misrepresenting quality, and similar frauds that a simple price check would not catch. More straightforwardly, collusion among sellers to fix prices artificially is a classic form of *ramaim* that free-market competition alone cannot remedy. It is worth noting that the *Rambam* rules in accordance with Rav Yitzchak's position — officials are appointed for both measures and prices.

### Price Gouging

This discussion of *ramaim* opens into a broader theme: the historical phenomenon of price gouging tied to religious observance. When a community is known to *need* certain goods — fish for *Shabbos*, *matzah* for *Pesach*, or whatever the religious occasion demands — unscrupulous vendors exploit that need to charge inflated prices. The *gemara* itself records cases of this kind, and the communal response was often dramatic: Rabbis and community leaders would issue a prohibition on purchasing the gouged item until the price came down. This was a form of organized communal pressure — effectively a boycott — to counteract the leverage that religious necessity created for dishonest sellers. Similar dynamics appear in later *teshuvot* regarding funeral expenses, where communities adopted the principle of maintaining minimal expenditures rather than allowing funeral homes to exploit the grief and guilt of mourners. The appointment of *agardomin* for prices, in Rav Yitzchak's view, is precisely the institutional response to such structural vulnerabilities.

## Weighing Procedure



### Standard Units

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: הָיָה מְבַקֵּשׁ מִמֶּנּוּ לִיטְרָא – שׁוֹקֵל לוֹ לִיטְרָא. חֲצִי לִיטְרָא – שׁוֹקֵל לוֹ חֲצִי לִיטְרָא. רְבִיעַ לִיטְרָא – שׁוֹקֵל לוֹ רְבִיעַ לִיטְרָא. מַאי קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן? דִּמְתַקְּנִינַן מַתְקָלֵי עַד הָכִי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara discusses several *halakhot* related to the cases of the mishna. **The Sages taught:** If the buyer **requested from** the seller **a *litra*** of a specific item, **he weighs for him** using **a *litra*** weight. If he asks for **half a *litra*, he weighs for him using a half-*litra*** weight. If he wants **one-quarter** of **a *litra*, he weighs for him** using **a quarter**-***litra*** weight. The Gemara asks: Isn’t this obvious? **What is** this *baraita* **teaching us?** The Gemara explains: It teaches **that one sets weights until this** amount, one-quarter of a *litra*, but not less. If a buyer asks the seller to weigh a smaller amount for him, his request is not granted.

A *baraita* teaches: *hayah mevakesh mimenu litra shokel lo litra, chatzi litra shokel lo chatzi litra, revia litra shokel lo revia litra* — if someone wants to buy a pound, weigh it with a one-pound weight; half a pound, with a half-pound weight; a quarter pound, with a quarter-pound weight. The *gemara* asks: *mai kamashmalan* — what does this add? The answer is that the *baraita* establishes which specific weight denominations must be available and used: a full pound, a half, and a quarter. The reason for mandating these specific units is twofold. First, if only large weights are available, buyers may be forced to overbuy. Second, and more significantly, the differences between these denominations — half, quarter — are large enough that the weights cannot easily be confused or swapped. Weight units that are too close in size to one another create the risk that a slightly lighter weight will be substituted undetected.

### Three-Quarter Measure

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: הָיָה מְבַקֵּשׁ מִמֶּנּוּ שְׁלֹשָׁה רִבְעֵי לִיטְרָא, לֹא יֹאמַר לוֹ: שְׁקוֹל לִי שְׁלֹשָׁה רִבְעֵי לִיטְרָא אַחַת אַחַת. אֶלָּא שׁוֹקֵל לִיטְרָא, וּמַנִּיחַ רְבִיעַ לִיטְרָא עִם הַבָּשָׂר.
> **Translation:** Furthermore, **the Sages taught:** If the buyer **requested from him three-quarters** of **a *litra*,** and there is no weight equal to this amount, the buyer **may not say to him: Weigh for me three** units using **a quarter-*litra*** weight, **one** by **one,** so that the seller lets the scales tilt by a handbreadth as he measures each quarter-*litra* weight. **Rather, he weighs** the merchandise all at once, as he places **a *litra*** weight on one pan of the scale, and **places** on the other pan of the scale **a quarter-*litra* weight** together **with the meat** that is being sold.

The case of a three-quarter pound purchase presents an interesting problem. The *baraita* rules: *lo yomar lo shokel li shlosha reviei litra achat achat* — one should not make three separate quarter-pound weighings. Instead: *shokel litra umeiniach revia litra im habasar* — one places a one-pound weight on one side of the scale and adds a quarter-pound weight *together with* the meat on the other side, so that they balance. Why not simply use a half-pound weight and a quarter-pound weight on the counterbalancing side? The *Rashbam* explains the objection to three separate weighings: even in a locale where exact-balance (*me'aynin*) weighing is the norm, the honest dealer will always err ever so slightly in the buyer's favor, because perfect precision is unattainable. Three separate weighings would give the buyer three such tips in his favor, which is more than the norm provides for. Hence the single combined weighing.

### Ten Pounds

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: הָיָה מְבַקֵּשׁ מִמֶּנּוּ עֶשֶׂר לִיטְרִין, לֹא יֹאמַר לוֹ: שְׁקוֹל לִי אַחַת אַחַת וְהַכְרִיעָהּ; אֶלָּא שׁוֹקֵל לוֹ כּוּלָּן בְּבַת אַחַת, וְנוֹתֵן הֶכְרֵעַ אֶחָד לְכוּלָּן.
> **Translation:** **The Sages** likewise **taught:** In a case where the buyer **requested from** the seller **ten *litra*** of merchandise, the buyer **may not say to him: Weigh for me** each *litra* **one** by **one, and let** the scales **tilt** each time, as on every occasion that the seller does this the buyer receives more than that for which he paid. **Rather, he weighs all** the merchandise **at the same time and lets** the scales **tilt once for all** the merchandise.

The same principle governs larger quantities. *Hayah mevakesh mimenu eser litrin* — if someone wants to buy ten pounds — *lo yomar lo shkol li achat achat v'yachria* — one should not make ten separate one-pound weighings, each with its own slight tip in the buyer's favor. Instead, *shoklan kulan b'vat achat v'noten hechra echad l'kulan* — weigh all ten pounds at once, with a single tip for the entire transaction.

### Scale Mechanics



A *baraita* now turns to the physical specifications of the scale itself, referring throughout to the *nefesh moznayim* — the entire scale assembly. For scales used to weigh the heaviest goods — the *gemara* will clarify these are the scales of smiths dealing in brass and iron — the following specifications apply: *tluya ba'avir gimmel tefachim v'gvoha min ha'aretz gimmel tefachim* — the suspended scale must be at least three *tefachim* below the ceiling point from which it hangs, and at least three *tefachim* above the ground. *V'hakaneh v'hamachta shela yod-bet tefachim* — the bar (*kaneh*) and the ropes (*machta*, or *masna*) together measure twelve *tefachim*.

The *Rashbam* explains this as follows: the bar is four *tefachim* in length, and the ropes on each side are four *tefachim* each, yielding four plus four plus four equals twelve. The practical reasoning behind the ceiling and floor clearances is that heavy goods, when placed on one side of an unloaded scale, will cause a dramatic drop; if the pan swings too close to the ceiling at the top or the ground at the bottom, it will be obstructed, distorting the reading. There is also a physical principle at work — the longer the ropes, the more sensitive the scale, since any slight imbalance causes a larger visible arc of movement.

### Scale Sizes

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: נֶפֶשׁ מֹאזְנַיִם – תְּלוּיָה בָּאֲוִיר שְׁלֹשָׁה טְפָחִים, וּגְבוֹהָה מִן הָאָרֶץ שְׁלֹשָׁה טְפָחִים, וְקָנֶה וּמִתְנָא שֶׁלָּהּ שְׁנֵים עָשָׂר טְפָחִים. וְשֶׁל צַמָּרִים וְשֶׁל זַגָּגִין – תְּלוּיָה בָּאֲוִיר שְׁנֵי טְפָחִים, וּגְבוֹהָה מִן הָאָרֶץ שְׁנֵי טְפָחִים, וְקָנֶה וּמִתְנָא שֶׁלָּהּ תִּשְׁעָה טְפָחִים.
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught: The fulcrum of a scale** must be **suspended in the air** so that the point the lever goes through it is at a distance of **three handbreadths** from the ceiling, **and** the pans of the scale must be **three handbreadths above the ground. And** the scale’s **lever and cord,** from which each pan is suspended, must be **twelve handbreadths** long. **And** the fulcrum of a scale **of wool-weavers and glassmakers** must be **suspended in the air** at a distance of **two handbreadths** from the ceiling, **and** the pans of the scale must be **two handbreadths above the ground. And its lever and cord** must be **nine handbreadths** long.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְשֶׁל חֶנְווֹנִי וְשֶׁל בַּעַל הַבַּיִת – תְּלוּיָה בָּאֲוִיר טֶפַח, וּגְבוֹהָה מִן הָאָרֶץ טֶפַח, וְקָנֶה וּמִתְנָא שֶׁלָּהּ שִׁשָּׁה טְפָחִים. וְשֶׁל טוּרְטָנִי – תְּלוּיָה בָּאֲוִיר שָׁלֹשׁ אֶצְבָּעוֹת, וּגְבוֹהָה מִן הָאָרֶץ שָׁלֹשׁ אֶצְבָּעוֹת, וְקָנֶה וּמִתְנָא שֶׁלָּהּ אֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ.
> **Translation:** **And** the fulcrum of a scale **of a storekeeper and of a homeowner** must be **suspended in the air** at a distance of **one handbreadth** from the ceiling, **and** the pans of the scale must be **one handbreadth above the ground. And its lever and cord** must be **six handbreadths long. And** the fulcrums of small **scales [*turtanei*]** used for weighing gold and silver must be **suspended in the air** at a distance of **three fingerbreadths** from the ceiling, **and** the pans of the scales must be **three fingerbreadths above the ground.** The *tanna* continues: **But** with regard to **its lever and cord, I do not know** their required length.

For sellers of lighter goods — specifically wool merchants and glass sellers (it is notable that glass was sold by weight) — the specifications are reduced. The scale hangs two *tefachim* from the ceiling, stands two *tefachim* from the ground, and the combined bar and ropes measure nine *tefachim* (bar of three *tefachim* plus ropes of three *tefachim* on each side). For a retail storekeeper or private householder selling still lighter quantities, the scale need only be one *tefach* from ceiling and ground, with a combined bar and rope measure of six *tefachim* (bar of two *tefachim* plus ropes of two *tefachim* on each side).

### Metal Scales

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאֶלָּא הָךְ קַמַּיְיתָא – דְּמַאי?
> **Translation:** Since the function of each type of balance scale is mentioned with the exception of the first type, the Gemara asks: **And that** large balance scale, which is mentioned **first, for what** is it used?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַב פָּפָּא: דִּיגָרוֹמֵי.
> **Translation:** **Rav Pappa says:** It is a balance scale **for blacksmiths,** who weigh heavy pieces of metal.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבִּי מָנִי בַּר פַּטִּישׁ: כְּדֶרֶךְ שֶׁאָמְרוּ לְעִנְיַן אִיסּוּרָן, כָּךְ אָמְרוּ לְעִנְיַן טוּמְאָתָן.
> **Translation:** **Rabbi Mani Bar Pattish says: Just as** the Sages **said with regard to the prohibition of** the scales that one may not use a scale that does not meet the criteria listed in the *baraita*, **so** too **they said** that this applies **with regard to their ritual impurity.** In other words, if the cords and pole are not attached in the proper manner, they are not susceptible to ritual impurity as part of the scale.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן? תְּנֵינָא: חוּט מֹאזְנַיִם שֶׁל חֶנְוָנִי וְשֶׁל בַּעֲלֵי בָתִּים – טֶפַח! קָנֶה וּמִתְנָא שֶׁלָּהּ אִיצְטְרִיכָא לֵיהּ, דְּלָא תְּנַן.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is** this statement **teaching us? We learned** in a mishna (*Kelim* 29:5): With regard to the **rope** from which the **scales** are suspended, if the balance scale belongs **to a storekeeper or to homeowners** it must be **one handbreadth** in length for it to be susceptible to ritual impurity. Why, then, is the statement of Rabbi Mani Bar Pattish necessary? The Gemara answers: Although the mishna in tractate *Kelim* discusses the rope from which the scales are suspended, **it was** still **necessary for** Rabbi Mani Bar Pattish to mention the *halakha* with regard to the scale’s **lever and cord, which we did not learn about** in that mishna.

For the *turtani* — the seller of precious metals, essentially a smith or jeweler — the scale need only be three *etzba'ot* (fingers) from the ceiling and three *etzba'ot* from the ground, roughly half a *tefach*. For this scale, however, the *baraita* states: *vekaneh u'masna shelo eini yodea* — the dimensions of the bar and ropes are unknown. This is a striking admission. Why, having specified every other scale precisely, does the author not know this one? One might have expected that for precious metals — where the stakes of imprecision are highest — the specifications would be the most carefully defined. Indeed, one might reason that precisely because of the value involved, the ropes should be *longer*, not shorter, to maximize sensitivity: the longer the rope, the more sensitive the scale's response to small differences in weight, which is exactly what a dealer in precious metals requires. The *gemara* leaves this unresolved.

The *gemara* then turns to the largest scale specified — the one with a total of twelve *tefachim* across the bar and ropes, designed for the heaviest goods. *Amar Rav Pappa: degurmei* — Rav Pappa identifies this as the scale used by smiths of brass and iron, who sell large quantities of heavy, relatively low-value material, which is why the scale must be correspondingly large. Having now catalogued all the different sellers and their corresponding scale dimensions, *Amar Rav Mani bar Patish: kederech she'amru le'inyan issurin kach amru le'inyan tumasan* — just as these dimensions were stated with respect to the *issurin*, so too were they stated with respect to ritual impurity. It is worth pausing on the word *issurin* here: while we habitually associate it with ritual prohibition, in this context it refers to the prohibitions against cheating in business — the obligation to maintain just weights and measures. The scale's dimensions are not merely technical recommendations; they bear directly on whether the merchant is fulfilling that obligation. After all, dishonest weighing need not involve falsified weights; one can also rig the scale itself, or simply allow it to be less sensitive than it should be, in a way that systematically advantages the seller.

## Equipment Materials



### Pan Materials

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: אֵין עוֹשִׂין מִשְׁקָלוֹת – לֹא שֶׁל בַּעַץ, וְלֹא שֶׁל אֲבָר, וְלֹא שֶׁל גִּיסְטְרוֹן, וְלֹא שֶׁל שְׁאָר מִינֵי מַתָּכוֹת. אֲבָל עוֹשֶׂה הוּא שֶׁל צוּנְמָא וְשֶׁל זְכוּכִית.
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught: One may not prepare weights of tin [*ba’atz*], nor of lead, nor of a metal alloy [*gisteron*], nor of any other types of metal,** because all of these deteriorate over time and the buyer will ultimately pay for more merchandise than he receives. **But one** may **prepare** weights **of** hard **rock and of glass.**

A *baraita* rules: *ein osin mishkalos lo shel ba'atz v'lo shel avar v'lo shel gisteron v'lo shel shear minei matachos* — the pans of the scale (the *mishkalos*, and here the word refers to the pans, not the weights themselves) may not be made of tin, lead, mixed alloy (*gisteron*, per the *Rashbam*), or other metals. Instead, *ela oseh hu shel tsunma v'shel zechuchis* — one should make the pans of rock or glass.

It is worth pausing on the word *mishkalos* here. One might naturally read it as referring to the weights themselves — the standard masses placed on one side of the scale — but that is not what the *baraita* means at this point. The term refers to the pans, the receptacles into which goods are placed for weighing. That clarification matters for understanding what follows.

Why not metal pans? *Tosafot* cites Rabbeinu Tam's explanation: the concern is specifically for scales used to weigh meat, fat, and other moist goods — *shelachluchis midabek bahen u'misyabesh* — because the residue from such goods adheres to the metal pan and dries there, creating a buildup that affects subsequent weighings. This concern parallels the earlier *mishna*-level obligation to wipe and clean measuring vessels; even the pan itself must be constructed of material that does not retain residue. It is not, as Rashi suggested, a concern about the metal wearing away — these are hard metals and will not abrade — but a concern about the *accumulation* of material on them.

### Leather Linings

An exception exists: *aval mishkalos sheshoklim bahen kesef v'zahav mutar la'asosan shel mataches ela shetzarich lachaposo or* — for scales used to weigh gold and silver, metal pans are permitted, because precious metal transactions do not involve moist goods and therefore carry no residue concern. However, these pans must be lined with leather, for a different reason: without a protective lining, the gold or silver being weighed might scrape against the metal pan and lose minute quantities in the process. The leather lining protects the integrity of the metal being weighed — much as precious gems and metals are presented today in velvet-lined cases to protect their surface.

### Cleaning Tools

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: אֵין עוֹשִׂין הַמֶּחָק שֶׁל דְּלַעַת – מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא קַל, וְלֹא שֶׁל מַתֶּכֶת – מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא מַכְבִּיד. אֲבָל עוֹשֵׂהוּ שֶׁל זַיִת וְשֶׁל אֱגוֹז, שֶׁל שִׁקְמָה וְשֶׁל אֶשְׁבְּרוֹעַ.
> **Translation:** **The Sages** further **taught: One may not prepare the leveler,** used to remove the excess from the mouth of a vessel, **from a gourd, because it is a light** material and does not level effectively, thereby causing a loss for the seller. **And** it may **not** be made **of metal, because it weighs down** and removes too much of the merchandise, leading to a loss for the buyer. **But one** may **prepare it from** the wood **of an olive** tree, **or of a nut** tree, or **of a sycamore** tree, **or of a boxwood** tree, which are of medium weight.

The *baraita* next addresses the *machak* — the tool used to clean out a measuring container (such as a one-liter vessel used to measure wine or grain). *Ein osim hamachak shel dala'at* — one may not make it of a gourd, *mipnei shehu kal* — because it is too light and will not do a thorough job. *Velo shel matechet* — nor may it be made of metal, *mipnei shehu machbid* — because it is too heavy and may scrape away at the walls of the measuring vessel itself, enlarging its interior volume and thereby disadvantaging the seller in future transactions.

### Uniform Cleaning

The *baraita* further specifies: *ein osim et hamachak tziddo echad av vetziddo echad katzar* — one may not make the *machak* with one side wide and one side narrow. The *Rashbam* explains that a narrower edge can get into the nooks and crevices of the vessel more thoroughly, while a wider edge will clean less completely. If the tool has one of each, both buyer and seller have an interest in which end is used: the buyer will want the narrow end used to ensure the full volume is available, while the seller will prefer the wide end. This would reintroduce the very asymmetry the halacha seeks to prevent. The *machak* must be uniform, so that the manner of cleaning is consistent and neither party can manipulate it to their advantage.

### Cleaning Technique

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: אֵין עוֹשִׂין אֶת הַמֶּחָק צִדּוֹ אֶחָד עָב וְצִדּוֹ אֶחָד קָצָר. לֹא יִמְחוֹק בְּבַת אַחַת, שֶׁהַמּוֹחֵק בְּבַת אַחַת – רַע לַמּוֹכֵר וְיָפֶה לַלּוֹקֵחַ; וְלֹא יִמְחוֹק מְעַט מְעַט – שֶׁרַע לַלּוֹקֵחַ וְיָפֶה לַמּוֹכֵר.
> **Translation:** **The Sages taught: One may not prepare the leveler** in such a manner that **one of its sides is thick and one** other **side is thin,** because in such a case the two sides will not level equally. Furthermore, **one may not level all at once,** by a single quick movement, **as one who levels all at once** acts in a manner that is **bad for the seller and good for the buyer,** because he removes less of the excess than one who levels in the regular fashion. **And** conversely **one may not level little by little,** i.e., with several slow movements, **as** this is **bad for the buyer and good for the seller.**

Beyond the tool's construction, the *baraita* governs the speed of its application. *V'lo yimchok bevat achat shehamochek bevat achat ra lamocher* — one may not clean in a single rapid swipe, because a quick clean is disadvantageous to the seller. Nor, in the opposite direction, *v'lo yimchok me'at me'at she'ra lalokeach veyofeh lamocher* — may one clean very slowly and repeatedly, which disadvantages the buyer. The text here presents some difficulty: intuitively, one might have expected the directionality to run the other way — a quick clean that leaves residue would seem to harm the buyer by leaving less volume available, while an overly thorough clean would harm the seller. The *Rashbam*'s explanation of this passage is puzzling, and several attempts to re-read the passage as referring to the pans or the weights themselves (rather than the measuring container) do not resolve the difficulty cleanly.

### Rabban Yochanan

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** עַל כּוּלָּן אָמַר רַבָּן יוֹחָנָן בֶּן זַכַּאי: אוֹי לִי אִם אוֹמַר, אוֹי לִי אִם לֹא אוֹמַר; אִם אוֹמַר – שֶׁמָּא יִלְמְדוּ הָרַמָּאִין, וְאִם לֹא אוֹמַר – שֶׁמָּא יֹאמְרוּ הָרַמָּאִין: אֵין תַּלְמִידֵי חֲכָמִים בְּקִיאִין בְּמַעֲשֵׂה יָדֵינוּ. אִיבַּעְיָא לְהוּ: אַמְרַהּ, אוֹ לָא אַמְרַהּ? אֲמַר רַב שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר רַב יִצְחָק: אַמְרַהּ – וּמֵהַאי קְרָא אַמְרַהּ: ״כִּי יְשָׁרִים דַּרְכֵי ה׳, וְצַדִּקִים יֵלְכוּ בָם, וּפֹשְׁעִים יִכָּשְׁלוּ בָם״.
> **Translation:** **Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai said with regard to all** these *halakhot*: **Woe to me if I say** them, and **woe unto me if I do not say** them. **If I say** them, **perhaps swindlers will learn** new methods of cheating of which they were previously unaware. **And if I do not say** them, **perhaps swindlers will say: Torah scholars are not well versed in our handiwork. A dilemma was raised before** the Sages: **Did** Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai decide to **say** these *halakhot* in public **or did he not say** them? **Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzḥak says: He said them, and he said them on** the basis of **this verse: “For the ways of the Lord are right, and the just walk in them; but transgressors stumble over them”** (Hosea 14:10).

On all of these practices — each of which describes a method by which one party could subtly cheat the other — *Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai* made a famous remark, preserved in a *mishna* in *Keilim*: *oy li im omer, oy li im lo omer* — "Woe to me if I say it, and woe to me if I do not say it." The dilemma: *im omer, shema yilmedu ha-rama'in* — if he publicizes these methods of cheating, he risks teaching the cheaters new tricks they had not thought of. But *v'im lo omer, shema yomru ha-rama'in ein talmidei chachamim beki'in b'ma'asei yadeinu* — if he stays silent, the cheaters will say that the *talmidei chachamim* have no idea how they operate, and they will continue with impunity.

The *gemara* records a dispute about what Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai ultimately decided. Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak rules: *amrah* — he did say it, and he based his decision on the *pasuk*, *ki yesharim darchei Hashem, tzaddikim yelchu vam u'fosha'im yikashlu vam* — "the paths of God are straight; the righteous walk in them and the wicked stumble upon them." Torah knowledge is meant to guide the righteous, and that purpose cannot be abandoned because the wicked might misuse it. The disclosure of cheating methods is necessary for honest people to protect themselves, and the responsibility for its misuse lies with the one who misuses it.

## Scripture Derivations



### Land Measurement

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״לֹא תַעֲשׂוּ עָוֶל בַּמִּשְׁפָּט; בַּמִּדָּה, בַּמִּשְׁקָל וּבַמְּשׂוּרָה״; ״בַּמִּדָּה״ – זוֹ מְדִידַת קַרְקַע, שֶׁלֹּא יִמְדּוֹד לְאֶחָד בִּימוֹת הַחַמָּה וּלְאֶחָד בִּימוֹת הַגְּשָׁמִים. ״בַּמִּשְׁקָל״ – שֶׁלֹּא יַטְמִין מִשְׁקְלוֹתָיו בְּמֶלַח. ״בַּמְּשׂוּרָה״ – שֶׁלֹּא יַרְתִּיחַ.
> **Translation:** § The Gemara further discusses weights and measures. **The Sages taught: “You shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in measure [*bammidda*], in weight, or in measure [*uvamesura*]”** (Leviticus 19:35). The *baraita* defines these terms: **“In measure [*bammidda*],” this** is referring to **measuring land,** teaching **that** in order to measure land in a just manner **one may not measure for one** person **in the summer** when the measuring rope has become dry and short, **and for one** other individual **in the rainy season,** when the measuring rope is wet and limp, and therefore stretches more. **“In weight”** means **that one may not cover his weights in salt,** as salt erodes the weights, causing a loss for the buyer. **“Or in measure [*bamesura*]”** means **that one may not cause** liquid he is measuring **to foam** by pouring it speedily, as this results in a loss for the buyer, who receives less of the liquid than the amount for which he paid.

The *baraita* now turns to the verse *lo sa'asu avel bamishpat bamiddah bamishkal u'vamsurah* — "you shall not do injustice in judgment, in measure, in weight, or in liquid measure." The *baraita* expounds each term. *B'middah* — in measure — refers to land measurement: *shelo yimdod l'echad biyemos hachamah v'la'echad biyemos hageshamim* — one may not measure land for one party in the summer and for another in the rainy season. The reason is that measuring ropes absorb moisture in the rainy season and stretch, so the same rope will give a different reading in winter than in summer. Using different seasonal conditions for different parties is a form of deception, even if the physical instrument — the rope — is the same object.

### Weight Storage

*B'mishkal* — in weights — teaches: *shelo yatmin mishklosav bamelach* — one may not store the weights in salt. *Rashi* interprets this as a concern that salt makes the weights heavier, which would benefit the buyer at the seller's expense. *Tosafot*, however, cites Rabbeinu Tam's objection: the context, by analogy to the subsequent liquid-measure case, is clearly speaking about the seller's conduct — *dumya d'vamsurah, shelo yartiah*, just as the liquid-measure prohibition targets the seller's manipulation, so too here. Rabbeinu Tam therefore explains that salt actually makes the weights *lighter* — it draws out moisture from the material, reducing their mass — which benefits the seller at the buyer's expense. There is also a terminological point worth noting: the word *mishklosav* here refers to the weights themselves, not the pans of the scale, which is how the term was used earlier. This matters because *Rashi*'s reading — that salt makes things heavier — would make more sense if *mishklosav* referred to the pans, since it would then depend on which side of the scale was being affected. Rabbeinu Tam's reinterpretation of both the direction of the physical effect and the commercial context hang together: once we understand that the subject is the seller and the object is the weights themselves, the ruling coheres as a straightforward prohibition on fraudulent underweighting.

### Liquid Measures

*U'vamsurah* — in liquid measures — the *baraita* teaches: *shelo yartiah* — one may not pour with froth. Pouring frothy liquid into a measure means that part of what is being sold is foam rather than substance; the buyer is receiving less than the full volume he paid for. Notably, it is not the measuring vessel itself that is false, but the manner of using it — and the transgression is still prohibited by this *pasuk*.

### Tosafot Question

*Tosafot* raises the question that applies to all of these cases: *pshita d'gezel gamur hu* — obviously, each of these practices constitutes outright theft. Why does the Torah need a special *pasuk* to prohibit them? Does *lo tigzol* not already cover every case? *Tosafot*'s answer is precise: *d'asa kra l'chayeiv m'shaat hartichah af al pi she'adayin lo madad* — the *pasuk* creates liability from the *moment of the improper act* even before the actual theft is consummated. In the case of pouring with froth, the transgression begins the moment one pours with froth, even before one has collected payment or completed the transaction. A parallel formulation appears in the discussion in *Eizehu Neshech*: *lav d'chsiv b'mishkalos lama li litmon mishkalos even u'melach hagzeilah mealyei hi* — if you are maintaining fraudulent weights, that is already full-fledged *gezeilah*, so why the extra *lav*? The answer there too is: *laavor lav m'shaat asiyah* — you transgress the prohibition at the moment you *create* the fraudulent weights, before you have used them in any transaction at all.

### Kal Vachomer

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְקַל וָחוֹמֶר – וּמָה מְשׂוּרָה, שֶׁהִיא אֶחָד מִשְּׁלֹשִׁים וְשִׁשָּׁה בַּלּוֹג – הִקְפִּידָה עָלָיו תּוֹרָה; קַל וָחוֹמֶר לְהִין, וַחֲצִי הִין, וּשְׁלִישִׁית הַהִין, וּרְבִיעִית הַהִין; וְלוֹג, וַחֲצִי לוֹג, וּרְבִיעִית, וַחֲצִי תּוֹמֶן, וְעוּכְלָא.
> **Translation:** The Gemara notes: **And** it can be inferred by means of an ***a fortiori*** inference **that if** in the case of **a *mesura*, which is** a measure equivalent to merely **one thirty-sixth of a *log*, the Torah was particular that** one must measure honestly, so too, ***a fortiori*,** one must be careful in the case **of one *hin*, and a half-*hin*, and a third-*hin*, and a quarter-*hin*,** which is twelve *log*, **and one *log*, and a half-*log*, and a quarter-*log***, **and a half-*tomen*,** i.e., one-sixteenth of a *kav*, **and** even **an *ukla*,** a smaller unit, as defined below.

The *baraita* closes with a *kal vachomer*: *v'kal vachomer mah mesurah she'hi echad mishloshim v'shishah b'log hikpid alav Torah* — the *mesurah* is one of the smallest liquid measures, one thirty-sixth of a *log*, an amount whose monetary value is negligible in absolute terms. Yet the Torah is exacting about even this tiny measure. *Kal vachomer* — how much more so with the larger measures listed in the *baraita* — the *hin*, the half-*hin*, the third-*hin*, the quarter-*hin*, the *log*, the half-*log*, the quarter-*log*, the half-*tuman*, the *uchla* — each of which represents a more substantial volume, and correspondingly greater potential for harm when used dishonestly. The daf ends here, and the exposition of these various measures will continue in the next session.
