## Warnings & Severity



### God's Name Erased

The Gemara opens with a discussion of the foundational concern underlying the *sotah* ritual: the erasure of God's name. The *mishnah* had established that the *beit din* impresses upon the woman the severity of what she is about to undergo — just as witnesses in capital cases are solemnly warned — because of the prohibition against erasing God's name unnecessarily. The relevant phrase is *assur lishmo hagadol shenichtav bikdushah shelo yimcha al hamayim*: the great name, written in holiness, must not be erased over the water without cause.

This concern is central to understanding the *sotah* ritual's inner logic. Part of the ritual's profound beauty, as reflected in *aggadata*, is that God is willing to have His name erased in order to restore *shalom bayit* between a husband and wife. Nevertheless, the prohibition against erasing God's name remains in force, and so the *beit din* works to prevent that erasure wherever possible.

### Paradox of Warnings

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וּמְאַיְּימִין עָלֶיהָ וְכוּ׳. וּרְמִינְהוּ: כְּדֶרֶךְ שֶׁמְּאַיְּימִין עָלֶיהָ שֶׁלֹּא תִּשְׁתֶּה כָּךְ מְאַיְּימִין עָלֶיהָ שֶׁתִּשְׁתֶּה. אוֹמְרִים לָהּ: בִּתִּי, אִם בָּרוּר לָךְ הַדָּבָר שֶׁטְּהוֹרָה אַתְּ עִמְדִי עַל בּוּרְיִיךְ, וּשְׁתִי. לְפִי שֶׁאֵין מַיִם הַמָּרִים דּוֹמִין אֶלָּא לְסַם יָבֵשׁ שֶׁמּוּנָּח עַל בָּשָׂר חַי. אִם יֵשׁ שָׁם מַכָּה — מְחַלְחֵל וְיוֹרֵד, אֵין שָׁם מַכָּה — אֵינוֹ מוֹעִיל כְּלוּם.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And they threaten her** in order that she admit her sin, to obviate the need to erase God’s name. **And** the Gemara **raises a contradiction** from that which was taught in a *baraita* in the *Tosefta* (1:6): **In the same manner that they threaten her so that she will not drink, so too, they threaten her so that she will drink,** as **they say to her: My daughter, if the matter is clear to you that you are pure, arise for** the sake of **your clear** position **and drink.** If you are innocent you have nothing to fear, **because the bitter water is similar only to a dry poison placed on the flesh. If there is a wound** there, the poison will **penetrate and enter** the blood stream, but if **there is no wound there, it does not have any effect.** This teaches that the woman is warned not to drink if she is guilty, but if she is not guilty she is encouraged to drink. There is no mention of the latter in the mishna.


The Gemara then raises what appears to be a sharp contradiction. A *baraita* from the *Tosefta* expands on the notion of warning the woman and states: *kederech shema'amin aleha shelo tishteh kach ma'amin aleha shetishteh* — just as we warn her not to drink, so too we warn her to drink. This seems paradoxical. If the entire purpose of warning her is so that she will not go through with the ritual and thereby cause God's name to be erased, what could it possibly mean to warn her to drink?

The *baraita* itself elaborates: we tell her, "If it is clear to you that you are innocent, stand firm and drink, because the bitter water is like a dry medicine placed on raw flesh — if there is a wound, it dissolves into it and takes effect; if there is no wound, it does nothing." In other words, if she is innocent, the water will have no effect and it is actually beneficial for her to proceed with the ritual. The destruction of God's name will then serve a purpose.

### Before/After Erasure

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לָא קַשְׁיָא: כָּאן — קוֹדֶם שֶׁנִּמְחֲקָה מְגִילָּה. כָּאן — לְאַחַר שֶׁנִּמְחֲקָה מְגִילָּה.
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: This is **not difficult. Here** the mishna is referring to **before the scroll was erased,** and at that point the woman is warned only not to drink if she is guilty, so that the name of God will not be erased. **There** the *baraita* is referring to **after the scroll was erased.** Then she is warned that if she is innocent she should drink because if she now refuses to drink, it will turn out that the scroll was erased for no purpose.

The Gemara resolves the apparent contradiction: *lo kashya kan kodem shenimchakah hamegillah kan le'achar shenimchakah hamegillah* — there is no difficulty, because these are two distinct stages. Before the *Megillat Sotah* has been erased into the water, the *beit din* tries to encourage the woman to admit guilt, so that the erasure of God's name is avoided altogether. Once the *megillah* has already been erased, however, we do not want that erasure to have been in vain, and so we now encourage her to drink and affirm her innocence, telling her the water will benefit rather than harm her.

The *mishnah* had stated that they say before her *devarim she'einam kedai lishoman* — things that are not suitable to be heard, yet are said to her anyway. The Gemara clarifies that these are *devarim shel aggadah*, aggadic narratives, stories that will move her emotionally. Why are they described as *einam kedai lishoman*? That becomes clear as the Gemara provides its examples.

### Judah & Reuben

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאוֹמֵר לְפָנֶיהָ וְכוּ׳. תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: אוֹמֵר לְפָנֶיהָ דְּבָרִים שֶׁל הַגָּדָה, וּמַעֲשִׂים שֶׁאֵירְעוּ בִּכְתוּבִים הָרִאשׁוֹנִים, כְּגוֹן: ״אֲשֶׁר חֲכָמִים יַגִּידוּ וְלֹא כִחֲדוּ מֵאֲבוֹתָם״.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And** the judge **says in her presence** matters that are not worthy of being heard by her and all her father’s family in order to encourage her to admit her sin. The Gemara cites a *baraita* that details what was said. **The Sages taught** in a *baraita*: The judge **says in her presence words of homiletical** interpretation **and** mentions **incidents that happened** to previous generations that are recorded **in the early** prophetic **writings. For example,** they expound the following verse: **“That wise men told and did not hide from their fathers”** (Job 15:18); this teaches that even during the time of the forefathers, there were people who admitted their sins despite the shame they incurred.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יְהוּדָה הוֹדָה וְלֹא בּוֹשׁ, מֶה הָיָה סוֹפוֹ — נָחַל חַיֵּי הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא. רְאוּבֵן הוֹדָה וְלֹא בּוֹשׁ, מֶה הָיָה סוֹפוֹ — נָחַל חַיֵּי הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא. וּמָה שְׂכָרָן? מָה שְׂכָרָן?! כִּדְקָא אָמְרִינַן! אֶלָּא: מָה שְׂכָרָן בָּעוֹלָם הַזֶּה, ״לָהֶם לְבַדָּם נִתְּנָה הָאָרֶץ וְלֹא עָבַר זָר בְּתוֹכָם״.
> **Translation:** For example, **Judah admitted** that he sinned with Tamar **and was not embarrassed** to do so, and **what was his end? He inherited the life of the World-to-Come. Reuben admitted** that he lay with his father’s concubine Bilhah **and was not embarrassed,** and **what was his end? He** too **inherited the life of the World-to-Come.** The Gemara asks: **And what is their reward?** The Gemara interjects: **What is their reward?** Their reward was clearly **as we say,** that they inherited the life of the World-to-Come. The Gemara clarifies: **Rather,** the second question was: **What is their reward in this world?** The Gemara answers by citing the next verse in the book of Job: **“To them alone the land was given, and no stranger passed among them”** (Job 15:19). Judah was given the kingship, and Reuben inherited a portion of land in the Transjordan before the other tribes.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא בִּיהוּדָה אַשְׁכְּחַן דְּאוֹדִי, דִּכְתִיב: ״וַיַּכֵּר יְהוּדָה וַיֹּאמֶר צָדְקָה מִמֶּנִּי״, אֶלָּא רְאוּבֵן מְנָלַן דְּאוֹדִי?
> **Translation:** The Gemara questions the source for Reuben’s admission. **Granted, with regard to Judah we have found** a source **that he admitted** his sin with Tamar, **as it is written: “And Judah acknowledged them and said: She is more righteous than I”** (Genesis 38:26). Judah admitted that he was the one who had impregnated Tamar. **But from where do we** derive **that Reuben admitted** his sin?

The Gemara's *baraita* specifies the content of these narratives. We tell her stories from the earlier writings, from the lives of the founding figures of Israel, citing a verse from Iyov: *asher chakhamim yagidu velo kichadu me'avotam* — the wise ones have spoken of these things and have not hidden them from their ancestors. Two paradigmatic examples are then given.

*Yehudah hodah velo bosh* — Yehuda admitted his sin and was not embarrassed. *Mah hayah sofo? Nachal chayei ha'olam haba* — his end was that he inherited the life of the World to Come. Similarly, *Reuven hodeh velo bosh mah hayah sofo nachal chayei ha'olam haba* — Reuven admitted his sin and was not embarrassed, and he too inherited the World to Come.

Here the rabbis are making use of the *pshat* reading of narratives that, from the perspective of *Torah she-be-al peh*, are generally understood more carefully. We are told in *Megillah* that the story of Reuven is not translated into *Targum* during public reading, precisely so that people should not be misled by the *pshat* understanding — which sounds as though Reuven literally slept with his stepmother, while *Chazal* maintain the incident was something less severe. Yet here, the Gemara is explicitly drawing on the *pshat* reading of these stories as emotionally powerful examples of repentance and admission of guilt. This reflects a broader tension in rabbinic thought about how to read *Tanach* and how much to engage with the personalities of its protagonists as real, flawed human beings capable of sin and *teshuvah*.

The reason these narratives are *einam kedai lishoman* — not suitable to be heard — is precisely because they reflect badly on great *tzaddikim* or on figures who appear prominently in the *Torah*. Yet they are told to the *sotah* anyway, because inspiring her to confess and repent overrides that concern. The *Rema* cites this principle in one of his *teshuvot*, noting that the concern of embarrassing a *talmid chakham* can be overridden in the context of a process aimed at eliciting confession and restoring *shalom bayit*.

### Judah's Punishment

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּאָמַר רַבִּי שְׁמוּאֵל בַּר נַחְמָנִי אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מַאי דִּכְתִיב ״יְחִי רְאוּבֵן וְאַל יָמֹת״, ״וְזֹאת לִיהוּדָה״?
> **Translation:** The Gemara answers: It is **as Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani says** that **Rabbi Yoḥanan says: What is** the meaning of that **which is written** concerning Reuben and Judah in Moses’ blessing of the tribes at the end of his life: **“Let Reuben live and not die in that his men become few”** (Deuteronomy 33:6), and immediately afterward, in the following verse, it is stated: **“And this for Judah,** and he said: Hear, Lord, the voice of Judah, and bring him in unto his people; his hands shall contend for him, and You shall be a help against his adversaries” (Deuteronomy 33:7). What is the connection between the blessing of Reuben and that of Judah, juxtaposed with the conjunction “and”?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** כׇּל אוֹתָן שָׁנִים שֶׁהָיוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל בַּמִּדְבָּר, הָיוּ עַצְמוֹתָיו שֶׁל יְהוּדָה מְגוּלְגָּלִין בָּאָרוֹן, עַד שֶׁעָמַד מֹשֶׁה וּבִקֵּשׁ עָלָיו רַחֲמִים. אָמַר לְפָנָיו: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, מִי גָּרַם לִרְאוּבֵן שֶׁהוֹדָה — יְהוּדָה: ״וְזֹאת לִיהוּדָה״?!
> **Translation:** Rabbi Yoḥanan says: **All those years that the Jewish people were in the desert, the bones of Judah,** which the Jewish people took with them from Egypt along with the bones of his brothers, **were rolling** around **in the coffin, until Moses arose and asked for compassion on** Judah’s behalf. Moses **said before** God: **Master of the Universe, who served as the impetus for Reuben that he admit** his sin, through which he merited a blessing and was not excluded from the count of the twelve sons of Jacob (see Genesis 35:22)? It was **Judah,** as Reuben saw him confess his sin, and thereby did the same. Moses continues in the next verse: **“And this for Judah,”** as if to say: Is this Judah’s reward for serving as an example of confessing to one’s sins, that his bones roll around?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מִיָּד: ״שְׁמַע ה׳ קוֹל יְהוּדָה״, עָל אֵיבְרֵיהּ לְשָׁפָא. וְלָא הֲוָה קָא מְעַיְּילִין לֵיהּ לִמְתִיבְתָּא דִרְקִיעָא, ״וְאֶל עַמּוֹ תְּבִיאֶנּוּ״. וְלָא הֲוָה קָא יָדַע מִשְׁקַל וּמִטְרַח בִּשְׁמַעְתָּא בַּהֲדֵי רַבָּנַן, ״יָדָיו רָב לוֹ״. לָא הֲוָה קָא סָלְקָא לֵיהּ שְׁמַעְתָּא אַלִּיבָּא דְהִילְכְתָא, ״וְעֵזֶר מִצָּרָיו תִּהְיֶה״.
> **Translation:** **Immediately** after Moses prayed, the verse states: **“Hear, Lord, the voice of Judah”** (Deuteronomy 33:7). **His bones** then **entered** their **sockets [*shafa*],** and his skeleton was reassembled. **But** the angels still **did not elevate** him **into the heavenly study hall.** Moses then prayed: **“And bring him in unto his people”** (Deuteronomy 33:7), i.e., those in the heavenly study hall. This prayer was accepted, **but he** still **did not know** how **to deliberate** in Torah matters **with the** heavenly **sages.** Moses then prayed: **“His hands shall contend for him”** (Deuteronomy 33:7), meaning that he should have the ability to contend with them in study. But still **he was unable to draw** conclusions from **his discussion in accordance with the *halakha*.** Moses then prayed: **“And You shall be a help against his adversaries”** (Deuteronomy 33:7).

The Gemara then asks: we said both Yehuda and Reuven inherited *Olam Haba* — what further reward is there to discuss? *Ela mah secharan ba'olam hazeh* — what was their reward even in this world?

For Reuven, the *pasuk lahem levadam nitnah ha'aretz* hints at his being among the tribes who received the first inheritance on the east side of the *Yarden*, a share in *Eretz Yisrael* that was his own. For Yehuda, we know he admitted his sin because the *pasuk* says explicitly: *vayakker Yehuda vayomer tzadkah mimmeni* — Yehuda recognized and said, "She is more righteous than I." But how do we know that Reuven admitted his sin? The Gemara cites Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: what does it mean when Moshe blesses Reuven at the end of *Sefer Devarim*, saying *yechi Reuven ve'al yamot*, "Reuven shall live and not die"? And immediately following is the blessing for Yehuda, introduced with the unusual word *vezot* — a connection that the Gemara draws upon to link the two.

The Gemara then presents a striking teaching: *kol otan shanim shehayu Yisrael bamidbar hayu atzmotav shel Yehuda mitgalgelin ba'aron* — throughout the forty years that the Jewish people were in the desert, the bones of Yehuda were rolling around in the ark, dislocated, until Moshe prayed on his behalf. The specific punishment was *eivarei deshapa* — his limbs were disjointed from their sockets — an image that Rashi connects to the *treifah* discussed in *Chullin* on daf 42b, *buka d'atma deshaph midukhtei*, the dislocated hip joint. Even in death, Yehuda was punished for his role in the episode with Tamar.

What prompted Moshe's prayer? *Amar lefanav Ribbono shel Olam mi garam leReuven shehodeh* — who was it that caused Reuven to do *teshuvah*? It was Yehuda. Yehuda's character, his willingness to admit wrongdoing publicly, inspired Reuven's own *teshuvah*. And so it was not even Yehuda's own *teshuvah* directly that earned him this redemption, but the fact that his example had inspired another person to repent. This is a striking teaching about the ripple effects of genuine admission of sin.

The Gemara then parses the blessing of Yehuda in *parashat v'zot habracha* as a series of remedies for the punishments Yehuda had suffered. *Shema Adonai kol Yehuda* — God heard Yehuda's cry, releasing him from his dislocated bones. *V'el amo tevi'enu* — bring him back to his people, meaning they finally allowed him into the *yeshivah* of heaven, the *metivta d'rakiya*, from which he had been excluded. *Yadav rav lo* — his hands were strengthened, corresponding to his now being permitted to participate in the *shakla v'tarya*, the back-and-forth of debate in the heavenly *beit midrash*, from which he had previously been barred even after gaining entry. And *v'ezer mizarav tihyeh* — let there be help against his enemies, meaning he could finally arrive at halakhic conclusions, *aliba d'hilkhata*, in his learning.

### Reuben's Public Apology

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** בִּשְׁלָמָא יְהוּדָה דְּאוֹדִי, כִּי הֵיכִי דְּלֹא תִּישָּׂרֵף תָּמָר. אֶלָּא רְאוּבֵן, לְמָה לֵיהּ דְּאוֹדִי? וְהָאָמַר רַב שֵׁשֶׁת: חֲצִיף עֲלַי דִּמְפָרֵיט חֶטְאֵיהּ? כִּי הֵיכִי דְּלָא לִיחַשְׁדוּ אֲחוֹהִי.
> **Translation:** The Gemara discusses the propriety of admitting one’s sins in public. **Granted,** with regard to **Judah,** it was proper **that he admitted** his sin in public, as he did so **in order that Tamar not be burned** innocently. **But why did Reuben admit** his sin in public? **But didn’t Rav Sheshet say: I** consider one **who specifies his sins** in public to be **brazen,** as one who does so indicates that he is not embarrassed by his actions? The Gemara answers: The reason he admitted his sin in public was **in order that his brothers should not be suspected** of having committed the deed.

The Gemara then returns to Reuven and poses a question. *Bishlama biYehuda ashkechan de'odei* — we understand why Yehuda had to make a public admission of his sin: *ki heichi d'lo tisoraf Tamar*, so that Tamar would not be burned. Yehuda had no choice but to speak publicly to save her life. But why did Reuven need to make a public admission? *Lama lei de'odei* — there was seemingly no practical purpose; no one's life was at stake. Moreover, does not Rav Sheshet say that it is brazen to publicize one's own sins, *chatzip alai diprat chataei*?

The Gemara answers: *ki heichi d'lo lyechshadu achvohi* — Reuven was concerned that if he did not publicly acknowledge what he had done, his brothers would fall under suspicion. Something had become known, but without knowing who was responsible, people might have suspected any of his brothers. By making a public declaration, he ensured that no one else would be considered *chashud* for that sin.

This reflects the broader principle, noted especially in the *Rambam*, that for sins *bein adam lachavero*, there is an obligation to publicly acknowledge and apologize, because another person has been hurt and public rectification is part of the repair. For sins *bein adam lamakom*, by contrast, it is generally preferable to keep the matter private, between the person and God, without unnecessary public disclosure. Reuven's case was exceptional: the public nature of his admission was not self-serving, but was required specifically to protect his brothers from wrongful suspicion.

## Woman's Confession



### Ketubah Receipt

The Gemara then transitions to the case in which the woman admits to having committed adultery. *Im amra tmea ani* — if she says she is impure, *shovrim ktubatah* — we break her *ketubah*. The question is what exactly this means. *Shmaat mina kotvim shovar* — does it mean we write a receipt acknowledging that the *ketubah* has been settled, leaving the original document in place? Or does it mean something else?

Abaye proposes emending the reading: *tni mikrat*, we should read it as *koraat*, she tears up the *ketubah*. If you tear up the *shtar* itself, the instrument of debt is destroyed and cannot be claimed. Rava objects: *veha shoveret katani* — the *mishnah* uses the word *shoveret*, which implies breaking, not tearing. You cannot simply change the *mishnah*'s language.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִם אָמְרָה ״טְמֵאָה אֲנִי״ וְכוּ׳. שָׁמְעַתְּ מִינַּהּ כּוֹתְבִין שׁוֹבָר.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **If** after the judge’s warning **she says: I am defiled,** she writes a receipt for her marriage contract. The Gemara comments: **You** can **learn from this** mishna **that one writes a receipt** to serve as proof that a debt has been paid rather than tearing the promissory note. This matter is the subject of a dispute between the *tanna’im* in tractate *Bava Batra* (170b).

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי, תְּנִי: ״מְקָרַעַת״. אֲמַר לֵיהּ רָבָא: וְהָא ״שׁוֹבֶרֶת״ קָתָנֵי! אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא: בִּמְקוֹם שֶׁאֵין כּוֹתְבִין כְּתוּבָּה עָסְקִינַן.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said: Teach** in the mishna differently. Rather than understanding that she writes a receipt, explain it to mean: **She tears** her marriage contract. **Rava said to him: But** the mishna **teaches** explicitly that **she writes a receipt. Rather,** to explain the mishna, **Rava said: We are dealing with a place in** which **they do not write a marriage contract,** as they rely on the rabbinical ordinance that all wives are entitled to the sum of a standard marriage contract upon divorce or being widowed, even if no marriage contract has been written. Because there is no marriage contract to tear, a receipt is written so that the man can prove that he no longer has a monetary obligation. However, generally, it is possible that the document would be torn, and no proof can be adduced from this mishna.

### Ketubah Document


Rava therefore offers a different resolution: *bemakom she'ein kotvim ktubah askinan* — we are discussing a locale where it was not the practice to write a physical document for the *ketubah*. The *ketubah* obligation existed, but was conducted entirely orally. In such a case, there is no document to tear. The *mishnah*'s language of *shovrim* then means that we write a receipt — a *shovar* — to formally dissolve her claim.

This is a noteworthy window into the historical development of the *ketubah* as an institution. The *sugyot* across *Shas* present a complex picture: in some places the *ketubah* appears *d'Oraita*, in others *d'Rabbanan*. The *Rambam* at the beginning of *Hilchot Ishut* formulates it strikingly, stating that the *mitzvah* of *kiddushin* requires marriage *bekiddushin uvektubah* — the *ketubah* is part of the *mitzvah* of *kiddushin* itself.

Rashi at the bottom of the page notes two alternatives for how, in a place without a written *ketubah*, a woman would collect her entitlement. If divorced, *mishenitgarsha motzia gita vegova ba* — she brings the *get* itself as proof of her status and collects on the basis of that. If widowed, *u'chshenitalmena mevia edei mita vegova* — she must bring witnesses who testify to her husband's death before she can claim from his estate. This latter process is considerably more burdensome, requiring *edei mita* rather than simply producing a document. It is also worth noting that in monetary matters generally, the principle of *hamotzi mechavero alav haraaya* applies — the burden of proof falls on the one seeking to extract money from another — making this already a more demanding standard than simply presenting a *shtar*. In a certain sense, one can trace in this the practical impetus for institutionalizing the written *ketubah*: the difficulty for a widow of having to produce testimony made it far simpler, ultimately, to give the woman a *shtar* she could hold in her hand.

## Eastern Gate Ritual



### Nikanor Gate

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאִם אָמְרָה ״טְהוֹרָה אֲנִי״ מַעֲלִין אוֹתָהּ לְשַׁעֲרֵי מִזְרָח. מַעֲלִין אוֹתָהּ?!
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **But if** after the warning **she** maintains her innocence and **says: I am pure, they** would **bring her up to the Eastern Gate.** The Gemara asks: Would **they bring her up?**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָתָם קָיְימָא! דְּמַסְּקִינַן לַהּ וּמַחֲתִינַן לַהּ כְּדֵי לְיַיגְּעָהּ. דְּתַנְיָא רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן אֶלְעָזָר אוֹמֵר: בֵּית דִּין מַסִּיעִין אֶת הָעֵדִים מִמָּקוֹם לְמָקוֹם, כְּדֵי שֶׁתִּטָּרֵף דַּעְתָּן עֲלֵיהֶן וְיַחְזְרוּ בָּהֶן.
> **Translation:** **She is** already **standing there** in the Temple courtyard, as that is where the Sanhedrin sits. The Gemara answers: This teaches **that they** would **bring her up and** would **bring her down** repeatedly **in order to fatigue her,** with the hope that her worn-down mental state will lead to her confession. This was also done with witnesses testifying in cases of capital law, **as it is taught** in the *Tosefta* (*Sanhedrin* 9:1): **Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar says:** In cases of capital law, the **court brings the witnesses from** one **place to** another **place in order to confuse them so that they will retract** their testimony if they are lying.

The *mishnah* continues: *de-in amarta tehorah ani ma'alin ota le-sha'ar hamizrach* — if she says she is innocent, they bring her up to the eastern gate, which is the *Sha'ar Nikanor*, the great gate at the boundary between the *Ezrat Nashim* and the main *Azarah* of the *Beit Hamikdash*. This gate had fifteen steps ascending to it and two smaller doors on either side. It was the central point for those who had no reason to enter the *Azarah* itself — *Yisraelim*, women, and others — who nevertheless came to the *Beit Hamikdash*.

The Gemara asks: *ma'alin ota* — what does it mean they bring her up? *Hatam ka'ima* — she was already there, since the earlier stages of the ritual had already taken place at the *Sha'ar Nikanor*. The Gemara clarifies: *ma'alin ota* refers to bringing her up from the *Beit Din Hagadol she-biYerushalayim*, the *Lishkat Hagazit*, which straddles the boundary between the *Azarah* and the *Ezrat Nashim* — half *kodesh* and half *chol* — but which is also adjacent to the *Sha'ar Nikanor*. The Gemara notes that it is precisely here, *lifnei Hashem*, that the woman must be made to drink, as the *pasuk* requires: *ve-he'emid ha-kohen et ha-ishah lifnei Hashem*.

The same location requirement applies to the *metzora*, whose purification process also requires the *kohen* to stand him at the entrance of the *Mishkan* or *Beit Hamikdash*: *ve-he'emid ha-kohen ha-mitaher*. The symbolic power of this is notable — the *metzora* is excluded even from walled cities, and then for his *taharah* he is brought back inside, to the very threshold of the sacred.

### Making Her Weary

The Gemara then states: *ma'akimim ota u-mekifim ota kedei le-yage'ah* — they make her walk all around the *Har Habayit* in order to tire her out. The purpose is to wear her down so that, if she is guilty, she will be more likely to admit it rather than proceed to drink the water. Conversely, if she is genuinely innocent, the fact that she is willing to drink even after this exhausting ordeal demonstrates the sincerity of her claim.

### Witnesses Comparison

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** שֶׁשָּׁם מַשְׁקִין אֶת הַסּוֹטוֹת וְכוּ׳. בִּשְׁלָמָא סוֹטוֹת, דִּכְתִיב: ״וְהֶעֱמִיד הַכֹּהֵן אֶת הָאִשָּׁה לִפְנֵי ה׳״, מְצוֹרָעִין נָמֵי, דִּכְתִיב: ״וְהֶעֱמִיד הַכֹּהֵן הַמְטַהֵר וְגוֹ׳״, אֶלָּא יוֹלֶדֶת מַאי טַעְמָא?
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **Because there,** at the Eastern Gate, **they give the *sota* women** the bitter water **to drink,** and there the lepers and women who have given birth are purified. The Gemara asks: **Granted, the *sota* women** are given the bitter water to drink there, **as it is written: “And the priest shall stand the woman before the Lord”** (Numbers 5:18), and the Eastern Gate is directly opposite the Sanctuary, which is the area referred to as “before the Lord.” Similarly, with regard to **lepers as well,** this is **as it is written: “And the priest that cleans** him shall set the man that is to be cleansed, and those things, before the Lord” (Leviticus 14:11). **But what is the reason that** a woman **who has given birth** must also be purified there?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אִילֵּימָא מִשּׁוּם דְּאָתְיָין וְקָיְימָין אַקּוּרְבָּנַיְיהוּ, דְּתַנְיָא: אֵין קׇרְבָּנוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם קָרֵב אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן עוֹמֵד עַל גַּבָּיו. אִי הָכִי — זָבִין וְזָבוֹת נָמֵי! אִין הָכִי נָמֵי, וְתַנָּא חֲדָא מִינַּיְיהוּ נְקַט.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **If we say** it is **because** of the requirement **for** the women who have given birth to **come and stand over their offerings, as it is taught** in a *baraita*: **The offering of a person is brought only if he stands over it** while it is being sacrificed, and that is why they stand at this gate, which is as close to the sacrifice as they are permitted to be while they are ritually impure. **If that is so,** then the same *halakha* should apply to **men who experience a gonorrhea-like discharge [*zavim*] and women who experience a discharge of uterine blood after their menstrual period [*zavot*] as well.** They are also ritually impure while their offerings are sacrificed. Why would the mishna then specify women who have given birth? The Gemara answers: **Yes, it is indeed so, and the *tanna* cited one of them,** and the same *halakha* applies to all others in that category.

A *tanna* draws an explicit comparison to the procedure in capital cases. Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar states: *Beit Din mesa'in et ha-edim mi-makom le-makom kedei she-titaref da'atan aleihem ve-yahzeru ba-hen* — the *beit din* makes witnesses in capital cases schlep from place to place in order to tire them out and induce them to reconsider whether they really wish to testify. The parallel is precise: in capital cases the witnesses may be putting an innocent person to death if they are lying, and we create disincentives for false testimony. At the same time, if they are telling the truth, they are absolutely obligated to testify — indeed, it is a great *mitzvah* to do so. The same dual dynamic applies to the *sotah*.

The Gemara also notes the broader question of who else must present themselves at the *Sha'ar Nikanor*. A *yoledet*, a woman who has given birth, must bring her *korban* there as well, by the rule that *ein korbano shel adam karev ela im ken omed al gabav* — a person's sacrifice cannot be offered unless that person is present. The Gemara raises *zavim* and *zavot* as additional examples of *mechusei kapparah*, and concludes that the *mishnah* simply listed one representative case — *tana chada mineihu nakat*.

### Two Sotahs Together

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: אֵין מַשְׁקִין שְׁתֵּי סוֹטוֹת כְּאַחַת, כְּדֵי שֶׁלֹּא יְהֵא לִבָּהּ גַּס בַּחֲבֶירְתָּהּ. רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: לֹא מִן הַשֵּׁם הוּא זֶה, אֶלָּא אָמַר קְרָא: ״אֹתָהּ״ — לְבַדָּהּ.
> **Translation:** § **The Sages taught** in a *baraita* in the *Tosefta* (1:6): **Two *sota* women are not given to drink simultaneously, in order that the heart** of each one **not be emboldened by the other,** as there is a concern that when one sees that the other woman is not confessing, she will maintain her innocence even if she is guilty. **Rabbi Yehuda says: This is not for** that **reason. Rather,** it is because **the verse states:** “And the priest shall bring **her [*ota*]** near and stand her before the Lord” (Numbers 5:16). Rabbi Yehuda explains his inference: The word “*ota*” indicates her **alone,** and therefore there is a Torah edict not to have two women drink the bitter water simultaneously.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְתַנָּא קַמָּא, הָכְתִיב ״אֹתָהּ״? תַּנָּא קַמָּא — רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן הִיא, דְּדָרֵישׁ טַעַם דִּקְרָא, וּמָה טַעַם קָאָמַר: מָה טַעַם ״אוֹתָהּ״ לְבַדָּהּ — כְּדֵי שֶׁלֹּא יְהֵא לִבָּהּ גַּס בַּחֲבֶירְתָּהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** as for **the first *tanna*, isn’t it written “*ota*”?** The Gemara answers: **The first *tanna* is** actually **Rabbi Shimon, who interprets the reasons of** *halakhot* written in **verses, and he is saying: What is the reason? What is the reason** the Torah requires **her alone,** that each *sota* drink individually? **In order that the heart** of each woman **not be emboldened by the other.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַאי בֵּינַיְיהוּ? אִיכָּא בֵּינַיְיהוּ רוֹתֶתֶת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **What is** the difference **between them?** Why should it matter if this *halakha* is due to a logical reasoning or due to a Torah edict? The Gemara answers: The difference **between them** is in a case where one of the women is **trembling** from fear. Since she has obviously not been emboldened by the presence of the other, Rabbi Shimon would allow her to be given to drink at the same time as the other.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְרוֹתֶתֶת מִי מַשְׁקִין? וְהָא אֵין עוֹשִׂין מִצְוֹת חֲבִילוֹת חֲבִילוֹת.
> **Translation:** The Gemara asks: **And** if she is **trembling,** can the court **give her to drink** at the same time as the other? **But** there is a general principle that **one does not perform mitzvot in bundles,** as one who does so appears as if the mitzvot are a burden upon him, and he is trying to finish with them as soon as possible.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דִּתְנַן: אֵין מַשְׁקִין שְׁתֵּי סוֹטוֹת כְּאַחַת, וְאֵין מְטַהֲרִין שְׁנֵי מְצוֹרָעִין כְּאַחַת, וְאֵין רוֹצְעִין שְׁנֵי עֲבָדִים כְּאַחַת, וְאֵין עוֹרְפִין שְׁתֵּי עֲגָלוֹת כְּאַחַת, לְפִי שֶׁאֵין עוֹשִׂין מִצְוֹת חֲבִילוֹת חֲבִילוֹת.
> **Translation:** **As we learned** in a *baraita*: **Two *sota* women are not given to drink simultaneously, and two lepers are not purified simultaneously, and two slaves are not pierced simultaneously, and two heifers do not have their necks broken simultaneously, because one does not perform mitzvot in bundles.** Accordingly, even Rabbi Shimon would agree that under no circumstances can a priest give two *sota* women to drink simultaneously. How, then, can the Gemara say that a trembling woman can be given to drink together with another *sota*?

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי, וְאִיתֵּימָא רַב כָּהֲנָא, לָא קַשְׁיָא: כָּאן בְּכֹהֵן אֶחָד, כָּאן בִּשְׁנֵי כֹּהֲנִים.
> **Translation:** **Abaye said, and some say** it was **Rav Kahana** who said: This is **not difficult. Here,** the second *baraita*, which says that it is prohibited to give two *sota* women to drink simultaneously because one does not perform mitzvot in bundles, is speaking **with regard to one priest. There,** Rabbi Shimon in the first *baraita*, who permits a trembling *sota* to be given to drink together with another *sota*, is speaking **with regard to two priests.** Since no individual priest is giving two women to drink simultaneously, mitzvot are not being performed in bundles.

The *baraita* states: *ein mashkin shtei sotos ke'achas* — one may not administer the *sotah* water to two women simultaneously. The concern expressed by the first opinion is *kedei shelo yehei libah gas bechavertah* — one woman should not be emboldened by the presence of the other. If two women are undergoing the ritual together and both are stubborn, each one may be emboldened by the other's stubbornness into proceeding to drink the water even if guilty.

Rabbi Yehuda disagrees not with the ruling but with its basis: *lo min hashem hu zeh ela amar kra osah levadah* — this is not derived from *sevara*, from logical reasoning, but directly from the *pasuk*, which says *osah levadah*, "her alone." This is a *gezeirat hakatuv*. This constitutes a good example of a *machloket* in which both *tanna'im* agree on the *halakhah* but differ on whether its source is logical or scriptural. Notably, the Gemara treats a compelling *sevara* as carrying *d'Oraita* force — *tana kamma*, identified as Rabbi Shimon, holds that we *darshin ta'ama dekra*, we interpret the *pasuk* in light of its reasoning, so the *gezeirat hakatuv* is bounded by the logic behind it.

The *nafka mina* between the two opinions concerns a woman who is *rotetet* — particularly fearful and visibly trembling. According to Rabbi Shimon, such a woman could theoretically be made to drink alongside another *sotah*, since there is no concern that she would be emboldened by the other woman's presence. According to Rabbi Yehuda, the *gezeirat hakatuv* is absolute regardless of the circumstances. The *Tosafot HaRosh* adds that one would still need to account for the case where one woman is fearful but the other is not — the latter could still embolden the former, requiring the prohibition to cover both scenarios.

The Gemara then introduces a broader principle: *ein osin mitzvos chavilos chavilos* — one may not bundle *mitzvot* together. Rashi explains that this is because doing so gives the appearance of treating *mitzvot* as a heavy burden one is trying to rush through, as though eager to discharge one's obligations and move on — *nire'eh kemo shehayah alav lemassoi u-memaher leforok masa'o*. *Tosafot* in *Moed Katan* offers a different and more positive framing: just as we do not combine one celebration with another — *ein me'arvin simcha besimcha* — so too each *mitzvah* deserves its own separate focus and its own *simcha shel mitzvah*. One of the *acharonim*, Rav Yosef Engel, notes an additional technical dimension: the principle of *osek bemitzvah patur min hamitzvah* — one who is occupied with a *mitzvah* is exempt from another *mitzvah* — may itself create a structural barrier to performing two *mitzvot* simultaneously.

The *baraita* lists further applications of *ein osin mitzvos chavilos chavilos*: two *metzoraim* cannot undergo the purification ritual simultaneously; two *avadim Ivrim* cannot have their ears pierced at the same time; two *agalot arufot* cannot be performed together. Notably, all of these examples involve performing two instances of the *same mitzvah* rather than two different *mitzvot* in parallel — an important distinction, since no one would suggest that one cannot *daven minchah* while simultaneously shaking a *lulav* simply because they are two different *mitzvot*.

The *machloket* over whether the piercing of a slave's ear is a *mitzvah* at all — and therefore subject to *ein osin mitzvos chavilos chavilos* — is itself debated by later authorities. The *Chazon Ish* holds it is a *reshut*, entirely optional, and the master could refuse even if the slave requests to remain; the *Magen Avraham* holds that once the slave requests to be *nirtza*, it becomes obligatory for the master.

The Gemara ultimately resolves the apparent tension between the principle of *ein osin mitzvos chavilos chavilos* and the possibility of having two *sotahs* drink on the same day: *lo kashya kan bekohen echad kan beshnei kohanim* — there is no contradiction, since the prohibition applies to a single *kohen* who would be performing the ritual for two women. Two different *kohanim* could each administer the water to a different *sotah*, even at the same time, since neither *kohen* individually is bundling *mitzvot*.

## Exposure & Humiliation



### Uncovering Hair

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהַכֹּהֵן אוֹחֵז בִּבְגָדֶיהָ. תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: ״וּפָרַע אֶת רֹאשׁ הָאִשָּׁה״, אֵין לִי אֶלָּא רֹאשָׁהּ. גּוּפָהּ מִנַּיִן — תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״הָאִשָּׁה״. אִם כֵּן מָה תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״וּפָרַע אֶת רֹאשָׁהּ״ — מְלַמֵּד שֶׁהַכֹּהֵן סוֹתֵר אֶת שְׂעָרָהּ.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And the priest grabs hold of her clothing** and pulls them until he reveals her heart, and he unbraids her hair. The Gemara cites the source for these acts. **The Sages taught:** The verse states: “And the priest shall stand the woman before the Lord **and uncover the woman’s head”** (Numbers 5:18). From this verse **I have** derived **only** that he uncovers **her head; from where** do I derive that he uncovers **her body? The verse states: “The woman,”** rather than just stating: And uncovers her head. This indicates that the woman’s body should be uncovered as well. **If so, what** is the meaning when **the verse states** specifically: **“And uncover her head”?** Once it has stated that he uncovers the woman, it is already apparent that she, including her hair, is uncovered. It **teaches that the priest** not only uncovers her hair but also **unbraids her hair.**

The *baraita* cites the *pasuk* *u-farah et rosh ha-ishah* — the *kohen* unbraids or uncovers the woman's hair. The Gemara asks: from *roshah* alone, we know only about her head. From where do we derive that her body itself must also be exposed? The Gemara answers: the *pasuk* does not say merely *u-farah et roshah* but rather *u-farah et rosh ha-ishah* — the full word *ha-ishah*, "the woman," is included, extending the obligation to expose her body as well. And if so, what does the specific mention of *roshah* teach? *Melamed shehakohen soter et se'arah* — it teaches that the *kohen* undoes and loosens her hair specifically.

This *sugya* is also notable as the primary source for the *halakhic* discussion of hair covering for a married woman. The *gemara* in *Kesuvos* draws on this passage to argue that at least a minimal degree of hair covering is a *de'Oraita* obligation — the very fact that uncovering the *sotah*'s hair constitutes a degradation implies that covering it is normally required by Torah law. The further questions of *das Moshe* versus *das Yehudis*, how much hair must be covered, and how much of that obligation is societal rather than scriptural, are all major *halachic* questions in their own right and well beyond the scope of the present discussion.

### Hirhurim Concern

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** רַבִּי יְהוּדָה אוֹמֵר: אִם הָיָה לִבָּהּ וְכוּ׳. לְמֵימְרָא דְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה חָיֵישׁ לְהִרְהוּרָא, וְרַבָּנַן לָא חָיְישִׁי?
> **Translation:** The mishna continues by citing that **Rabbi Yehuda says: If her heart was** attractive he would not reveal it, and if her hair was attractive he would not unbraid it. The Gemara asks: **Is this to say that Rabbi Yehuda,** who maintains that it is prohibited to uncover an attractive woman, **is concerned about** onlookers having sexual **thoughts, and the Rabbis,** who permit it, **are not concerned** about this?

Rabbi Yehuda raises a consideration: what if the woman is particularly attractive? This brings into focus a broader question: how concerned are we about *hirhurim*, inappropriate sexual thoughts, among those witnessing the *sotah* ritual? *L'meimra de'Rabbi Yehuda chaish l'hirhur* — it would appear that Rabbi Yehuda is concerned about *hirhurim* arising from the public exposure of the *sotah*. And *v'rabbanan lo chaishi* — the Rabbanan are less concerned.

### Execution Comparison

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְהָא אִיפְּכָא שָׁמְעִינַן לְהוּ, דְּתַנְיָא: הָאִישׁ, מְכַסִּין אוֹתוֹ פֶּרֶק אֶחָד מִלְּפָנָיו, וְהָאִשָּׁה, שְׁנֵי פְּרָקִים — אֶחָד מִלְּפָנֶיהָ וְאֶחָד מִלְּאַחֲרֶיהָ, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁכּוּלָּהּ עֶרְוָה, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: הָאִישׁ נִסְקָל עָרוֹם, וְאֵין הָאִשָּׁה נִסְקֶלֶת עֲרוּמָּה!
> **Translation:** **But we have heard the opposite from them, as it is taught** in the *Tosefta* (*Sanhedrin* 9:6): Although **a man** condemned to stoning is stoned unclothed, the court **covers him** with **one** small **piece** of material **in front of him,** to obscure his genitals, and they cover **a woman with two** small **pieces** of material, **one in front of her and one behind her, because all of her** loins are **nakedness,** as her genitals are visible both from the front and from the back. This is **the statement of Rabbi Yehuda. And the Rabbis say: A man is stoned** while **naked, but a woman is not stoned** while **naked,** but fully clothed. Apparently, Rabbi Yehuda is not concerned that the onlookers seeing the woman unclothed will lead to sexual thoughts, but the Rabbis are concerned about this.

However, the Gemara notes that this apparent alignment seems to be precisely the reverse of what these same authorities hold elsewhere. In the context of execution by *skilah*, a *baraita* states that a man is stripped of all clothing except *perek echad milfanav* — one piece of cloth in front. A woman, by contrast, receives two pieces — *echad milfaneha v'echad milachareha* — because, as Rashi explains, *she'beit haboshet ne'erat mishnei tzadim*, a woman's private area is exposed from both sides and therefore requires coverage on both sides. But notably, even according to this *baraita*, a woman's entire torso would be uncovered during execution by *skilah* — indicating that the Rabbanan are not especially concerned about *hirhurim* in that context. Yet that is described as *divrei Rabbi Yehuda* — Rabbi Yehuda's position. Meanwhile, *v'chachamim omrim ha'ish niskal arom, v'ein ha'ishah niskalet aroma* — the Sages say a man is stoned completely naked, but a woman is not stoned naked at all; she must be clothed.

This presents a puzzling inversion: in the context of *sotah*, Rabbi Yehuda appears more protective of the woman from *hirhurim*, while in the context of *skilah* the Rabbanan are the ones who require that a woman not be exposed naked.

### Raba's Rationale

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רַבָּה: הָכָא טַעְמָא מַאי — שֶׁמָּא תֵּצֵא מִבֵּית דִּין זַכָּאִית, וְיִתְגָּרוּ בָּהּ פִּרְחֵי כְהוּנָּה. הָתָם — הָא מִסְתַּלְּקָא. וְכִי תֵּימָא: אָתֵי לְאִיגָּרוֹיֵי בְּאַחְרָנְיָיתָא — הָאָמַר רָבָא: גְּמִירִי דְּאֵין יֵצֶר הָרָע שׁוֹלֵט אֶלָּא בְּמַה שֶּׁעֵינָיו רוֹאוֹת.
> **Translation:** **Rabba said: What is the reason here,** with regard to a *sota*, that Rabbi Yehuda is concerned? **Perhaps** the *sota* **will leave the court** having been proven **innocent, and the young priests** in the Temple who saw her partially naked **will become provoked by** the sight of **her. There,** in the case of a woman who is stoned, **she departs** from this world by being stoned and there is no concern for sexual thoughts. The Gemara comments: **And if you would say** that the fact that she is killed is irrelevant to their sexual thoughts, as the onlookers **will be provoked with regard to other** women, this is not a concern. As **didn’t Rava say:** It **is learned** as a tradition **that the evil inclination controls only that which** a person’s **eyes see.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר רָבָא: דְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה אַדְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה קַשְׁיָא, דְּרַבָּנַן אַדְּרַבָּנַן לָא קַשְׁיָא? אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא: דְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה אַדְּרַבִּי יְהוּדָה לָא קַשְׁיָא, כִּדְשַׁנִּין.
> **Translation:** **Rava said:** Is the contradiction between one statement **of Rabbi Yehuda** and the other statement **of Rabbi Yehuda difficult,** while the contradiction between one statement **of the Rabbis** and the other statement **of the Rabbis** is **not difficult?** There is also an apparent contradiction between the two rulings of the Rabbis, as with regard to a *sota*, they are not concerned about sexual thoughts, but with regard to a woman who is stoned they are. **Rather, Rava said:** The contradiction between one statement **of Rabbi Yehuda** and the other statement **of Rabbi Yehuda** is **not difficult, as we answered** above.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** דְּרַבָּנַן אַדְּרַבָּנַן נָמֵי לָא קַשְׁיָא. הָכָא טַעְמָא מַאי? מִשּׁוּם ״וְנִוַּסְּרוּ כׇּל הַנָּשִׁים״. הָתָם — אֵין לְךָ יִיסּוּר גָּדוֹל מִזֶּה.
> **Translation:** The contradiction between one ruling **of the Rabbis and** the other ruling **of the Rabbis** is **not difficult as well. Here,** with regard to a *sota*, **what is the reason** that her hair and body are uncovered? **Because** of what is stated in the verse, that other women should be warned: “Thus will I cause lewdness to cease out of the land, **that all women may be chastened** not to do after your lewdness” (Ezekiel 23:48). **There,** with regard to stoning, **you have no greater chastening than** seeing **this** stoning itself.

Rava resolves both the internal contradiction within Rabbi Yehuda's position and that within the Rabbanan's position simultaneously: *de-Rabbi Yehuda ad-Rabbi Yehuda lo kashya*, and *de-rabanan ad-rabanan nami lo kashya*. The concern in the *sotah* case is not *hirhurim* per se — it is *mishum venissaru kol hanashim*, because all women must witness the *sotah*'s fate and be warned by her example. The humiliation of the *sotah* serves a broader deterrent and educational purpose. In the case of execution, however, *ein lecha yisur gadol mizeh* — there is no humiliation greater than death itself, and there is no need to add further degradation.

Rava identifies a more specific concern driving the *sotah* case: *sh'ma totzi mibeit din zakai v'yitgaru bah pirchei kehunah* — perhaps she will leave the *sotah* ritual having been found innocent after drinking the water, and the young *kohanim* who are present in the *Azarah* (*metzuyin ba'azara*, as Rashi notes) will be stirred by inappropriate thoughts about her. If she has been exposed in public in the course of the ritual, these *kohanim* will have seen her, and that sight will provoke *hirhurim*. It is worth noting that the Gemara qualifies this concern by the woman's appearance, which raises an obvious difficulty: *erva* is *erva*, and it is not clear why the degree of *hirhurim* she might provoke should depend on how attractive she is. The Gemara does not fully resolve this tension, and it remains a genuine problem.

But why not apply that concern to execution as well? *Hatam mistalka* — in the case of execution, she is gone; she has died. The *kohanim* will not see her again. *Gemiri d'ein yetzer hara sholet ela b'ma she'einav ro'ot* — it is a received principle that the *yetzer hara* only takes hold with respect to what a person's eyes have actually seen. Once she is dead, there is no ongoing exposure to provoke ongoing *hirhurim*. But in the *sotah* case, if she is found innocent, she returns to her husband and continues to live in the community, and the *kohanim* who saw her exposed will encounter her again.

### Bru Lo Mitah Yafah

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכִי תֵּימָא לַעֲבֵיד בַּהּ תַּרְתֵּי — אָמַר רַב נַחְמָן אָמַר רַבָּה בַּר אֲבוּהּ, אָמַר קְרָא: ״וְאָהַבְתָּ לְרֵעֲךָ כָּמוֹךָ״ — בְּרוֹר לוֹ מִיתָה יָפָה.
> **Translation:** **And if you would say that two** forms of chastening, both stoning and humiliation, **should be done with her, Rav Naḥman said** that **Rabba bar Avuh said:** The **verse states: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself”** (Leviticus 19:18), teaching that even with regard to a condemned prisoner, **select a good,** i.e., a compassionate, **death for him.** Therefore, when putting a woman to death by stoning, she should not be humiliated in the process.

The Gemara then raises the question of whether we should administer both punishments — expose her publicly *and* execute her — in the case of a guilty *sotah*. The answer draws on one of the most fundamental principles of rabbinic capital law: *ve'ahavta lere'acha kamocha beru lo mitah yafah* — from the verse "love your neighbor as yourself," the rabbis derive that one must provide an appropriate death, a *mitah yafah*, even to one who is executed. This *drashah* is invoked repeatedly throughout *Sanhedrin* in connection with all four *mitot beit din*, consistently functioning to pull back from what the *Torah* might appear to mandate explicitly, in order to preserve the physical integrity of the human body.

As Professor Lorberbaum has argued, the underlying principle is *tzelem Elokim* — the human being possesses a physical form that reflects the divine image, and capital punishment must therefore be carried out in a manner that does not deform or disfigure the body. *Beru lo mitah yafah* does not necessarily mean the most pleasant death, but the death that leaves the body most intact and unspoiled. By this standard, adding public humiliation on top of execution would violate the principle — we cannot require someone to be both publicly exposed and put to death.

### Pain vs. Humiliation

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** לֵימָא דְּרַב נַחְמָן תַּנָּאֵי הִיא? לָא, דְּכוּלֵּי עָלְמָא אִית לְהוּ דְּרַב נַחְמָן. וְהָכָא בְּהָא קָמִיפַּלְגִי, מָר סָבַר: בִּזְיוֹנֵיהּ עֲדִיף לֵיהּ טְפֵי מִצַּעְרָא דְגוּפֵיהּ, וּמָר סָבַר: צַעְרָא דְגוּפֵיהּ עֲדִיף לֵיהּ טְפֵי מִבִּזְיוֹנֵיהּ.
> **Translation:** The Gemara suggests: **Let us say that** the statement **of Rav Naḥman is** a dispute between ***tanna’im*,** and according to Rabbi Yehuda there is no mitzva to select a compassionate death. The Gemara refutes this: **No,** it may be **that everyone agrees with** the opinion of **Rav Naḥman, and here they disagree about this:** One **Sage,** i.e., the Rabbis, **holds:** Minimizing **one’s degradation is preferable to him than** minimizing **his physical pain.** Therefore, the Rabbis view the more compassionate death as one without degradation, even if wearing clothes will increase the pain of the one being executed, as the clothes will absorb the blow and prolong death. **And** one **Sage,** Rabbi Yehuda, **holds that** minimizing **physical pain is preferable to a person than** minimizing **his degradation,** and therefore the one being executed prefers to be stoned unclothed, without any chance of the clothing prolonging the death, although this adds to the degradation.

The Gemara then addresses a specific *machloket* regarding what *mitah yafah* means in practice, directly relevant to the question of execution: *mar savar bizyona adif lah tfei mitza'ara d'gufa* — one opinion holds that emotional humiliation is worse than physical pain, and therefore being executed naked (which accelerates death by removing cushioning from the stones but is more humiliating) is actually the *better* death — more pain, less humiliation. *U-mar savar tza'ara d'gufa adif lei tfei mibizyonei* — the other opinion holds that physical pain is worse, and therefore being clothed at execution (which cushions the impact but prolongs death) is preferable — more humiliation, but less physical suffering. Both opinions apply the principle of *mitah yafah*, but they disagree about which factor is the dominant consideration in determining the most appropriate death.

### Removing Jewelry

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָיְתָה מְכוּסָּה לְבָנִים וְכוּ׳. תָּנָא, אִם הָיוּ שְׁחוֹרִים נָאִים לָהּ — מְכַסִּין אוֹתָהּ בְּגָדִים מְכוֹעָרִים.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: If **she was dressed in white** garments, he would cover her with black garments. A Sage **taught: If black** garments **are becoming to her,** then **she is covered in unsightly garments.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** הָיוּ עָלֶיהָ כְּלֵי זָהָב וְכוּ׳. פְּשִׁיטָא, הַשְׁתָּא נַוּוֹלֵי מְנַוֵּויל לַהּ, הָנֵי מִיבַּעְיָא? מַהוּ דְּתֵימָא: בְּהָנֵי אִית לַהּ בִּזָּיוֹן טְפֵי, כִּדְאָמְרִי אִינָשֵׁי: שָׁלִיחַ עַרְטִיל וְסָיֵים מְסָאנֵי. קָא מַשְׁמַע לַן.
> **Translation:** The mishna teaches: If **she was wearing gold adornments** or other jewelry, they are removed from her. The Gemara asks: **Isn’t** this **obvious? Now** that the priest **renders her unattractive** by uncovering her and dressing her in unsightly garments, **is it necessary** to teach that they remove **these** adornments from her? The Gemara answers: **Lest you say** that **with these** adornments on her, **she has more degradation, as people say** in a known aphorism: **Undressed, naked, and wearing shoes.** This means that a naked person who wears shoes emphasizes the fact that he is naked. Perhaps one would think that by a *sota* wearing jewelry, her nakedness is emphasized and her degradation is amplified. Therefore, the mishna **teaches us** that this is not so.

The *mishnah* states that if the woman was dressed beautifully — wearing white garments or fine jewelry — she is dressed in ugly or worn clothing for the ritual. The Gemara initially treats it as obvious (*pshita*) that her jewelry must be removed, since the entire purpose of the process is *nivvul*, to make her look degraded. But the Gemara raises a counterintuitive possibility: *behanei it lah bizyuna tfei* — perhaps leaving her jewelry on is actually *more* humiliating, because of the common proverb about a person who walks around naked but wearing shoes. That is, a figure who retains tokens of elegance and status while being otherwise degraded is in some respects more pitiable and embarrassing than one who is stripped completely. The woman wearing her fine jewelry through the *sotah* ritual only highlights the contrast between what she once was and what she has now been reduced to — which could be argued to intensify the disgrace rather than diminish it. The Gemara concludes — *kamashma lan* — we do not apply this reasoning; the jewelry is removed.

### Egyptian Rope

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְאַחַר כָּךְ מֵבִיא חֶבֶל וְכוּ׳. בְּעָא מִינֵּיהּ רַבִּי אַבָּא מֵרַב הוּנָא: חֶבֶל הַמִּצְרִי מַהוּ שֶׁיְּעַכֵּב בְּסוֹטָה? מִשּׁוּם שֶׁלֹּא יִשָּׁמְטוּ בְּגָדֶיהָ מֵעָלֶיהָ הוּא, וּבְצִלְצוֹל קָטָן נָמֵי סַגִּי, אוֹ דִילְמָא מִשּׁוּם דְּאָמַר מָר ״הִיא חָגְרָה לוֹ בְּצִלְצוֹל, לְפִיכָךְ כֹּהֵן מֵבִיא חֶבֶל הַמִּצְרִי וְקוֹשֵׁר לָהּ לְמַעְלָה מִדַּדֶּיהָ״ — מְעַכֵּב?
> **Translation:** The mishna continues: **And afterward** the priest **would bring** an Egyptian **rope,** and he would tie it above her breasts. **Rabbi Abba raised a dilemma before Rav Huna: What is** the *halakha* as to whether the lack of **an Egyptian rope will preclude** the performance of the rite **with regard to a *sota*?** Does any means of tying suffice? Perhaps the primary function of the rope **is so that her clothes will not fall off her, and** therefore even **a small ribbon [*tziltzul*] would also suffice. Or, perhaps** the rope is used **because** of what **the Master said: She girded** herself **with** a comely **ribbon** when she committed her transgression, and **therefore the priest brings** specifically **an Egyptian rope,** which is coarse, **and ties it above her breasts.** If that is the case, then the Egyptian rope should be **indispensable.**

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֲמַר לֵיהּ, תְּנֵיתוּהָ: וְאַחַר כָּךְ מֵבִיא חֶבֶל הַמִּצְרִי וְקוֹשְׁרוֹ לָהּ לְמַעְלָה מִדַּדֶּיהָ, כְּדֵי שֶׁלֹּא יִשָּׁמְטוּ בְּגָדֶיהָ מֵעָלֶיהָ.
> **Translation:** Rav Huna **said to him: You learned** the answer to this dilemma in a *baraita* that teaches: **And afterward** the priest **would bring an Egyptian rope** and he would tie it above her breasts, **so that her clothes would not fall off her.** The *baraita* states that the use of an Egyptian rope is primarily for holding up her clothing, and therefore use of specifically Egyptian rope is not essential.

After the tearing of the garment, the *kohen* brings a *chevel hamitzri*, an Egyptian rope, and ties it above her breasts to prevent her clothing from falling down entirely. The *Yerushalmi* offers two explanations for why specifically an Egyptian rope: either because her conduct recalled the licentiousness of Egypt, *k'ma'aseh eretz mitzrayim*, or alternatively the term is not *chevel mitzri* but *chevel matzui* — a common rope woven from palm fronds, rough and coarse, which was the standard material of the time.

The Gemara records a question posed by Rav Abba to Rav Huna: is the *chevel hamitzri* *me'akev* — is it an indispensable part of the *sotah* ritual, without which the process is invalid? Two possible explanations for its use are considered. If the reason is simply *shelo yishm'tu bgadeha me'aleha*, so that her clothes do not fall down, then even a small cord would suffice and the specific Egyptian rope would not be essential. But if the reason is rooted in *middah keneged middah*, corresponding to her having provocatively girded herself, then the specific rope carries substantive significance and might be *me'akev*.

The Gemara resolves the question by citing a more expanded version of the *mishnah*, a *baraita*, which makes explicit that the reason for the rope is purely functional — *k'dei shelo yishm'tu bgadeha me'aleha* — so that her garments do not fall. Since the reason is purely practical, the specific Egyptian rope is not *me'akev*.

*Tosafot* at the top of the following page raises an additional question prompted by the *Yerushalmi*: the rope is purchased from *mishirei halishkah*, public temple funds. This raises a parallel question about other instruments of capital punishment — the sword used for beheading, the cord used for *chenek* — whether those too are purchased with public funds, and how far the parallel between *sotah* and *mitat beit din* extends.

## Witnesses to Ritual



[DAF:7b]
### Contradiction Resolved

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** וְכׇל הָרוֹצֶה לִרְאוֹת בָּהּ יִרְאֶה וְכוּ׳. הָא גוּפָא קַשְׁיָא. אָמְרַתְּ: כָּל הָרוֹצֶה לִרְאוֹת בָּהּ רוֹאֶה, אַלְמָא לָא שְׁנָא גַּבְרֵי וְלָא שְׁנָא נְשֵׁי, וַהֲדַר תָּנֵי: כׇּל הַנָּשִׁים מוּתָּרוֹת לִרְאוֹתָהּ: נָשִׁים — אִין, אֲנָשִׁים — לָא.
> **Translation:** § The mishna teaches: **And anyone who desires to watch her may watch,** except for her slaves and maidservants, who are not permitted to watch because her heart is emboldened by them. And all of the women are permitted to watch her. The Gemara comments: **This** matter **is itself difficult,** as there is an internal contradiction in the mishna. First **you say: And anyone who desires to watch her may watch. Apparently,** there **is no difference** whether the onlookers are **men and** there **is no difference** whether they are **women;** all are permitted to observe the rite. **And then** the mishna **teaches: And all of the women are permitted to watch her,** which indicates **women, yes,** they may watch her, but **men, no,** they may not.

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: תַּרְגְּמַהּ אֲנָשִׁים. אָמַר לֵיהּ רָבָא: וְהָא ״כָּל הָרוֹצֶה לִרְאוֹת בָּהּ רוֹאֶה״ קָתָנֵי!
> **Translation:** **Abaye said: Interpret** the first statement, which permits all people to observe the *sota*, as pertaining **to women,** but men may not be onlookers. **Rava said to him: But** it **teaches** in that first statement that **anyone who desires to watch her may watch,** and one cannot limit this to women.

The Gemara notes that the *mishnah* appears to contradict itself. On one hand it states *ve-chol ha-rotzeh lir'ot bah yera'eh* — whoever wishes to see may come and see, with no distinction between men and women. On the other hand, the *mishnah* also states *kol ha-nashim mutarot lir'otah* — all women are permitted to see her, implying that women yes, men no.

Abaye attempts to resolve the contradiction by suggesting *targemah anashim* — that even the first clause refers only to women. Rava objects: *ve-ha kol ha-rotzeh lir'ot bah yera'eh* — but the first clause clearly says "whoever wishes," which is inclusive language, not restricted to women.

### Women Obligated

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** אֶלָּא אָמַר רָבָא: כׇּל הָרוֹצֶה לִרְאוֹת בָּהּ רוֹאֶה, לָא שְׁנָא גַּבְרֵי וְלָא שְׁנָא נְשֵׁי. וְנָשִׁים חַיָּיבוֹת לִרְאוֹתָהּ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ״וְנִוַּסְּרוּ כׇּל הַנָּשִׁים וְלֹא תַעֲשֶׂינָה כְּזִמַּתְכֶנָה״.
> **Translation:** **Rather, Rava said: Anyone who desires to watch her may watch,** there **is no difference** whether the onlookers are **men** and there **is no difference** whether they are **women. And** the next clause of the mishna teaches that **women are obligated to watch her, as is stated:** “Thus will I cause lewdness to cease out of the land, **that all women may be chastened not to do after your lewdness”** (Ezekiel 23:48).

Rava offers a different resolution: *kol ha-rotzeh lir'ot bah ro'eh lo shena gavrei ve-lo shena nashei* — whoever wishes to come and see, whether men or women, may come and see. Both are permitted. The second clause, however, conveys something more: *nashim chayavot lir'otah* — women are not merely permitted but *obligated* to attend.

### Women's Lesson

The obligation for women to witness the *sotah* ritual is derived from the *pasuk*: *ve-nissaru kol ha-nashim ve-lo ta'aseina ke-zimatchanah* — all women are to be chastened and warned so that they do not engage in the same *zimah*, the same prohibited sexual behavior, that the *sotah* may have committed. Women must be present specifically so that they will internalize the lesson and be deterred from similar conduct. Men are permitted to attend but are not obligated; women are not merely permitted but are required to come and witness.

## Middah Keneged Middah



### Measure for Measure

> **Hebrew/Aramaic:** מַתְנִי׳ בְּמִדָּה שֶׁאָדָם מוֹדֵד — בָּהּ מוֹדְדִין לוֹ. הִיא קִשְּׁטָה אֶת עַצְמָהּ לַעֲבֵירָה — הַמָּקוֹם נִוְּולָהּ. הִיא גִּלְּתָה אֶת עַצְמָהּ לַעֲבֵירָה — הַמָּקוֹם גִּלָּה עָלֶיהָ. בַּיָּרֵךְ הִתְחִילָּה בַּעֲבֵירָה תְּחִילָּה וְאַחַר כָּךְ הַבֶּטֶן — לְפִיכָךְ תִּלְקֶה הַיָּרֵךְ תְּחִילָּה וְאַחַר כָּךְ הַבֶּטֶן, וּשְׁאָר כָּל הַגּוּף לֹא פָּלַט.
> **Translation:** MISHNA: The mishna teaches lessons that can be derived from the actions and treatment of a *sota*. **With the measure that a person measures, he is measured with it.** For example, **she,** the *sota*, **adorned herself to** violate **a transgression, the Omnipresent** therefore decreed that **she** be **rendered unattractive; she exposed herself for** the purpose of violating **a transgression,** as she stood in places where she would be noticed by potential adulterers, so **the Omnipresent** therefore decreed that **her** body be **exposed** publicly; **she began her transgression with** her **thigh and afterward with** her **stomach, therefore the thigh is smitten first and then the stomach, and the rest of all** her **body** does **not escape** punishment.

The final *mishnah* of the chapter introduces the principle of *middah keneged middah* — the manner in which a person conducts himself or herself is precisely the measure by which God responds. *Be-middah she-adam moded bah modedin lo.* The *sotah*'s punishment corresponds exactly to her sin. *Hi kishtah atzmah la-aveirah, ha-Makom nivvelah* — she adorned herself with jewelry for the sake of her sin; God therefore takes away her beauty. *Hi giltah et atzmah la-aveirah, ha-Makom gilah aleiha* — she revealed herself for her sin; God therefore reveals her.

### Order of Punishment

The *mishnah* specifies the sequence of the *sotah*'s physical punishment: *be-yarech hitchilah ba-aveirah* — she began her sin with her thigh, a euphemism for her genitalia. *Tcilah ve-achar kach ha-beten* — and then her womb, corresponding to the concern about pregnancy from the adulterous relationship. Therefore, *lokah ha-yarech tcilah ve-achar kach ha-beten* — the punishment begins with the thigh and only then affects the womb. *Ve-sha'ar kol ha-guf lo palat* — the rest of the body is entirely unaffected by the *sotah* water; only those specific parts of the body that were implicated in the sin are punished. The Gemara will elaborate on these *pesukim* and their implications in the discussion that continues on daf 8b.
