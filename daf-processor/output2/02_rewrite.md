# Sukkah 2 — Daf Yomi Shiur

## Masechet Overview

### Five Chapters Intro

*Masechet* Sukkah consists of five chapters, and while one might expect the tractate to open with a discussion of the *mitzvah* of sitting in a *sukkah*, it in fact begins with the structural requirements of the *sukkah* itself. The first two chapters are devoted to the structure; toward the end of the second chapter, the *masechet* turns to the *mitzvah* of dwelling in the *sukkah*. The third chapter is devoted to *lulav* and *esrog*, and the fourth and fifth chapters address *Hallel* and related topics. The first three chapters are certainly primary in terms of the objects of the *mitzvah* and the *mitzvot* themselves that apply on *Sukkot*.

### Rishonim & Sources

Unlike *Yoma*, where much of the material is not practically relevant today, *Sukkah* is almost entirely applicable, and accordingly there is a considerably richer body of *Rishonim* engaging with it. Beyond *Rashi* and *Tosafot*, there is a *Ritva* on *Sukkah*, as well as substantial discussions by the *Rif*, the *Ramban*, and the *Ba'al HaMaor*. On the third chapter, dealing with *lulav* and *esrog*, there exist entire monographs: the *Ra'avad* composed a *Hilchos Lulav* and the *Ramban* wrote a *Hilchos Lulav v'Esrog*, so there is a notably greater depth of *Rishonim* on this *masechet* than on *Yoma*.

### Three Criteria

The *masechet* opens at 2a with the following *mishna*:

> *Sukkah shehi gevoha lemala me'esrim ama pesula* — A *sukkah* that is higher than twenty *amot* is invalid. *Rebbi Yehuda machshir* — Rabbi Yehuda declares it valid. *Veshe'eina gevoha asara tefachim* — And if it does not have a minimum height of ten *tefachim*, *veshe'ein la shalosh o shelosha defanos* — if it does not have three walls, *veshechamasa meruba metzilasa* — and if the sunlight inside the *sukkah* exceeds the shade — *pesula* — it is invalid.

Three primary criteria emerge: the height must fall between ten *tefachim* and twenty *amot*; there must be a minimum of three walls; and the *sukkah* must provide more shade than sunlight. Conspicuously absent from the *mishna* are specifications about the other dimensions — minimum area, width, and length — as well as any discussion of what materials are valid for walls or for *sechach*. This last omission is itself noteworthy, since the final criterion — that shade must exceed sunlight — raises a preliminary question: is that a statement about the quality and nature of the *sechach*, or merely about the experiential reality of being inside a *sukkah* at any given moment? Whether a *sukkah* is invalid the moment more sun than shade happens to be entering, or whether invalidity derives from a structural deficiency in the *sechach* itself, is a question that will occupy the *masechet* considerably.

### Psul vs. Takanta

The *Gemara* opens by noting that *Masechet Eruvin* begins with a nearly identical *mishna*: *mavoi shehu gavoah me'esrim ama yima'et* — an alleyway whose beam (*korah*) is higher than twenty *amot* must be lowered — and that Rabbi Yehuda likewise disagrees there. The *Gemara* then poses a precise linguistic question:

> *Mai shna gabei sukkah detani psulah, umai shna gabei mavoi detani takanta* — What is different about *sukkah* that the *mishna* uses the language of invalidity (*psulah*), while by the *mavoi* it uses the language of remedy (*takanta* — fix it)?

Why does the *sukkah* *mishna* say it is *invalid*, while the *mavoi* *mishna* says *lower it*?

### Rashi: Reality Layer

The *Gemara* offers a first answer: *sukkah de'oraisa tani psulah, mavoi derabbanan tani takanta* — the *sukkah* requirement is biblical, so the language of invalidity is used; the *mavoi* requirement is rabbinic, so the language of remedy is used. But why should that distinction produce a difference in formulation?

*Rashi* offers a conceptually rich explanation. Regarding the *sukkah*, *Rashi* writes: *de'esrim ama hilchesa gmiri lah mide'oraisa vekodem shenishnes hamishnah ne'emar shiur miSinai* — the twenty-*amah* limit is a tradition from Sinai, and it represents an objective reality that preceded the *mishna*'s formulation. The *mishna*, in other words, is not legislating; it is reporting a pre-existing metaphysical fact. A *sukkah* higher than twenty *amot* is, from the *Torah*'s perspective, objectively invalid — *pasul* reflects an objective state of affairs.

By contrast, *Rashi* explains regarding the *mavoi*: *dekulhu derabbanan demide'oraisa sagi lei beshalosh mechitzos ve'eino tzarich lekorah zu ella miderabbanan* — the *mavoi* has no such pre-existing objective invalidity. Biblically, three walls suffice; it is the rabbis themselves who required the *korah*, and who decided it is no good if placed too high. Since the rabbis are here legislating rather than reporting, they cannot say the structure is "invalid" in some objective sense, because they themselves created the requirement. What they can do is tell you: we don't like it this way — fix it. There is something philosophically arresting in this distinction: it evokes the notion of *halacha* as creating objective realities, and the difference between a pre-existing halakhic truth and a norm that the rabbis are constructing anew.

### Tosafot: Brevity

*Tosafot* offers two alternative explanations, both more pragmatic in character. The first concerns the risk of misinterpretation: if the *mishna* says *fix it* with respect to a biblical requirement, one might reason that if one failed to fix it, perhaps the situation is still valid after the fact. The language of *pasul* — invalid — forecloses that reasoning entirely. By contrast, for a rabbinic requirement, one is willing to accept the risk of such misinterpretation, because something is gained by the formulation *lower it*: it tells you directly how to remedy the situation. Since the *sukkah* requirement is biblical, the *mishna* does not risk ambiguity and says *invalid*.

The second explanation in *Tosafot* is stylistic. The *Gemara* elaborates: *sukkah shenafishi mileiah tani pasul* — the *sukkah mishna* covers many problems (too high, too low, too much sunlight, too few walls), so spelling out a remedy for each would require an unwieldy list. It is simpler and more concise to say *invalid*. The *mavoi* *mishna*, on the other hand, deals with a single scenario — the beam is too high — and can therefore afford to tell you how to fix it, in keeping with the general principle, also found at the opening of *Pesachim*, that one should always teach in the most concise manner possible. The two explanations in *Tosafot* are thus, respectively, that the biblical-versus-rabbinic distinction determines the willingness to risk misinterpretation, and that sheer conciseness drives the choice of language in the *sukkah* case.

It is worth observing, as a broader point about the structure of Talmudic tractates, that the opening *sugyot* of a *masechet* tend — according to the view of *Rav Sherira Gaon* and scholars who follow him — to be of *Savoraic* composition, or at least to share certain features: they are *stam*, with no named Amoraic or Tannaitic statements, they engage in comparative and linguistic analysis of *mishnayot*, and they introduce themes that will resonate throughout the tractate. Here, the pairing of *sukkah* and *mavoi* already hints at a shared conceptual basis for the twenty-*amah* limit — a question that will be explored as the *masechet* unfolds.

## 20-Amah Sources

### Rava: Awareness

Having established the linguistic contrast between the two *mishnayot*, the *Gemara* now turns to the substantive question: *Mina hani mili* — from where is the twenty-*amah* limit derived? What is its biblical or conceptual source?

*Rava* answers first: *Amar Rava de'amar kra, lema'an yedu doroseichem ki basukkos hoshavti es bnei Yisrael* — the verse states, "In order that your generations should know that I caused the children of Israel to dwell in *sukkot*" (Leviticus 23:43). From this *Rava* derives:

> *Ad esrim ama adam yodeia shehu dar basukkah* — up to twenty *amot*, a person is aware that he is dwelling in a *sukkah*. *Lema'ala me'esrim ama ein adam yodeia shehu dar basukkah* — higher than twenty *amot*, he is not aware that he is dwelling in a *sukkah*. *Mishum delo shalta bei eina* — because the eye does not perceive it.

What does the eye fail to perceive? Looking upward inside a very tall structure, one sees the walls but not the *sechach*. This brings in a foundational principle of the entire *masechet*: for *Chazal*, the essential element (*ikkar*) of the *sukkah* is the *sechach*. *Rashi* states this explicitly in the five words immediately before the *Gemara* begins: *ve'al shem hasechach keruya sukkah* — the *sukkah* is named for the *sechach*. Accordingly, whenever a verse refers to a *sukkah*, it is generally understood to be referring to the *sechach* dimension of the structure, not the walls.

For *Rava*, then, *lema'an yedu* — "in order that they should know" — is not merely a statement about the historical purpose of the *mitzvah* (recalling the wilderness experience) but becomes an operative requirement in how the *mitzvah* is performed. One must be aware, while sitting in the *sukkah*, that one is under *sechach*. If the *sechach* is so high that one does not naturally perceive it, that awareness is absent and the *sukkah* is invalid.

### Rav Zeira: Shade

*Rav Zeira* derives the limit from a different verse, in Isaiah (4:6): *Vesukkah tihyeh letzel yomam meichorev* — "and a *sukkah* shall be for shade by day from the heat." Although the context of this verse is a Messianic vision — it follows a description of divine clouds over *Zion* evoking the wilderness pillar of cloud and fire — *Rav Zeira* reads the use of the word *sukkah* as conveying a requirement about the actual *sukkah* of the *mitzvah*. The verse says the *sukkah* provides shade, and this becomes a structural criterion:

> *Ad esrim ama adam yoshev betzel sukkah* — up to twenty *amot*, a person sits in the shade of the *sukkah* (i.e., the shade produced by the *sechach*). *Lema'ala me'esrim ama ein adam yoshev betzel sukkah ela betzel defanot* — higher than twenty *amot*, the person is not sitting in the shade of the *sechach* but in the shade of the walls.

The walls of such a tall structure are what shade the interior below; the *sechach*, situated so high above, does not effectively block the sunlight from reaching the person below. The invalidity, for *Rav Zeira*, is thus a deficiency of the *sechach*: it does not function as shade-providing *sechach* in practice.

It is worth pausing to appreciate the richness of the Isaianic verse being introduced here. The passage evokes the cloud and fire of the Exodus — *anan yomam ve'ashan venogah esh lehavah laylah* — "a cloud by day and smoke and the glow of fire by night" — and connects the Messianic vision of divine protection with the word *sukkah*, already implicitly raising the theme of *sukkah kineged ananei kavod* — the *sukkah* corresponding to the clouds of glory — a theme that will recur throughout the tractate. The *Gemara* is quietly weaving together the *sukkah* of the *mitzvah* and the *sukkah* of Messianic redemption.

### Abaye: Mountains

*Abaye* challenges *Rav Zeira*'s shade-based reasoning with the following objection:

> *Amar lei Abaye: ela me'ata, oseh sukkato be'ashrot karnayim, hachi nami dela havei sukkah?*

If one builds a *sukkah* in a valley between two tall mountains, does that mean it is not a valid *sukkah* — because the mountains, rather than the *sechach*, are providing the shade of the interior?

### Zeira: Schach Quality

*Rav Zeira* responds:

> *Hatam, dal ashrot karnayim, ika tzel sukkah* — there, if you remove the mountains, the *sechach* still provides shade. *Hacha, dal defanot, leika tzel sukkah* — here, if you remove the walls, you do not have the shade of the *sechach*.

The distinction is intrinsic versus extrinsic shade. When a *sukkah* is built between mountains, the *sechach* itself is constructed and situated in a way that it produces shade on its own; the mountains add shade extrinsically. But when the *sukkah* is twenty *amot* tall, the *sechach* — being a small covering at a great height — would not, even in the absence of the walls, provide effective shade. Physically, when *sechach* is placed at extreme height, all but the most direct overhead sunlight reaches below it; the shade produced is negligible. The invalidity, therefore, is not that something external is providing shade instead of the *sechach*, but that the *sechach* itself is incapable of providing shade regardless of the walls.

### Rava: Temp Structure

*Rava* — in a second statement — offers a third derivation, based on a different verse: *basukkot teshu shivat yamim* — "you shall dwell in *sukkot* for seven days" (Leviticus 23:42). *Rava* reads the word *sukkot* as implicitly contrasting with one's permanent home:

> *Amra Torah, kol shivat hayamim, tze midirat keva veshev bedirat arai* — the *Torah* says: for all seven days, leave your permanent dwelling and sit in a temporary dwelling.

This introduces another foundational theme of the *masechet*: the *sukkah* is conceptually defined as a *dirat arai* — a temporary structure — as opposed to a *dirat keva* — a permanent one. From this, *Rava* derives the twenty-*amah* limit:

> *Ve'ad esrim ama, adam oseh dirato dirat arai* — up to twenty *amot*, it is possible to construct a dwelling that is temporary in character. *Lemala me'esrim ama, de'adam oseh dirato dirat keva* — higher than twenty *amot*, a person only builds to such dimensions when constructing a permanent structure. *Ella dirat keva* — it is a permanent dwelling, and is therefore invalid.

### Abaye: Materials

*Abaye* challenges *Rava*'s reasoning:

> *Amar lei Abaye: ela me'ata, asu mechitzot shel barzel vesichach gaban, hacha nami dlav sukkah?*

If one built walls of iron — a paradigmatically permanent building material — and placed *sechach* on top, would that also be invalid as a *dirat keva*? The question has practical force: if one went out immediately after *Yom Kippur* and constructed solid brick-and-mortar walls with *sechach* on top, is that not a permanent structure? And yet presumably all would agree it is a valid *sukkah*.

### Rava: Dimensions

*Rava* responds with precision:

> *Amar lei: hachi ka'amina lach. Ad esrim ama, de'adam oseh dirato dirat arai* — up to twenty *amot*, the dimensions themselves are such that the structure can be a temporary dwelling. Therefore, *ki avidei dirat keva, nami nafik* — even if you used materials associated with permanent construction, you fulfill your obligation. *Lemala me'esrim ama, de'adam oseh dirato dirat keva* — higher than twenty *amot*, those dimensions only ever occur in the context of permanent construction. Therefore, *ki avidei dirat arai, nami lo nafik* — even if you made it out of straw, if the dimensions are those of a permanent structure, you do not fulfill your obligation.

The key insight is that in *halacha*, as in most halakhic categories, the requirement of temporary versus permanent is translated into quantifiable terms — dimensions — rather than into judgments about building materials. The materials do not determine whether a structure is a *dirat arai* or a *dirat keva*; the dimensions do.

## Tosafot: Permanence

### Tosafot: Nails

This resolution by *Rava* prompts a related question explored by *Tosafot*: if building material is irrelevant to the *dirat arai* requirement, can one nail the *sechach* planks in permanently — with metal nails, perhaps even plastered in place — such that no rain can penetrate, and yet still be valid? *Tosafot* asks directly: *vehalo yachol likvoa hanessarim bamasmerim shelo yerdu geshamim basukkah* — can one affix planks with nails such that rain will not descend into the *sukkah*? Would that be valid?

### Tosafot: Non-Permanent

*Tosafot* answers that while the walls of a *sukkah* may be constructed from permanent materials — that question has been resolved — the *sechach* is different. Since *sukkah* is named for and defined by its *sechach*, *Tosafot* writes: *mikol makom baschach sheikar hasukkah al shem haschach, lo mishtarya hasukkah ad deavid lah arai* — the *sukkah* is not valid unless the *sechach* itself is made in a temporary manner. Although *Tosafot* does not fully quantify what "temporary" means in this context, the point is that when the *sechach* itself is constructed in a way that makes it a permanent roof, the structure ceases to be a *sukkah* and becomes a house.

### Rain Sign

One of the markers *Tosafot* adduces for this category of permanent *sechach* is rain impermeability. If the *sechach* is constructed so that no rain can enter at all, that is a sign (*siman*) that it has become a permanent roof. This is also connected to the statement that rain falling on *Sukkot* is a sign of divine displeasure (*siman kelalah bachag*) — a teaching that presupposes the *sukkah* must be capable of letting rain in. If a perfectly rain-proof *sukkah* were valid, the sign would lose its meaning. *Tosafot* notes, with some irony, that the very verse from *Yeshayahu* that *Rav Zeira* cited — *vesukkah tihyeh letzel yomam meichorev, ulemachseh ulemistor mizerem umimatar* — "and the *sukkah* shall be for shade from the heat and a refuge from storm and rain" — suggests the *sukkah* provides protection from rain, yet most authorities hold that a *sukkah* must allow at least some rain to pass through the *sechach*.

The upshot of *Tosafot*'s analysis is a non-quantifiable but real category of *sechach* permanence. Even where building material and ordinary structural considerations are irrelevant, the *sechach* itself — precisely because the *sukkah* is named for it — must retain the character of a temporary covering. Rain impermeability is one concrete manifestation of losing that character.

## Debate Limits

### Yoshiya: Wall Gap

The *Gemara* now considers qualifications on the debate about the twenty-*amah* limit. The first is in the name of *Rebbi Yoshiyah*, transmitted by *Rav*: *makhlokes beshein defanos megi'os laskhakh* — the debate between the *Sages* and *Rabbi Yehuda* applies only when the walls do not reach the *sechach*, i.e., there is a gap between the top of the walls and the *sechach*. *Aval defanos megi'os laskhakh, afilu lemala me'esrim amah kesherah* — but if the walls reach all the way up to the *sechach*, even if the *sukkah* is higher than twenty *amot*, everyone agrees it is valid.

### Eye Following

The *Gemara* immediately asks: *keman* — according to whose reasoning does this qualification make sense? The answer is that it fits only *Rava*'s first reasoning — the requirement that the person be aware he is under *sechach*, because *delo shalta bei eina* — the eye does not perceive the *sechach* when the structure is too tall. If the walls reach all the way up to the *sechach*, one's eye naturally travels upward, following the wall until it reaches the intersection of wall and roof, and so will perceive the *sechach* even at great height. But if the walls end in open air — with a gap between wall-top and *sechach* — the eye stops at the end of the wall and does not naturally continue upward to seek the *sechach*. This qualification cannot apply to *Rava*'s second reasoning about temporary versus permanent structure (whether the walls reach the *sechach* has no bearing on whether the structure's dimensions imply permanence), nor to *Rav Zeira*'s shade reasoning (whether the walls reach the *sechach* has no bearing on whether the *sechach* provides shade from that height).

### Huna: Area

A second qualification comes from *Rav Huna* in the name of *Rav*: *makhlokes beshein bah ela arba amos* — the debate between the *Sages* and *Rabbi Yehuda* applies only to a *sukkah* no larger than four *amot* by four *amot*. *Aval yesh bah yoser me'arba amos al arba amos, afilu lemala me'esrim amah kesherah* — if the *sukkah* is larger than four by four *amot*, even if it is higher than twenty *amot*, everyone agrees it is valid.

### Shade Proportional

The *Gemara* again asks: *keman*? This qualification fits only *Rav Zeira*'s shade reasoning. *Mishum tzel* — the question is whether the *sechach* provides shade in practice. *Vekivan d'rivcha, ika tzel sukkah* — since the *sechach* has significant width and area, even if it is very high up, it will produce shade below it. The underlying physical intuition is that a tiny patch of *sechach* at extreme height allows sunlight to reach below it from almost all angles except directly overhead; but a *sechach* of substantial area, even at great height, blocks enough sunlight from various angles to provide meaningful shade.

### Proportionality

*Tosafot* raises a natural follow-up: if a larger *sechach* can provide shade even when very high, should not the permissible height be proportional to the width of the *sechach*? If four by four *amot* allows up to twenty *amot*, then ten by ten *amot* should allow twenty-five *amot*, thirty by thirty even more — and so on, yielding a chart of permissible heights correlated to *sechach* dimensions. *Tosafot* acknowledges this is logically defensible, but then offers the more standard rabbinic answer: *kim lehu lerabanan de'afilu begovah elef, de'afilu begovah elef, i efshar shelo yikanes tzel skhakh purta* — the Rabbis knew that once the *sechach* is larger than four by four *amot*, even at a height of a thousand *amot* some shade from the *sechach* will inevitably reach below. And once it is less than four by four, even at twenty *amot* it will not provide adequate shade. This is analogous to the principle stated elsewhere in *Yoma* regarding minimum quantities: *im hi rechava yoter me'arba amot kedatnan beperek Yom Hakippurim gabi kusevta demishpag daitza de'amrinan kulei alma tuva ve'agma potash purta* — as with the minimum measure of a pressed date for *Yom Kippur*, once you have the right minimum amount, even *Og Melech Habashan* eating it registers as significant; a little less than that registers for no one. The Rabbis set fixed numbers even where a strictly proportional rule might seem more logical, because quantified thresholds are what *halacha* requires.

### Chana: Minimum

A third qualification is stated by *Rav Chana bar Rabba* in the name of *Rav*: *makhlokes beshe'eina machzeket ela kedei rosho verubo veshulchano* — the debate applies only when the *sukkah* holds no more than the absolute minimum: one's head and most of one's body and one's small table. This minimum — applicable when one eats in a reclining position, so that the body is extended — is defined later in the tractate as approximately seven *tefachim* by seven *tefachim*. *Aval machzeket yoter mikedei rosho verubo veshulchano, afilu lemala me'esrim ama kesherah* — once it is even slightly larger than this absolute minimum, everyone agrees it is valid even if higher than twenty *amot*.

### Chicken Coop

The *Gemara* asks: *keman* — according to whose reasoning does this qualification fit? And the answer is: *delo k'chad* — it does not fit any of the three models. One might try to fit it into *Rav Zeira*'s shade model by arguing that once the *sechach* is slightly larger than the minimum, shade at great height is provided — but the *Gemara* is unconvinced, since if the shade model requires at least four by four *amot* for shade to be produced at height, a *sechach* barely larger than seven by seven *tefachim* will certainly not produce shade at twenty *amot*. *Tosafot* therefore offers its own rationale: a *sukkah* that is simultaneously tiny in area and extremely tall is not recognizable as a *sukkah* at all — *lul shel tarnigolim*, a chicken coop. An absurdly disproportionate structure, tiny and extraordinarily tall, no longer carries the character of a dwelling, and so the twenty-*amah* invalidity does not even apply; it is simply not a *sukkah* at all. Once the *sukkah* is slightly larger than the absolute minimum — and thereby no longer resembles such an absurd structure — the debate about twenty *amot* resumes.

### Gemara: Two Models

The *Gemara* then considers whether the qualifications of *Rav Huna* and *Rav Chana bar Rabba* are really two answers to the same underlying question — namely, what is the minimum *kosher* size of a *sukkah* — and that both agree the twenty-*amah* debate applies only to the minimum size, disagreeing only about what the minimum size is. *Mar savar hechsher sukkah b'arba amot* — one holds the minimum *sukkah* is four by four *amot*; *umar savar hechsher sukkah b'machzeket rosho verubo veshulchano* — the other holds it is the size of head-body-and-table. The *Gemara* initially entertains this reading but ultimately rejects it: *dekulei alma hechsher sukkah b'rosho verubo veshulchano* — everyone agrees the minimum *sukkah* is the head-body-and-table measure of seven *tefachim* by seven *tefachim*. The dispute between *Rav Huna* and *Rav Chana bar Rabba* is not about the minimum size of a *sukkah*, but about when the twenty-*amah* problem begins.

According to *Rav Chana bar Rabba*: the debate is only in an absolute minimum-sized *sukkah* (seven by seven *tefachim*); once even slightly larger, everyone agrees it is valid at any height — the *Tosafot* logic being that the disproportionate-chicken-coop problem disappears once there is a little more area. According to *Rav Huna*: the debate extends to any *sukkah* up to four by four *amot*; once it is four by four *amot* or more, everyone agrees it is valid even very high — the shade logic being that at four by four *amot*, shade is guaranteed even at great height. The *Gemara* thus insists that the qualifications are genuinely about different models for why twenty *amot* is a problem, not about a subsidiary dispute regarding minimum *sukkah* size.

## Hilni Precedent

### Hilni's High Sukkah

The *Gemara* now introduces the story of Queen *Hilni* as *Rabbi Yehuda* cites it in support of his ruling that a *sukkah* higher than twenty *amot* is valid. The relevant text:

> *Amar Rabbi Yehuda: ma'aseh b'Hilni hamalka b'Lod, she'haysa sukkasa gvoha me'esrim ama, ve'hayu zkeinim nichnasim ve'yotzim l'sham, ve'lo amru lah davar* — Rabbi Yehuda related: there was an incident with Queen *Hilni* in *Lod* — her *sukkah* was higher than twenty *amot*, and the elders would enter and exit there and said nothing to her.

*Rabbi Yehuda* brings this as a practical precedent: the elders' silence is an implicit endorsement of the validity of a *sukkah* higher than twenty *amot*.

### Queen Status

The *Sages* respond: *misham ra'aya? Isha haysa* — can you bring a proof from there? She was a woman, and women are exempt from the *mitzvah* of *sukkah* (*ptura min hasukkah*). The elders' silence is fully explained by her exempt status; they had no reason to intervene.

### Seven Sons

*Rabbi Yehuda* counters: *ve'lo shiva banim havu lah?* — did she not have seven sons who were with her in the *sukkah*? And furthermore: *ve'od kol ma'aseh lo asta ela al pi chachamim* — she conducted herself in all matters only in accordance with the ruling of the *Sages*.

### Probability Logic

The *Gemara* interjects: why does *Rabbi Yehuda* need to add that she followed the *Sages* in all things, if the point about seven sons is already a sufficient reply? The *Gemara* explains that *Rabbi Yehuda* anticipates a further objection: the sons might all have been very young — *banim ktanim hayu, u'ktanim pturim min hasukkah* — and young children (*ketanim*) are exempt from *sukkah*. *Rabbi Yehuda*'s response is a probabilistic argument: *keivan de'shiva havu, i efshar d'lo hava b'hu chad she'eino tzarich l'imo* — since there were seven of them, it is impossible that not even one of them had reached the age at which he no longer needed his mother — which is the halakhic threshold for rabbinic obligation in *sukkah* from a *chinuch* perspective. As the *Gemara* defines this threshold later, it is the age at which a child no longer wakes in the night calling for his mother; once he has reached that level of independence, he is obligated rabbinic ally in *sukkah* by reason of *chinuch*.

It is worth noting that *Rabbi Yehuda* himself knew the actual facts of the story and did not need to infer from probability that one of the sons was of *chinuch* age — he knew the particulars. The *Gemara*, however, constructs a logical demonstration that at minimum, one of the seven sons must have been of obligated *chinuch* age, so that the premise of the argument is established even without insider knowledge of the facts.

### Piety Argument

A final objection is then anticipated: even if one of the sons was obligated *miderabbanan* by reason of *chinuch*, perhaps *Hilni* was not careful about rabbinic requirements? The reply is the second element of *Rabbi Yehuda*'s statement: *ve'od kol ma'aseh lo asta ela al pi chachamim* — she conducted herself in all matters according to the *Sages*. *Hilni* was known for her exceptional piety and her deference to rabbinic authority in all things, so it cannot be argued that she was indifferent to rabbinic obligations. This piety argument closes the series of rebuttals to those who sought to dismiss her case as irrelevant.
