## Chapter 1: the avot and the general rules
- **1:1 pp.1-3.** Four avot nezikin: the ox (shor), the pit (bor), the mav'eh and the hav'er (fire). The Gemara disputes what they are. Rav: the mishnah counts all damage categories (the ox covers keren, shen and regel; mav'eh is a person who damages). Shmuel (followed by Rambam, Bartenura and Kehati): the mishnah counts only the four property-damage categories that are mu'ad from the start: regel (the "ox"), shen (the "mav'eh", from "nivu'a"), bor and esh. Keren is not counted in 1:1 though it is damage by an ox (2:4-2:5). Each of the four has a feature the others lack, so the Torah wrote each. What they share: damaging is their way, guarding them is the owner's duty, and when they damage the damager pays from the best of the land (b'meitav ha'aretz).
- **1:2 pp.4-6.** "Whatever I am bound to guard, if I did not guard it I have prepared (hichsharti) its damage," even if only partly prepared (a pit of nine handbreadths in which an animal falls and dies: liable for payment). Payments apply to property that is not hekdesh (no me'ilah), belongs to Israelites (bnei brit), and is owned (not hefker). They do not apply in the damager's own domain. In a domain shared by damager and damaged: for foot and tooth he is exempt; for keren he pays. Kehati (pdf 6): the domain is "private" to the damaged and damager together (for example a courtyard shared by two owners). VERIFY the exact domain wording before use.
- **1:3 pp.6-7.** Damages are paid in money only (shaveh kesef), before a court of experts (not before a lay court), and the damage is proven by free Israelite witnesses. Women are included in the laws of damages. Kehati: orphans' property is collected only from land, not from movables (the Gemara's reason: movables of orphans are not "money" for this payment). VERIFY.
- **1:4 pp.8-11.** Five tamim and five mu'adim. The animal (behema) is tam: it is not mu'ad to gore, push, bite, lie on or kick (kicking: be'itah). Mu'adim: tooth (to eat what is fit for it), foot (to break while walking), an ox that has become mu'ad (after warnings), an ox that damages in the damaged person's domain, and a person. Wild animals (wolf, lion, bear, leopard, panther) and the snake are mu'adim, and the snake is always mu'ad; R. Eliezer exempts them when they are tame. A tam pays half damages from the body of the ox; a mu'ad pays full damages from the best of the land. The Gemara disputes whether a tam's half-damages is a fine (kenas) or money (mamon). Kehati reports the dispute and its consequences (pp.8-10). VERIFY before use.

## Chapter 2: the mu'adim in detail
- **2:1 pp.12-13.** Foot is mu'ad to break while walking. The animal is mu'ad to go its way and break; but if it kicked (be'itah) or flung pebbles from under its feet, the owner pays half damages (that is a toladah of keren, not of foot). If its foot broke one vessel which fell and broke another: full damages for the first, half for the second (the second is "pebbles", tzrarot).
- **2:2 pp.14-15.** Tooth is mu'ad to eat what is fit for it (fruit and vegetables). If it ate clothes or vessels the owner pays half damages (not its way; a toladah of keren). In the damaged person's domain he pays; in the public domain he is exempt, but if it benefited he pays what it benefited. How much: from within the courtyard's open area he pays what it benefited (not as the whole damage); from the courtyard sides, what it damaged; at the shop entrance, what it benefited; inside the shop, what it damaged (the Gemara's reading). VERIFY the cases.
- **2:3 p.16.** A dog and a kid that jumped from a roof and broke vessels pay full damages because they are mu'adim to jump. A dog that took a cake with an ember on it and went to a stack: full for the cake, half for the stack.
- **2:4 pp.17.** Who is tam and who is mu'ad: a mu'ad is one whose owner was warned three days (R. Yehuda); a mu'ad returns to tam after three days of not damaging (R. Yehuda). R. Meir: mu'ad when it has damaged three times; tam again only when children play with it and it does not gore. The halakha: Kehati (p.17, quoting Rambam) is like R. Meir for returning to tam, so an ox does not return to tam until children play with it and it does not gore. VERIFY exactly who the halakha follows for BECOMING mu'ad (p.17).
- **2:5 pp.18-19.** An ox that damages in the damaged person's domain: gore, push, bite, lie on, kick. In the public domain: half damages. In the damaged person's domain: R. Tarfon says full damages; the Sages say half. R. Tarfon's kal vachomer from tooth and foot (exempt in public, full damages in the damaged domain). The Sages answer with "dayo labo min hadin lihyot kenidon" (the conclusion may not exceed its source: half). Halakha: the Sages (Kehati). Kehati also gives the Gemara's discussion of why R. Tarfon holds the dayo does not apply here.
- **2:6 pp.20-21.** A person is always mu'ad: whether accident or intent, awake or asleep, a person who blinded another's eye or broke his vessels pays full damages.

## Chapter 4: the goring ox (read pp.37-50)
- **4:1 pp.37-39.** An ox that gored four or five oxen one after another: it pays the last victim first, then the one before, and so on from what remains. VERIFY (read quickly).
- **4:2 pp.40, 48-49.** An ox can be mu'ad to one kind of victim and not to another (mu'ad to people not to animals, to children not to adults): where it is mu'ad it pays full damages, elsewhere half. R. Yehuda: an ox that is mu'ad on Shabbat and not on weekdays pays full for Shabbat and half for weekdays; it returns to tam after three Sabbaths without goring (Kehati and the mishnah p.48-49). VERIFY wording.
- **4:3 p.50.** An Israelite's ox that gored an ox of the Temple (hekdesh), or the Temple's that gored an Israelite's: exempt, since the verse says "his neighbour's ox", not the Temple's. An Israelite's ox that gored a gentile's: exempt; a gentile's that gored an Israelite's, tam or mu'ad: full damages. Kehati's reasons: the Gemara (Habakkuk 3:6): the seven Noahide commandments were not kept, so their property was released to Israel; Rambam: a fine on gentiles who do not guard against damage; Meiri: this applies to nations not bound by religious norms. VERIFY the reasons.
- **4:4 pp.50-51.** The ox of a sighted person that gored the ox of a deaf-mute, imbecile or minor: liable; the ox of a deaf-mute, imbecile or minor that gored the ox of a sighted person: exempt, as they cannot be liable. The court appoints a guardian (apotropos) for their ox and warns it before him. If the deaf-mute is healed or the minor grows up: R. Meir says the ox returns to tam; R. Yose says it is in its presumption (still mu'ad); Rambam follows R. Yose. An ox trained to gore in the arena (shor ha'itztadin) that killed a person: not liable to death ("when an ox gores" and not "when they make it gore"). VERIFY.
- **4:5 p.52.** An ox that gored and killed a person: a mu'ad pays ransom (kofer) to the heirs and is stoned; a tam is stoned but its owner pays no kofer. Same for a boy or girl (a minor). For a slave or maidservant: 30 sela to the master (the same sum whether the slave was worth a mina or one dinar) and the ox is stoned. Kehati: a tam's owner is exempt from 30 sela as from kofer.
- **4:6 p.53.** An ox rubbing against a wall that fell on a person and killed him is not stoned, since it did not intend to kill. If it intended to kill an animal and killed a person, a gentile and killed an Israelite, or a non-viable infant and killed a viable one: exempt (both the ox from stoning and the owner from kofer).
- **4:7 p.54.** The ox of a wife, orphans, guardian, desert, hekdesh, a convert (ger) who died with no heirs: liable to death. R. Yehuda: ox of the desert, hekdesh and a ger who died are exempt, since they have no owners. The halakha does not follow R. Yehuda (Kehati).
- **4:8 p.55.** An ox that went out to be stoned and the owner dedicated it (hekdesh): not consecrated; if slaughtered its meat is forbidden. If the owner dedicated it before the verdict, it is consecrated and its meat is permitted after slaughter.
- **4:9 pp.56-57.** An owner who handed his ox to an unpaid guard, a borrower, a paid guard or a renter: they enter under the owner's place; a mu'ad pays full damages, a tam half. If the owner tied the ox properly and it escaped and damaged: R. Meir: liable whether tam or mu'ad; R. Yehuda: a tam pays, a mu'ad is exempt ("and he did not guard it" means the guarded one that was not guarded); R. Eliezer: only a knife (a very strong guard). Kehati summarises three disputes and rules like R. Yehuda. VERIFY.

## Chapter 5:1 (read pp.58-61)
- **5:1 pp.58-61.** An ox that gored a cow and the calf was found next to her, and it is not known whether she gave birth before the goring: the owner of the ox pays half damages for the cow and a quarter damages for the calf. The reverse (a cow gored an ox and a newborn is found beside her): half damages from the cow and a quarter damages from the calf. Kehati: the mishnah follows Sumchus, who divides money in doubt. The Sages: the one who wants to collect from his fellow must bring proof (hamotzi mechavero alav hara'ayah): the owner of the ox pays nothing for the calf. Halakha: the Sages (Kehati cites Rambam). Kehati also gives Rava's reading of the second part (the cow and calf belong to different people), where Rabbi Abaye disagrees. VERIFY.
